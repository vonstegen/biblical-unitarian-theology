## Project Scope and Method

This manuscript argues from the Bible alone — no commentaries, no systematic theologies, no creeds, no church fathers, no modern biblical scholarship are cited as evidence for any theological claim. The sole authority and source material is Scripture. Every claim is tagged:
- `[E]` for explicit text (the words of Scripture directly state it).
- `[I-band]` for strong implication (the text and its context compel the reading; `[I-high]` is the strongest, `[I-mid]` moderate, `[I-low]` weaker).
- `[H-band]` for hypothesis (a defensible reading that the text does not foreclose; `[H-high]` strongest, `[H-mid]` moderate, `[H-low]` weaker).

Every `[I]` and `[H]` claim carries an evidence sketch with "evidence for," "evidence against," and a "band justification." The band structure preserves what is tractable: enumerable textual evidence the user can audit, plus a qualitative confidence judgment grounded in that evidence.

The thesis is positive — it shows what Scripture itself says. The argument is exegetical, not dogmatic. The conclusions are what the text says, not what must be true.

### Part I — Foundations (The Character of God, Creation, Covenant)

- **[`chapters/Chapter_01_Character_of_God.md`](chapters/Chapter_01_Character_of_God.md)** — *Who is God, and how does His character define justice?* God is just, faithful, and immutable (Deuteronomy 32:4; Numbers 23:19; Malachi 3:6; James 1:17). Justice is the formal constraint that makes the debt-debtor framework coherent — corresponding justice, not arbitrary punishment. The chapter walks six passages (Exodus 34:6–7; Numbers 14:18; Psalm 86:15; Psalm 103:8; Joel 2:13; Jonah 4:2) where mercy and justice are structurally paired, then argues that the penalty declared in Eden (Genesis 2:17) requires corresponding satisfaction rather than simple waiver. This sets the formal constraint for the entire manuscript.

- **[`chapters/Chapter_02_Creation.md`](chapters/Chapter_02_Creation.md)** — *What was humanity created for in relation to God?* Humanity was created in God's image for covenantal obedience and dominion (Genesis 1:26–28; 2:15). The image is the structural anchor of the vocation; Adam does not act as a private individual but as the image-bearer, the representative. The chapter identifies the limit (the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, Genesis 2:9) and points forward to the covenant structure of Chapter 3.

- **[`chapters/Chapter_03_Covenant_of_Eden.md`](chapters/Chapter_03_Covenant_of_Eden.md)** — *What command, warning, and relationship defined Adam's vocation?* God spoke to Adam, gave a command (Genesis 2:16), attached a penalty (Genesis 2:17 — "in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die"), and then — once the command was broken — declared the consequences in detail (Genesis 3:17–19). The death-penalty declaration is `[E]` regardless of whether the formal term "covenant of works" is applied. The chapter's argument does not depend on the covenant-label question; it depends on the declared-penalty text.

### Part II — The Consequences of Adam's Sin (What Was Lost; What Passed to Humanity)

- **[`chapters/Chapter_04_Nature_of_Adams_Sin.md`](chapters/Chapter_04_Nature_of_Adams_Sin.md)** — *What kind of act was Adam's sin?* Adam's act is a covenantal transgression, not a moral lapse, cognitive error, or genetic accident. The chapter walks Romans 5:12–21 (the universality of consequence, the representative structure, the parallel redemption structure) and 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49 (the *in Adam* / *in Christ* parallel, the last-Adam typology) to establish that the *kind* of act determines the *kind* of redemption. The chapter explicitly rejects alternative classifications.

- **[`chapters/Chapter_05_What_Was_Lost.md`](chapters/Chapter_05_What_Was_Lost.md)** — *What exactly did Adam lose?* The chapter inventories eleven losses, each tagged with its textual basis and band: relational rupture with the divine presence, fear of the divine presence, the curse on the ground, the toil of the ground, the mortality of the body, the pain in childbearing, the rupture in the husband-wife relation, the expulsion from the garden, the blocked access to the tree of life, the creation's futility and groaning, and the hope of the redemption of the body. The image-of-God is *marred* (not lost; the image is constitutionally present post-Fall per Genesis 9:6; James 3:9; 1 Corinthians 11:7; Colossians 3:10; Ephesians 4:24) and will be *renewed* in the restoration.

- **[`chapters/Chapter_06_What_Passed_to_Humanity.md`](chapters/Chapter_06_What_Passed_to_Humanity.md)** — *What passed from Adam to humanity?* Death passed to all — `[E]` from Romans 5:12 ("death spread to all men because all sinned") and 1 Corinthians 15:22 ("in Adam all die"). The mechanism is ontic transmission at `[I-high]`: Adam's act produced the condition his descendants inherit. Forensic imputation of guilt is left open at `[I-mid]`. Ezekiel 18:20 ("the son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father") is the limit on direct imputation and is engaged fairly as complementary to the ontic condition.

### Part III — The Pattern of Disobedience (Innocence, Growth, Israel, Prophetic Critique)

- **[`chapters/Chapter_07_Innocence_and_Accountability.md`](chapters/Chapter_07_Innocence_and_Accountability.md)** — *What role do innocence, children, and moral accountability play?* Childhood is a real developmental moral category distinct from adult accountability. The text treats the child as a kind of person who is not held accountable in the adult sense. The age of accountability is `[H-mid]` — a plausible systematic inference but not a textual assertion. Innocence is meaningful without committing to a specific age.

- **[`chapters/Chapter_08_Growth_of_Disobedience.md`](chapters/Chapter_08_Growth_of_Disobedience.md)** — *Does Scripture describe disobedience becoming habitual or hardened?* Yes. The chapter inventories Romans 1:18–32 (Paul's sequence of suppressed knowledge, exchange, handing-over, and conduct) and shows that disobedience is depicted as repeatable across individuals and communities and capable of intensifying in social effect and judgment. The pattern prepares the question of a redeemer whose obedience answers more than one isolated failure (the sufficiency question is developed in Chapter 15).

- **[`chapters/Chapter_09_Israel_as_Pattern_of_Disobedience.md`](chapters/Chapter_09_Israel_as_Pattern_of_Disobedience.md)** — *How does Israel's history prepare for the Messiah?* Israel's history is the covenant-scale witness to the recurring pattern of disobedience that Chapter 8 establishes. The individual-accountability language of Ezekiel 18 is the complement of the constitutional transmission (Chapter 6), not its contradiction. The pattern prepares the need for a faithful human redeemer at the narrative level.

- **[`chapters/Chapter_10_Obedience_Above_Sacrifice.md`](chapters/Chapter_10_Obedience_Above_Sacrifice.md)** — *Does the Old Testament teach that obedience is greater than sacrifice?* Yes. The prophetic critique is the rejection of sacrifice disconnected from the obedience the covenant requires (1 Samuel 15:22–23; Isaiah 1:11–17; Amos 5:21–24; Micah 6:6–8; Hosea 6:6). The obedience the covenant requires is justice, kindness, humility, steadfast love, mercy, knowledge of God, and a broken and contrite heart. This supplies the language for Christ's active obedience in Chapter 12.

### Part IV — The Redeemer and the Cross (Why Human; Perfect Obedience; Gethsemane; Payment)

- **[`chapters/Chapter_11_Why_Must_the_Redeemer_Be_Human.md`](chapters/Chapter_11_Why_Must_the_Redeemer_Be_Human.md)** — *Why must the redeemer be human?* This is the prior load-bearing foundation for Part IV. It grounds the human debtor and penalty (Genesis 2:16–17; 3:17–19; Romans 5:12), then develops the same-order correspondence principle from the Adam-Christ man : man parallel and the explicit shared-human-condition texts (1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45–49; 1 Timothy 2:5–6; Hebrews 2:14–17; Galatians 4:4–5; Hebrews 4:15; 5:7–9). The pure-human representative conclusion remains `[I-high]`, with the "from heaven," "life-giving spirit," and Philippians 2 "form of God" counter-readings explicitly pressure-tested.

- **[`chapters/Chapter_12_Perfect_Obedience_of_Christ.md`](chapters/Chapter_12_Perfect_Obedience_of_Christ.md)** — *What was the nature of Christ's obedience?* Lifelong and willing. The chapter walks the textual evidence: John 8:29 ("I always do the things that are pleasing to him"), John 8:46 ("which of you convicts me of sin?"), 1 Peter 2:22–23 (he "committed no sin" and "entrusted himself to him who judges justly"), Hebrews 4:15 (tempted in every respect "yet without sin"), Hebrews 5:8–9 (learned obedience through what he suffered), Philippians 2:8 (obedient to the point of death). The categories (willing-doing and suffering-acceptance) are drawn from the text; the later labels ("active obedience" / "passive obedience") are identified as later systematic vocabulary and the substance is grounded in Scripture.

- **[`chapters/Chapter_13_Gethsemane_and_the_Two_Wills.md`](chapters/Chapter_13_Gethsemane_and_the_Two_Wills.md)** — *How is the obedience tested in Gethsemane?* The chapter walks the Matthean, Markan, and Lukan Gethsemane narratives (with the Aramaic *Abba*, the strengthening angel, the "not as I will" prayer), the Johannine "the cup" saying (John 18:11), and the Hebrews 5:7–8 reflection. The willingness is shown by the real counter-will (Matt 26:39; Mark 14:36; Luke 22:42), by the setting-aside (the same verses), and by the non-granting of the petition for removal. The two-wills question is engaged exegetically; the chapter stakes its argument on the textual categories the Synoptic and Johannine writers themselves supply, not on the later post-Chalcedonian formulation.

- **[`chapters/Chapter_14_Cross_as_Payment.md`](chapters/Chapter_14_Cross_as_Payment.md)** — *Why is the cross the payment of the debt?* The cross is the obedient man accepting the death penalty established in Chapters 1 and 3. The chapter walks Isaiah 53 (the suffering servant who bears the iniquities of the many, who makes many to be accounted righteous, who pours out his soul to death), Romans 3:21–26 (the propitiation by his blood), 2 Corinthians 5:21 (him who knew no sin was made sin for us), Galatians 3:13 (redeemed from the curse of the law), Hebrews 9:11–14, 22 (without shedding of blood there is no forgiveness), and the rest of the NT payment-language. The active obedience (Chapter 12) and the death are joined into one inseparable claim: the death is the culmination of the lifelong obedience, not a separate transaction.

- **[`chapters/Chapter_15_Why_One_Man_for_Many.md`](chapters/Chapter_15_Why_One_Man_for_Many.md)** — *Why is one righteous man's obedience sufficient for many?* This is the co-equal load-bearing mechanism built on Chapter 11's correspondence foundation; it cannot pass acceptance until Chapter 11 passes A11. Romans 5:18–19 and 1 Corinthians 15:20–22 state the one-act / many-recipients structure `[E]`; the claim that this structure explains why one corresponding man's obedience suffices is tagged `[I-high]`. The chapter then tests federal-headship, faith/application, scope, and the *en* relation without re-proving the same-order premise.

### Part V — The Vindication and the Conclusion

- **[`chapters/Chapter_16_The_Fathers_Vindication_and_the_New_Humanity.md`](chapters/Chapter_16_The_Fathers_Vindication_and_the_New_Humanity.md)** — *What does the Father do in response to the obedient representative's payment?* The resurrection is the Father's vindication of the Son — the public declaration that the payment is accepted and the obedience is complete. The New Testament consistently attributes the resurrection to the Father (Acts 2:24, 32; 3:15; 4:10; 5:30; 10:40; 13:30, 37; 17:31; Romans 4:24–25; 1 Corinthians 15:15; Ephesians 1:20; Colossians 2:12; 1 Thessalonians 1:10; Hebrews 13:20; 1 Peter 1:21). The new humanity is the restoration of what was lost in Chapters 5–6: the image renewed, the relational rupture healed, the constitutional condition reversed in the last Adam (2 Corinthians 5:17; Ephesians 2:15; 4:24; Colossians 3:10).

- **[`chapters/Chapter_17_Conclusions.md`](chapters/Chapter_17_Conclusions.md)** — *What is the overall argument?* The 10 research questions are mapped to their chapter of resolution with tags; what remains open is stated with tags and the reason the text does not resolve it; the debt-debtor thesis is summarized. The thesis: humanity incurred a debt to God through Adam's disobedience; God established death as the judicial consequence of sin and does not rewrite His own justice; redemption required a perfectly obedient human representative — the last Adam — whose willing lifelong obedience culminated in sacrificial death; the Father vindicated the obedient Son by raising him, creating the new humanity in him.

## How to Read This Manuscript

1. Start with [`INTRO.md`](INTRO.md) to understand the Bible-only constraint, the tagging system, the translation set, and the 10 research questions.
2. Read the chapters in order (Ch 1 → 17). The cross-links at the end of each chapter (Acceptance check) and the forward links in the body tell you which subsequent chapter is being presupposed.
3. Read Appendix B when you hit a high-Christology passage that the chapter tags as `[H]`; the appendix presents the range of readings without using them as evidence.
4. Read Appendix C (Glossary) as needed for unfamiliar terms.
5. Read Appendix D (Research Map) when you want to audit the textual evidence for a specific claim — it lists every text the manuscript cites and the chapters in which each is load-bearing.

The tagging system makes the boundary between what the text states and what the argument infers auditable. The Bible-only constraint makes the conclusions reproducible: any reader with the same text and the same exegetical method can check the manuscript's claims against the text itself.
