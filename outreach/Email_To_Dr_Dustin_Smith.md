Dear Dr. Smith,

I am writing as a listener first. I have listened to roughly 370 episodes of the Biblical Unitarian Podcast and am still going — I am a big fan of the work you have talked about through your podcasts so far. I am also a fan of how you use AI in areas of biblical translation: the AI Critical New Testament is part of the reason I believe AI-assisted work, honestly governed, belongs in this field — and that conviction is the one this manuscript's method is built on. Your podcast, alongside other unitarian podcasts, is where the thinking in this manuscript started. I also want to be honest up front: I have three of your books at home but have not read them yet. I am writing anyway, because the paper is not built on your published work — it is built on the questions your podcast set in motion.

Some context about who is asking. I am not an academic. I went to a Christian school as a kid, was raised Baptist and then non-denominational, and was always taught that the Bible is the final source of truth. Over the past five years or so, my questions about the logic of Father and Son became more frequent — and my church's answers stopped being able to carry them. This manuscript is an agentic work of my own developing thoughts and beliefs: drafted with AI assistance under my direction and a structured methodology, but the questions, the conclusions, and the theology are mine, and they are still developing.

The manuscript, *The Debt-Debtor Thesis of Redemption*, is a biblical theology of redemption from a Biblical Unitarian christology — seventeen chapters and three appendices, roughly 78,000 words. The working thesis: humanity incurred a debt to God through Adam's disobedience; God established death as the penalty; redemption required a corresponding human representative — the last Adam — whose lifelong willing obedience culminated in sacrificial death; and the Father vindicated him by resurrection.

Three features may be of interest to you specifically:

1. **A strict, auditable Bible-only method.** Every claim is tagged `[E]` (explicit text), `[I-band]` (strong implication), or `[H-band]` (hypothesis), and every non-explicit claim carries an evidence sketch with evidence-for, evidence-against, and a band justification. The boundary between what the text states and what the argument infers is checkable line by line.
2. **The load-bearing argument pair.** Chapter 11 argues why the redeemer must be human — the same-order correspondence drawn from the Adam–Christ parallel of Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15. Chapter 15 argues why one man's obedience suffices for many, from the one-act/many-recipients structure of Romans 5:18–19 and 1 Corinthians 15:20–22.
3. **Appendix B pressure-tests the hard high-Christology texts** — John 1:1, *monogenēs*, Philippians 2:6–11, Colossians 1:15–20, Hebrews 1, John 20:28, Titus 2:13, 2 Peter 1:1. These are the passages your podcast taught me to read slowly, against the grain of the creeds. The appendix sketches alternative readings from the text alone; I expect the three books on my shelf are where those readings have been argued most rigorously, and I am looking forward to finding out. The project also uses the Revised English Version among its reference translations.

I would be grateful for honest engagement with any single piece of it — the correspondence argument, the sufficiency mechanism, or the hard-texts appendix — whether as written comments, a conversation, or simply a pointer at the weakest link. The full manuscript is available in any form you prefer.

With respect and gratitude for your work,

Andrew Jochl
