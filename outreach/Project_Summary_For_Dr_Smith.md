## Thesis

Humanity incurred a debt to God through Adam's disobedience. God established death as the penalty for sin. Redemption therefore required a corresponding human representative — a man in the same order as the debtor — whose lifelong willing obedience culminated in sacrificial death. Jesus Christ fulfills this role as the last Adam, and the Father vindicated the obedient Son by raising him from the dead, creating the new humanity in him.

The spine verse is Romans 5:19: *"For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous."*

## Scope and method

- **Bible only.** No commentary, creed, church father, or modern scholar is cited as evidence for any theological claim. Secondary positions are mentioned only to be identified or dismissed. The argument must work from the text alone.
- **Auditable tagging.** Every claim is tagged `[E]` (the text states it directly), `[I-band]` (strong implication; high/mid/low), or `[H-band]` (hypothesis; high/mid/low). Every `[I]` and `[H]` claim carries an evidence sketch: evidence for, evidence against, and a band justification. A reader can audit every inference against the cited text.
- **Translations.** ESV (primary) and NASB 1995 (word-order cross-check); reference set: Young's Literal, Literal Standard Version, Rotherham's Emphasized Bible, the Revised English Version, and the Concordant Literal Version.
- **Negative scope.** The manuscript does not set out to prove or refute the Trinity, the deity of Christ in the Nicene metaphysical sense, or the immortality of the soul; it argues positively for what the text presents about redemption's mechanics.

## The ten research questions

1. Who is God according to Scripture, and how does his character define justice?
2. What exactly did Adam lose?
3. What passed from Adam to humanity?
4. Why must the Redeemer be human?
5. Does Scripture present Christ's active obedience and sacrificial death as inseparable?
6. Why is one righteous man's obedience sufficient for many?
7. What is the relationship between the Father's will and Jesus' will?
8. What role do innocence, children, and moral accountability play?
9. How does Israel's history of disobedience prepare for the Messiah?
10. Does the Old Testament teach that obedience is greater than sacrifice?

## Structure (five parts, seventeen chapters, three appendices)

**Part I — Foundations.** God's character defines justice (Ch 1: corresponding justice, not arbitrary punishment, from Exodus 34:6–7 and the paired mercy-justice texts). Humanity was created in God's image for covenantal obedience and dominion (Ch 2). God's command to Adam carried a declared death-penalty (Ch 3; the declaration is `[E]` regardless of the covenant-label question).

**Part II — Consequences of Adam's sin.** Adam's act was covenantal transgression, and the *kind* of act determines the *kind* of redemption (Ch 4, from Romans 5:12–21 and 1 Corinthians 15). Chapter 5 inventories eleven losses (relational rupture, curse, toil, mortality, expulsion, blocked tree of life, creation's futility). Chapter 6 argues death passed to all — `[E]` from Romans 5:12 and 1 Corinthians 15:22 — with ontic transmission at `[I-high]`, forensic imputation left open at `[I-mid]`, and Ezekiel 18:20 engaged as the limit on direct imputation.

**Part III — The pattern of disobedience.** Childhood as a real developmental moral category (Ch 7). Disobedience as repeatable and intensifying (Ch 8, Romans 1:18–32). Israel's history as the covenant-scale witness to the pattern (Ch 9). The prophetic critique: obedience above sacrifice — not the rejection of sacrifice as an institution, but of sacrifice disconnected from the obedience the covenant requires (Ch 10: 1 Samuel 15:22–23; Hosea 6:6; Matthew 9:13, 12:7; Isaiah 1; Jeremiah 7; Amos 5; Micah 6:6–8; Psalm 51).

**Part IV — The Redeemer and the cross.** The two load-bearing chapters: Ch 11 establishes why the redeemer must be human; Ch 15 establishes why one man's obedience suffices for many. Ch 12 (lifelong willing obedience: John 8:29, 46; 1 Peter 2:22–23; Hebrews 4:15, 5:8–9; Philippians 2:8), Ch 13 (Gethsemane as the tested obedience: the real counter-will, the setting-aside, the non-granted petition), Ch 14 (the cross as payment: Isaiah 53; Romans 3:21–26; 2 Corinthians 5:21; Galatians 3:13; Hebrews 9).

**Part V — Vindication.** The resurrection is the Father's public declaration that the payment is accepted (Ch 16; the NT consistently attributes the resurrection to the Father — Acts 2:24; Romans 4:24–25; Hebrews 13:20 and a dozen more). Chapter 17 maps the ten questions to their chapters of resolution and states what remains open.

**Appendices.** B: alternative exegetical readings of the hard high-Christology texts. C: glossary. D: full research map — every cited text with its chapter load.

## The load-bearing argument pair

**Ch 11 — Why must the redeemer be human?** The debtor is human (Genesis 2:16–17; 3:17–19; Romans 5:12); the penalty is human death; therefore the representative must stand in the same order as the debtor. The same-order correspondence principle is drawn from the Adam–Christ *man : man* parallel (1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45–49) and the explicit shared-human-condition texts (1 Timothy 2:5–6; Hebrews 2:14–17; Galatians 4:4–5; Hebrews 4:15; 5:7–9). The pure-human representative conclusion is `[I-high]`; the principal counter-readings ("from heaven," "life-giving spirit," Philippians 2 "form of God") are pressure-tested in the chapter and in Appendix B.

**Ch 15 — Why one man for many?** Romans 5:18–19 and 1 Corinthians 15:20–22 state the one-act/many-recipients structure `[E]`. The claim that this structure *explains* why one corresponding man's obedience suffices is `[I-high]` — the strongest claim the manuscript makes, and the one held most accountable. The chapter then tests scope, faith/application, and the *en* relation without re-proving the same-order premise.

- **Translation set.** The Revised English Version is among the project's reference translations; its literal-Hebrew/Greek footnotes informed several word-order and lexical arguments in Ch 5 and Ch 13.
