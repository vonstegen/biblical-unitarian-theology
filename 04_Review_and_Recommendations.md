## 2. What the Project Is About

### Working thesis (refined from the chat)

> Humanity incurred a debt to God through Adam’s disobedience. God established death as the penalty for sin and does not rewrite His own justice. Redemption required a perfectly obedient *human* representative—the last Adam—whose willing lifelong obedience culminated in sacrificial death. Scripture presents this as corresponding justice (man for man), not God paying Himself as a God-man of a different order than Adam.

### Core pillars

1. **Divine justice** — Fixed penalty (death); God’s word does not fail  
2. **Representative humanity** — Adam ↔ Christ (Romans 5; 1 Corinthians 15)  
3. **Perfect / willing obedience** — Fall by disobedience; redemption by free obedience  
4. **Sacrificial death** — Obedient representative accepts the judicial consequence  
5. **Exaltation** — Father vindicates and exalts the obedient Son  

### Narrative arc that has emerged

```
Creation (made for faithful obedience)
    → Adam (one disobedience → death)
    → Israel (repeated covenant disobedience)
    → Prophets (sacrifice without obedience rejected)
    → Jesus (lifelong obedience → Gethsemane climax)
    → Cross (obedient man bears death-penalty)
    → Exaltation (Father vindicates the obedient Son)
    → New humanity
```

### Spine verse

**Romans 5:19** is the strongest single organizing text:

> “For as by the one man’s disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man’s obedience the many will be made righteous.”

Almost every major theme (Adam, debt, justice, representation, last Adam, redemption) can be tested through this verse.

## 3. Strengths

### 3.1 Distinctive contribution

You are **not** only arguing “Jesus wasn’t God.” You propose a **legal / representative principle**:

> Because God’s justice is perfect, the substitute must *correspond* to the debtor. If the debtor is humanity, the substitute must be humanity—not a fundamentally different kind of being than Adam.

That is more precise than generic non-Trinitarian polemic. It engages Anselm halfway (man must pay) and then diverges (a true man *can* pay via representative headship, without requiring a God-man for “infinite worth”).

### 3.2 Sound method

- Prefer **explicit biblical statements** over later technical formulations  
- Frame **research questions before conclusions**  
- Distinguish what Scripture *says*, what it *strongly implies*, and what *you infer*  
- Build a **positive case** (debt-debtor + last Adam), not only critique  

Keep this. It is the academic backbone of the project.

### 4.3 Chapter numbering chaos

Chapters 7–11 have been reassigned multiple times (innocence, Israel, prophetic witness, Gethsemane, two wills). Until one outline is frozen, you cannot write without constant renumbering.

### 4.4 Arguments that need careful handling

| Claim / habit | Risk | Recommendation |
|---------------|------|----------------|
| “No verse says X” | True for many technical terms; does not alone disprove a doctrine | Use as a *hermeneutical* point; win on **positive** exegesis |
| “Overpaid” if God-man | Not biblical language; easy to attack | Prefer “not corresponding representative” (Rom 5 language) |
| “God didn’t care about sacrifices” | Overstates; God instituted sacrifice | Say: sacrifice *without* repentance/obedience was unacceptable |
| “Obedience to oneself doesn’t pay a debt” | Philosophical; not explicit in Scripture | Frame as research question; argue from Father/Son narrative |
| Jesus shared “same sinful nature plate” | Ambiguous; heresy traps if undefined | Define: shared *human condition / temptation world* without personal sin |
| Why one for many | Still underdeveloped | Center on **federal / representative headship** (Rom 5), not biology alone |
| Deity texts largely deferred | Academic readers will demand engagement | Treat John 1, Phil 2, Col 1–2, Heb 1, John 20:28 honestly in a dedicated section or appendix |

### 4.5 Secondary literature is thin

Chat mentions Anselm and Socinus lightly. A serious paper needs engagement with:

- Anselm, *Cur Deus Homo* (agree on “man must pay”; disagree on “only God can”)  
- Standard Romans commentaries on 5:12–21  
- Atonement surveys (e.g. representative / penal / moral-influence alternatives)  
- Biblical Unitarian and classical Trinitarian treatments of the last Adam  
- Secondary work on active obedience, Gethsemane, and “obedience better than sacrifice”

### 4.6 Scope creep

Vision has grown toward a **book-length biblical theology**. That is fine as a long-term archive. For a thesis or first manuscript:

- Pick **one spine** (Rom 5:19 + corresponding justice + willing human obedience)  
- Move side trails (Solomon/knowledge, Satan as parallel thesis, full childhood psychology) into appendices or later volumes  

### 4.7 Fairness to opposing views

When you argue from Gethsemane’s two wills, classical Trinitarians typically answer: **two persons** (Father and Son), and/or **two wills in the incarnate Christ** (dyothelitism). State that alternative fairly, then explain why your reading of the narrative remains preferable. Strength comes from engagement, not silence.

## 5. Perspective on the Core Claims

### 5.1 What is strongest

The **Adam–Christ parallel** in Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15 genuinely emphasizes *one man / one man*, disobedience / obedience, condemnation / justification. Building a theology of **corresponding human representation** from that is textually serious.

Gethsemane + Hebrews 5:7–8 strongly support **costly, willing** obedience—not a script that runs itself.

The prophetic “obedience over sacrifice” thread (1 Sam 15:22; Isa 1; Hos 6; Amos 5; Ps 51) is excellent preparation for arguing that **Christ’s sacrifice is inseparable from His obedience**.

### 5.2 What is promising but still hypothesis

- **Two debts** (death + failed obedience) — rich synthesis; mark as inference until each piece is shown from the text  
- **Eden/Gethsemane garden parallel** — theologically powerful; investigate whether the Gospels *intend* the parallel or whether it is a legitimate canonical reading  
- **Israel as intentional demonstration of inability** — historically true pattern; “intentional preparation for Messiah” needs careful OT theology, not assumption  
- **Children / innocence progression** — important for “what passed from Adam”; do not overclaim “children are very obedient”

### 5.3 What to reframe, not abandon

**Corresponding justice without “overpayment” language**

Keep:

> The representative must be truly human, corresponding to Adam, so that the Pauline one-man / one-man structure is preserved.

Drop or demote:

> A God-man would “overpay” the debt.

**Non-Trinitarian conclusions as results, not premises**

Your method is strongest when you say:

1. Here is what the text emphasizes (sent, obeys, dies, raised by God, last Adam)  
2. Here is what later formulas add  
3. Here is the reading that best fits the debt-debtor + representative frame  

Avoid leading every chapter with “the Trinity is false.” Lead with **justice, Adam, obedience, Christ**. Let Christology conclusions follow the argument.

### 5.4 Open questions that must eventually be answered

1. What exactly did Adam lose?  
2. What passed to humanity—death, guilt, corrupted desire, environment of sin, or some combination?  
3. When does moral accountability begin?  
4. Why is one righteous man’s obedience sufficient for many?  
5. Did Christ share our *condition* without sharing personal sin—and how does Scripture say that?  
6. Is “debt of obedience” explicit in Paul or a legitimate inference from Rom 5:19?  
7. How do key high-Christology passages fit (or resist) your framework?  
8. How does free forgiveness language (e.g. Matt 18 debt parable) relate to necessary payment?

## 8. Methodology Recommendations

### 8.1 Academic tone

Prefer:

- “This thesis argues…”  
- “This paper investigates whether…”  
- “Scripture emphasizes…”  

Avoid early overclaim:

- “This proves…”  
- “There is no possible other reading…”  

### 8.2 Every chapter template

1. Central research question (one sentence)  
2. Scope and limits  
3. Key passages  
4. Exegesis / biblical theology  
5. Working conclusion (labeled provisional until manuscript stage)  
6. Open sub-questions  
7. How this chapter feeds the next  

### 8.3 Explicit / implication / inference tags

When drafting, tag claims:

- **[E]** Explicit in the text  
- **[I]** Strong implication from context  
- **[H]** Hypothesis / inference to be tested  

Example: “Debt of obedience” = likely **[H]** built from Rom 5:19 **[E]** plus systematic synthesis.

### 8.4 Language to prefer

| Prefer | Instead of |
|--------|------------|
| Corresponding representative | Overpayment / underpayment |
| Willing obedience | Automatic sinlessness (as if no struggle) |
| Sacrifice without covenant faithfulness is rejected | God didn’t care about sacrifices |
| Last Adam / man Christ Jesus | Polemic-first “not a God-man” in every paragraph |
| Research question | Settled conclusion mid-exploration |

## 10. Suggested Formal Thesis Statement (draft for 00_Master)

You may adopt or revise this for Version 1.0:

> This project argues that, according to Scripture, humanity’s debt to God arose through the disobedience of one man, Adam, and that God Himself established death as the judicial consequence of sin. Because God’s justice and word do not fail, redemption required a corresponding human representative who would render the faithful obedience Adam failed to render and willingly accept the death that divine justice required. Jesus Christ fulfills this role as the last Adam: the perfectly obedient man whose lifelong submission to the Father—climaxing in Gethsemane and culminating in the cross—constitutes the biblical pattern of redemption. The Old Testament prepares this reading by portraying Israel’s repeated covenant disobedience and by teaching that sacrifice without obedience is unacceptable to God. The thesis therefore investigates redemption as the union of perfect human obedience and willing sacrificial death under the justice of God, tested primarily through Romans 5 and related biblical witnesses.
