# Translation Bias and Limitations Documentation

> **Purpose.** This document records, with sourced citations, the known
> biases, limitations, and editorial decisions of every translation in
> the project's citation set. The project uses these works — none is
> rejected — but each is treated as a *reading with a frame* rather than
> as a transparent window onto the original text. Where a translation's
> choices smooth a passage in a way that bears on the project's
> arguments, the smoothing is named here.
>
> **Source constraint reminder.** All primary research material is the
> Bible text itself. These translation-bias assessments come from
> *secondary* documentation (publishers' prefaces, scholarly reviews,
> textual-criticism literature) — they are not used as *evidence* for any
> theological claim. They are used only to flag where a translation
> editorial frame may have shaped a rendering, so the user can compare
> across translations and weight readings accordingly.
>
> **Canonical citation set:** ESV (primary), NASB (primary); YLT, LSV,
> Rotherham's Emphasized Bible, REV, CLV (reference). KJV/NKJV excluded
> (see §8).

## 1. English Standard Version (ESV) — Primary

**Publisher:** Crossway (2001; 2011 update; 2016 update; 2025 minor update)
**Textual basis (NT):** Nestle-Aland 27/28 (UBS 4/5); critical text, Westcott-Hort family
**Textual basis (OT):** Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia (BHS)
**Translation Oversight Committee (TOC):** ~13–14 members with broad evangelical credentials, including Dr. Lane T. Dennis (chair), Dr. Vern Sheridan Poythress (NT chair), Dr. Clifford John Collins (OT chair), Dr. Wayne Grudem, Dr. Frank Thielman, Dr. Dane Ortlund, Dr. R. Kent Hughes, Dr. Peter J. Williams, Dr. Justin Taylor, Dr. Kevin DeYoung. **J.I. Packer** served as General Editor; **Leland Ryken** as literary stylist. Supported by 50+ Translation Review Scholars and an Advisory Council.

**Sources:** [ESV Oversight Committee page](https://www.esv.org/about/oversight-committee/); [ESV Translation Philosophy](https://www.esv.org/about/translation-philosophy/); [ESV Preface](https://www.esv.org/preface/); [Crossway 2025 update article](https://www.crossway.org/articles/esv-bible-translation-update/); [2025 ESV text changes PDF](https://uploads.crossway.org/excerpt/esv-2025-text-changes.pdf).

### 1.1 Theological / doctrinal frame

The TOC explicitly states translators "believe that the Bible is the inerrant Word of God" and that the translation is to serve the church. Translation decisions are made within that frame. Packer emphasized "respect readers by not going beyond linguistic requirements or foreclosing interpretation" — this is the official interpretive philosophy.

**Implication:** Where the Greek/Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous and a doctrinal commitment favors one reading, the ESV's choice will generally be the orthodox one.

### 1.2 Known lexical choices that bear on this project

The clearest documented case is the rendering of *μονογενής* (*monogenēs*) in John's Gospel and 1 John. The ESV (2001) renders this as "only" or "only Son" (John 1:14, 18; 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9), continuing the RSV's departure from the traditional KJV "only begotten." The rationale:

- Mainstream 20th-century Greek lexicography treats *monogenēs* as primarily meaning "one of a kind / unique / only" (from *monos* + *genos*, "only / offspring"), not from *gennaō* ("beget"). In familial contexts (the 9 NT occurrences) it functions as "only [offspring]."
- The "begotten" rendering reflects Latin Vulgate *unigenitus* and later creedal development (Nicene "begotten, not made"), which the ESV committee judged should not be imported back into the translation of the Greek term itself.
- The 2025 ESV update revised John 1:18 from "the only God" to "God the only Son" — incorporating *some* descent/sonship implication while still not restoring "begotten." This shows the committee evolving its position; they explicitly considered both lexical and manuscript evidence.

**Sources:** [bible-researcher.com/esv.html](https://www.bible-researcher.com/esv.html); [Crossway ESV update article](https://www.crossway.org/articles/esv-bible-translation-update/); [Jeff Riddle on John 1:18](http://www.jeffriddle.net/2016/08/word-magazine-56-text-note-john-118.html); [Ezra Institute on the textual variant](https://www.ezrainstitute.com/resources/examining-the-textual-variant-in-john-118); [Logos forum discussion](https://community.logos.com/forums/topic/82974-why-does-esv-omit-begotten-in-john-118-316/).

**Implication for this project:** Passages where "only begotten" appears in KJV/NKJV (John 1:14, 18; 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9; Hebrews 11:17) will read as "only Son" or "one and only" in ESV. The lexical question — whether *monogenēs* implies *eternal generation* in the Johannine writings — is exactly the kind of claim the project should tag as `[H]` (hypothesis), not `[E]`, because the lexical evidence goes both ways and the ESV committee's choice does not foreclose the orthodox reading. See Appendix B.A.B.1.

### 1.3 Documented TOC decisions on specific lexical questions

These show the TOC working through exactly the kinds of Greek/Hebrew ambiguities the project will face:

**Hebrew *'ebed* / Greek *doulos* — "slave," "bondservant," "servant" (2011 update):** The TOC debated this at Tyndale House (filmed by BBC, 2010). Decision: render context-sensitively. "Slave" where absolute ownership is in view (e.g., Romans 6); "bondservant" for limited/contractual servitude (e.g., 1 Corinthians 7:21–24); "servant" for freer contexts (e.g., John 4:51). Footnotes identify the original terms and range of meaning.

**Genesis 3:16 / 4:7 — "desire for" vs "desire contrary to" / "and" vs "but" (2001 → 2016 → 2025 reversal):**
- **2001**: "Your desire shall be **for** your husband, **and** he shall rule over you" (with parallel in 4:7 for sin).
- **2016**: Changed to "**contrary to**" ... "**but**" (with footnote "Or *shall be toward*").
- **2025**: Reverted to "**for**" ... "**and**," with expanded footnote: "Or *to*, or *toward*, or *against*" (cross-referencing the parallel).

Rationale for the reversal: prefer a more natural rendering continuous with historic formal-equivalence translations (RSV, NASB, NKJV); avoid imposing an interpretive contrast not explicit in the Hebrew preposition *'el*; preserve the full range of interpretive options for readers rather than foreclosing them. This is a directly visible example of the TOC choosing to *not* decide a theological-interpretive question in the translation itself.

**John 1:18 (2025 update):** "the only God" → "God the only Son" — moved slightly toward sonship/descent while still not restoring "begotten."

**Other notable TOC principles:** Retain "behold" for *hinneh*/*idou*; do **not** capitalize deity pronouns (no original basis; absent from KJV stream); consistent "Christ" for *Christos*; YHWH as LORD (small caps), with distinctions for *'adonay* + YHWH.

**Source:** [Crossway 2025 update article](https://www.crossway.org/articles/esv-bible-translation-update/); [ESV Preface](https://www.esv.org/preface/).

### 1.4 Textual variant choices

The ESV prefers the Westcott-Hort / Nestle-Aland critical text over the Textus Receptus / Byzantine majority tradition. This affects:

- **John 1:18** — prefers *monogenēs theos* ("only God") over Byzantine *ho monogenēs huios* ("the only-begotten Son").
- **Mark 16:9–20** (longer ending) — in brackets as a later addition; many verses are footnoted or absent.
- **John 7:53–8:11** (Pericope Adulterae) — in brackets as not original to John's Gospel.
- **1 John 5:7** (Comma Johanneum, "the three that bear record in heaven") — omitted entirely; not in any known Greek manuscript before the 16th century.

**Sources:** same Crossway/Ezra Institute materials cited above; standard textual criticism literature.

### 1.5 Footnote philosophy

Footnotes are integral to the ESV translation (included in most editions) and serve to:

- Inform readers of textual variations and how the team resolved them
- Indicate significant alternative readings ("Or ...")
- Explain technical terms or difficult readings
- Provide original-language identification and ranges of meaning
- Support the essentially literal approach by preserving options without over-interpreting in the main text

**Source:** [ESV Preface](https://www.esv.org/preface/).

**Implication for this project:** When citing ESV for passages with footnotes, the footnote is part of the citation. A claim like "the ESV says X" without acknowledging the footnote alternative would misrepresent the translation.

### 1.6 Where ESV is *strong* for this project

- Generally recognized as the most-literal English translation in widespread evangelical use while remaining readable. ([bible-researcher.com review](https://www.bible-researcher.com/esv.html))
- Modern critical-text basis matches the textual mainstream of NT/OT scholarship.
- Crossway publishes translation notes, alternate readings, and textual-variant footnotes openly — readers can audit every choice.
- TOC has shown willingness to revise decisions (e.g., Genesis 3:16 / 4:7 reversal) when original-language analysis supports it — a healthy indicator of process integrity.

### 1.7 Where ESV is *weak* for this project

- The translation committee's *evangelical orthodoxy* commitment means lexical choices that bear on Christology (especially the *monogenēs* cluster) have been pre-decided in an orthodox direction.
- John 1:18, John 3:16, and 1 John 4:9 are exactly the verses a debt-debtor thesis will want to scrutinize most carefully. Use YLT and LSV (which retain some TR/Majority Text readings) and Rotherham (with its explicit anti-creedal framing) as cross-checks.
- The TOC is willing to *reverse* itself — that's good, but it also means the ESV is not as textually stable as the 2016 "Permanent Text" announcement implied. Quote with edition (e.g., "ESV 2016" or "ESV 2025") when the version matters.

## 2. New American Standard Bible (NASB) — Primary

**Publisher:** The Lockman Foundation (1971; updated 1977, 1995, 2020)
**Textual basis (NT, 1995):** Nestle-Aland 26th edition
**Textual basis (NT, 2020):** NA28 / ECM (Editio Critica Maior); updated lexical databases (HALOT, BDAG)
**Textual basis (OT, 1995):** Biblia Hebraica
**Textual basis (OT, 2020):** BHS / BHQ
**Translation philosophy:** "Essentially literal" word-for-word, with explicit evangelical commitments

### 2.1 Theological / doctrinal frame

The Lockman Foundation states a "Fourfold Aim" that includes giving "the Lord Jesus Christ His proper place (the place the Word gives Him)." Translators explicitly believe in the inerrancy of Scripture.

**Source:** [Lockman NASB page](https://www.lockman.org/new-american-standard-bible-nasb/); [Theopedia, NASB](https://theopedia.com/new-american-standard-bible); [Wikipedia, NASB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_American_Standard_Bible).

### 2.2 Gender language and the 2020 update

The 1995 NASB retained traditional masculine generics ("man," "brothers," "he who") consistent with formal equivalence and historical English Bible usage. The 2020 update adopted what Lockman calls "gender-accurate" language:

- "Brothers" (*adelphoi*) is rendered "brothers *and sisters*" with the addition in italics when addressing mixed Christian communities.
- Generic "man/men" becomes "person/people/one" in over 1,200 places where the original is generic rather than specifically male.
- Singular "he" is retained for generic singular antecedents (no "singular they" adoption) to preserve the formal equivalence principle.
- Male-specific terms (*anēr*, Hebrew *geber*) are never broadened.

**Source:** [Lockman gender-accurate language statement](https://www.lockman.org/gender-accurate-language-in-the-nasb-2020/); [Passion to Know More discussion](https://www.passiontoknowmore.com/post/2019/02/27/gender-neutral-is-here-to-stay).

### 2.3 Reception of the 2020 update — published criticism

The NASB 2020 update generated substantive published criticism and defense. Two representative strands:

**Critical (Messianic Light review):** The NASB 2020 "has wandered slightly away from the strictly literal path and produced a bible which is claimed to be 'gender-accurate' but in practice introduces interpretive choices about inclusivity that were not present in the 1995 edition." The criticism is that "gender-accurate" is functionally indistinguishable from "gender-neutral" for many readers, and that the change to render *adelphoi* as "brothers *and sisters*" introduces an interpretive addition where the Greek is genuinely plural-male-by-default. ([Messianic Light NASB 2020 Review](https://messianiclight.com/nasb2020-review/))

**Positive (Evangelical Bible Review):** The 2020 update "further improves accuracy where possible, modernizes language, and improves readability" while retaining formal equivalence. Lockman's own framing is that the changes are "indisputable" only when the original audience would have understood women to be included. ([Evangelical Bible Review on NASB 2020](https://evangelicalbible.com/nasb2020/); [Christian Book Distributors NASB page](https://www.christianbook.com/page/bibles/about-bibles/about-translations/about-the-nasb))

**Forum-level engagement:** On Puritan Board and Baptist Board, conservative readers expressed concern that the 2020 update's gender-accuracy principle introduces interpretive choices that the 1995 edition avoided. ([Puritan Board NASB 2020 thread](https://puritanboard.com/threads/nasb-2020-thoughts.115769/); [Baptist Board NASB 2020 thread](https://www.baptistboard.com/threads/new-american-standard-bible-2020-update.110611/))

**Implication for this project:** The 1995 and 2020 NASB editions are not interchangeable for citation. The 1995 edition's traditional masculine generics preserve the formal-equivalence argument that "man" can be generic; the 2020 edition's "brothers and sisters" rendering of *adelphoi* introduces an interpretive addition. The project should cite the 1995 NASB unless specifying otherwise.

### 2.4 Where NASB is *strong* for this project

- Generally considered the most literally precise English translation widely available. (Multiple reviews, including [Lockman NASB page](https://www.lockman.org/new-american-standard-bible-nasb/))
- Same critical-text basis as ESV — same textual-critical baseline.
- Word-order and syntax are preserved more strictly than ESV, making it ideal for syntax-based arguments (e.g., Romans 5:12–21 word-order observations).
- 1995 edition is widely available and well-known.

### 2.5 Where NASB is *weak* for this project

- Same evangelical doctrinal frame as ESV (§1.1).
- 1995 edition's archaic phrasing ("the one who believes *into him*") can be awkward; 2020 update improves readability but loses some word-order cues.
- Like ESV, omits Mark 16:9–20 and 1 John 5:7 (Comma Johanneum) as later additions.
- The 2020 update's gender-accuracy principle, while defended by Lockman, has drawn published criticism that the changes introduce interpretive choices the 1995 edition avoided.

## 3. Young's Literal Translation (YLT) — Reference

**Author:** Robert Young (1862; revised 1887, 1898)
**Textual basis:** Textus Receptus (majority / Byzantine text-type for NT); Masoretic Text for OT
**License:** Public domain

### 3.1 Translation philosophy

YLT is the most rigorously word-for-word translation in widespread English use. Young attempted to preserve every Greek/Hebrew tense form, particle, and word-order choice in English. The translator's stated aim (per the 1898 Preface): "a literal and idiomatic rendering of the Original Hebrew and Greek Texts."

**Source:** [CCEL, Young's Preface](https://www.ccel.org/bible/ylt/ylt.htm); [Wikipedia, YLT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%2527s_Literal_Translation); [bible-researcher.com review](https://www.bible-researcher.com/young.html).

### 3.2 Scholarly criticism of the tense system

The most distinctive — and most criticized — feature of YLT is Young's tense system. Young insisted that Hebrew does not have a future tense in the way English does, and that Greek tenses (especially the aorist) should be rendered with English forms that distinguish aspect (punctiliar vs. linear) rather than collapsing them into simple past/present/future. YLT therefore uses:

- "Is saying" / "is doing" for Greek/Hebrew present
- "Will say" / "will do" for Greek/Hebrew future
- "Said" / "did" for aorist
- "Is being said" for Greek/Hebrew passive

**Daniel B. Wallace** (Dallas Theological Seminary, author of *Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics*) is one of the most cited critics of YLT's tense approach. Wallace has argued in multiple publications that the English present-tense rendering of Greek aorists is misleading and that readers will misinterpret the aspect. ([Wallace, "Fifteen Myths about Bible Translation"](https://danielbwallace.com/2012/10/08/fifteen-myths-about-bible-translation/))

**Defense of YLT's tense system** has come from some Jewish and Greek-studies scholars who argue that the traditional English "past tense" rendering of Greek/Hebrew actually distorts the biblical sense of *olam* / *aion* (eternity / age). The Wikipedia article on YLT notes that Young's use of English present tense rather than past tense has been supported by scholars ranging from the medieval Jewish rabbi Rashi (who advised that Hebrew tenses are aspectual rather than temporal) to modern Hebrew grammarians. ([Wikipedia, YLT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%2527s_Literal_Translation))

### 3.3 The *olam* / *aion* problem specifically

YLT renders *olam* (Hebrew) and *aion* (Greek) as expressions like "to the age of the age" or "age-during" — avoiding "eternal" or "forever" because of a doctrinal commitment (held by some Restoration Movement and Christadelphian-influenced interpreters) that the words denote ages rather than timeless eternity. This is a *doctrinally-driven* translation choice that affects passages like Daniel 12:2 ("everlasting life" / "life to the age"), Matthew 25:46 ("eternal punishment" / "punishment to the age"), and Revelation 10:6 ("time no longer" / "time not yet").

**Sources:** [Wikipedia, YLT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%2527s_Literal_Translation); [puritanboard.com discussion](https://puritanboard.com/threads/ylt.84192/); [Got Questions on YLT](https://www.gotquestions.org/Youngs-Literal-Translation-YLT.html).

### 3.4 Other known biases and limitations

- **English is highly unnatural.** Common criticisms: awkward syntax, archaic tense forms, strange English construction. ([bible-researcher.com review](https://www.bible-researcher.com/young.html); [puritanboard.com discussion](https://puritanboard.com/threads/ylt.84192/))
- **Textual basis is Textus Receptus / Majority Text**, not the modern critical text. This means YLT includes passages that NA28/UBS excludes: Mark 16:9–20 (full), John 7:53–8:11 (Pericope Adulterae, footnoted in modern editions), and **the Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7–8)** in some printings.
- **No notes, no commentary.** YLT offers no translator's notes or alternate readings.
- **Doctrinally driven choices** beyond just tense: the Restoration Movement / Christadelphian-influenced reading of *olam* / *aion* and related terms shapes renderings.

### 3.5 Where YLT is *strong* for this project

- Surfaces Greek/Hebrew structure that smoother translations hide. Useful for syntax-based arguments.
- TR/Majority Text readings where they differ from the critical text — gives a perspective on what the majority manuscript tradition says.
- Public domain — can be quoted freely.
- The Restoration Movement / non-Trinitarian frame aligns with the project's perspective on Trinitarian readings.

### 3.6 Where YLT is *weak* for this project

- English is so awkward that readers will misread or dismiss it. Always pair with a smoother primary citation (ESV or NASB).
- TR/Majority Text basis means Comma Johanneum and other disputed passages appear in the text.
- **The *olam* / *aion* rendering is itself a doctrinally-driven choice** — it pre-decides a contested theological question (the duration of punishment; the nature of eternity) that the user may want to evaluate independently. Do not cite YLT's *olam* / *aion* renderings as evidence about what the text says; cite them as evidence about how one Restoration Movement reader rendered the text.

## 4. Literal Standard Version (LSV) — Reference

**Publisher:** Covenant Press (2020, 1st edition)
**Textual basis (NT):** Textus Receptus / Majority Text; consults Alexandrian and other traditions in specific instances
**Textual basis (OT):** Masoretic Text; with LXX and Dead Sea Scrolls consulted in specific instances
**License:** Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike (CC BY-SA) — openly licensed
**Author team:** Anonymous; published by Covenant Press, associated with the Covenant Christian Coalition

### 4.1 Translation philosophy

LSV is a major revision of YLT, modernizing the English while preserving the strict word-for-word approach. Uses "YHWH" for the divine name (rather than "LORD" or "Yahweh"), preserves verb tenses, and includes TR/Majority Text readings (longer ending of Mark, Johannine Comma considerations) with brackets and footnotes marking text-critical choices.

**Source:** [LSV Preface](https://www.lsvbible.com/p/the-preface-to-literal-standard-version.html); [Wikipedia, LSV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_Standard_Version); [Covenant Press LSV page](https://www.covenantpress.org/p/literal-standard-version.html); [Religion News on LSV release](https://religionnews.com/2020/02/12/the-brand-new-literal-standard-version-bible-translation-released-this-month/).

### 4.2 Reception

The LSV's reception has been mixed but generally positive on accuracy. Three representative strands:

**Positive (Amazon reviewer summary; widely repeated):** "It is the most literal version of the modern translations -- more literal and formal than the NASB." ([Amazon LSV listing](https://www.amazon.com/Holy-Bible-Literal-Standard-Version/dp/0999892487))

**Positive (Academic reviewer — Association for Mormon Letters):** "The LSV is the most literal translation of The Holy Bible, with significant improvement over previous literal translations." ([AML review by Jeffrey Needle](https://www.associationmormonletters.org/reviews/older-reviews/the-holy-bible-literal-standard-version-reviewed-by-jeffrey-needle/))

**Critical (Academic Biblical subreddit):** "The LSV does not read particularly well, and it has some 'big words' that I have had to look up definitions for. That said, as far as accuracy ..." — the criticism acknowledges the literal-rendering strength but notes readability limitations. ([r/AcademicBiblical discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/AcademicBiblical/comments/1j1fx59/literal_standard_version_of_the_bible/))

**Mixed (Religion News):** "Despite the translation being strictly word-for-word, the translators managed to maintain ease of readability throughout, although the target audience appears to be readers seeking formal equivalence rather than devotional reading." ([Religion News](https://religionnews.com/2020/02/12/the-brand-new-literal-standard-version-bible-translation-released-this-month/))

### 4.3 Known biases and limitations

- **New translation (2020) — limited scholarly review** compared to older versions. Multiple reviewers note that while the translation is impressively literal, the team is small and review by external textual scholars has been limited.
- **Textual basis is TR/Majority Text** — same caveats as YLT (§3.4) regarding Comma Johanneum, longer Mark, Pericope Adulterae.
- **Published by a Christian-nationalist ministry** (Covenant Christian Coalition, per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_Standard_Version)). The translation choices themselves are not flagged as carrying theological bias, but the publishing context should be noted.
- **Anonymous authorship and small team.** Translation decisions are not transparently attributable to specific scholars.
- **Inherits YLT's tense system and its limitations** — the same aspect-vs-time rendering and the same *olam* / *aion* problem apply.

### 4.4 Where LSV is *strong* for this project

- Modern English while preserving YLT-style word-for-word literalism — easier to read than YLT.
- CC BY-SA licensed — can be quoted and adapted freely.
- TR/Majority Text readings preserved with markers.

### 4.5 Where LSV is *weak* for this project

- Same TR-basis caveats as YLT.
- Same *olam* / *aion* doctrinal choice as YLT (LSV is a major revision of YLT).
- Limited scholarly review and anonymous team — use as a secondary cross-check rather than as a primary text.
- Readability criticisms suggest the audience is narrower than the LSV's publisher claims.

## 5. Rotherham's Emphasized Bible — Reference

**Author:** Joseph Bryant Rotherham (1902 complete edition; NT first 1897)
**Textual basis (NT):** Westcott-Hort 1892 revision (the same critical-text family that ESV/NASB descend from)
**Textual basis (OT):** Christian David Ginsburg's Masoretico-critical edition (with assured emendations)
**License:** Public domain

### 5.1 Translation philosophy

Rotherham's goal was to place modern readers "in as good a position as that occupied by the reader of the first century for understanding the Apostolic Writings" — explicitly *anti-creedal*, written from a "simple, apostolic viewpoint free of later traditional or mediaeval overlays." The translator was associated with the Churches of Christ (Restoration Movement), which has historically rejected creedal Trinitarianism.

The defining feature is *emphasis marking* — typographical devices (indentations, special symbols, varying type) that mark Hebrew/Greek word order, parallelism, narrative vs. speech, parenthetical phrases, and divine proclamations. This makes Rotherham uniquely useful for parsing difficult passages.

Notable translation choice: *baptizō* is rendered as "immerse" (not "baptize") — reflecting Restoration Movement theology but also the literal Greek sense.

**Source:** [Wikipedia, Rotherham's Emphasized Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotherham%27s_Emphasized_Bible); [bible-researcher.com review](https://www.bible-researcher.com/rotherham.html); [Wikisource, The Emphasised Bible Introduction](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Emphasised_Bible/Introduction).

### 5.2 Scholarly reception

The most cited scholarly endorsement comes from **John R. Kohlenberger III**, who wrote the foreword to the 1994 printing and whose assessment is repeatedly quoted:

> "The Emphasized Bible is one of the most innovative and thoroughly researched translations ever done by a single individual. Its presentation of emphases, parallelisms, narrative vs. speech distinctions, and structural elements of the Hebrew and Greek originals is unparalleled in English Bible translation."

(Kohlenberger is the author/editor of *The Strongest Strong's Exhaustive Concordance of the Bible* and a respected Bible-reference scholar; this is a primary scholarly endorsement, not a publisher blurb.)

**Sources:** [Wikipedia, Rotherham's Emphasized Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotherham%27s_Emphasized_Bible); [ETC Events Rotherham page](https://etcevents.co.uk/products/rotherhams-emphasized-bible); [Logos product page](https://www.logos.com/product/5934/the-emphasized-bible); [Spec's Fine Books 1902 edition](https://specsfinebooks.com/products/date-joseph-bryant-rotherham-the-emphasised-bible-vol-i-iv).

Independent reviews echo this: "Reading Rotherham's Bible translation is about as close as you can get to reading the scriptures in the original Hebrew and Greek, without ..." ([Bible Versions Discussion Board](https://www.tapatalk.com/groups/bibleversiondiscussionboard/rotherham-emphasized-bible-t376.html)).

### 5.3 Known biases and limitations

- **Restoration Movement / non-creedal frame.** Rotherham's stated goal is to read the New Testament "free of later traditional or mediaeval overlays." This is a conscious editorial decision that influences translation choices — particularly where creedal terms or Trinitarian formulations might otherwise have been imported.
- **Westcott-Hort critical text basis** — same as ESV/NASB; same textual-variant choices.
- **Archaic English** from 1902; emphasis marks require some learning to read fluently.
- **"Immerse" for *baptizō*** is a Restoration Movement distinctive. The literal Greek sense ("to immerse, dip") supports the rendering, but the translation choice reflects Rotherham's polemical context.
- **Single-author work.** Rotherham was one scholar doing the whole translation, not a committee. This means consistency but also means no peer review during translation.

### 5.4 Where Rotherham is *strong* for this project

- Anti-creedal framing aligns with the project's biblical-unitarian perspective.
- Emphasis marks surface Greek/Hebrew structure better than any other translation in the set.
- Same Westcott-Hort critical text basis as ESV/NASB — textual variants match the project's primary translations.
- Public domain.
- Genuinely literal with maximum word-order preservation.
- Kohlenberger's scholarly endorsement is independent and substantive.

### 5.5 Where Rotherham is *weak* for this project

- The anti-creedal framing means *some* translation choices may have been *intentionally* chosen to avoid Trinitarian readings, not just to be literal. Where Rotherham and ESV/NASB differ, the difference should be examined: is it Rotherham being more literal, or Rotherham reading his Restoration Movement lens into the text?
- The "immerse" rendering of *baptizō* is a known example of editorial frame shaping a translation choice.
- Single-author work without committee review means translation choices were not systematically vetted.

## 6. Revised English Version (REV) — Reference

**Publisher:** Spirit & Truth Fellowship International (revbible.com)
**Translation philosophy:** "Optimal equivalence" — formal-leaning but functional where the Hebrew/Greek form would obscure English sense
**Textual basis (NT):** Nestle-Aland 28th edition (2012)
**Textual basis (OT):** Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia 4th edition (2004)
**First printed NT:** 2014; ongoing revisions

### 6.1 Theological / doctrinal frame

REV is published by Spirit & Truth Fellowship International (STFI), a biblical unitarian ministry. Their stated doctrinal position: the Father (Yahweh) is the one true God; Jesus Christ is God's only begotten human Son and Messiah (not eternally pre-existent as a divine person or co-equal God); holy spirit is a gift from God (not a person); humans have free will; the soul is mortal.

The translation preface acknowledges upfront: *"the theological understanding of the translator always affects the way he or she translates the text. Thus, the degree to which the Bible is accurately translated is dependent upon the degree to which the translator accurately understands what the text of the Bible is saying."*

**Source:** [REV Introduction](https://revbible.com/Information/2/Introduction_to_the_REV_Commentary); [Spirit & Truth REV page](https://stfi.org/revised-english-version/); [REV iOS App Store description](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/rev-bible-app/id1381443899); [REV About page](https://revbible.com/about); [REV Introduction (alternate URL)](https://www.revisedenglishversion.com/info/introduction).

### 6.2 Translation features

- "Yahweh" for YHWH (not "LORD" or "Jehovah")
- "Holy Spirit" (uppercase) for God; "holy spirit" (lowercase) for the gift — distinguished by context
- Extensive footnotes giving literal Hebrew/Greek readings when the translation is idiomatic
- Italics for words not in the original text
- Brackets for passages highly doubtful as original (e.g., some TR additions)
- No capitalizing of pronouns when antecedent is ambiguous (deliberate to avoid interpretive bias)

### 6.3 Known biases and limitations

- **Explicitly unitarian-affiliated.** Translation choices will systematically smooth Trinitarian readings where the underlying Greek/Hebrew is ambiguous. STFI states this is the point of the project.
- **Very limited scholarly review** — small team, single ministry, recent translation.
- **Same critical-text basis as ESV/NASB** — same textual-variant choices.
- **The editorial frame is the most explicit of any translation in the set.** REV is upfront about it. Where ESV's editorial frame is implicit (committee orthodoxy), REV's is explicit (unitarian). Both need to be flagged; neither is "neutral."

### 6.4 Where REV is *strong* for this project

- Provides a unitarian-aligned reading alongside the orthodox-aligned ESV/NASB readings — useful for understanding how a unitarian scholar reads a passage.
- Same critical text as primary translations.
- Extensive footnotes showing literal Hebrew/Greek — excellent for parsing.
- Explicit acknowledgement of translator bias is *more* honest than translations that pretend neutrality.

### 6.5 Where REV is *weak* for this project

- Cannot be cited as primary evidence because of the explicit unitarian frame.
- New translation, limited review, small team.
- The same editorial smoothing that aligns with the project's perspective also makes it impossible to use as a neutral cross-check.

## 7. Concordant Literal Version (CLV) — Reference

**Author/compiler:** Adolph Ernst Knoch (mid-20th century)
**Publisher:** Concordant Publishing Concern (founded 1909)
**Translation philosophy:** Hyper-literal, concordant vocabulary, word-for-word

### 7.1 Theological / doctrinal frame

Concordant Publishing Concern is associated with a doctrinal package that includes universal reconciliation (the view that all will ultimately be saved), dispensational/covenant theology distinctive to the movement, and British-Israelist leanings (the belief that the Anglo-Saxon peoples are descended from the lost tribes of Israel). Knoch's doctrinal commitments shaped his translation choices.

**Source:** [Wikipedia, Concordant Version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concordant_Version); [Concordant Publishing Concern, Introduction to the CLNT](https://www.concordant.org/version/intro-to-the-concordant-new-testament/); [Concordant expositions on universal reconciliation](https://www.concordant.org/expositions/human-destiny/the-salvation-of-the-unbeliever/); [historical context](https://www.triumphofmercy.com/blog/distinguishing-biblical-universalism); [Concordant history](https://www.facebook.com/theHistoryofJehovahsWitnesses.NewBook.NY2013/posts/adolph-ernst-knoch-and-the-concordant-publishing-concern-refuge-for-former-witne/1147630491947303/).

### 7.2 Translation philosophy and methodology

The CLV's distinguishing feature is *concordant vocabulary*: each Greek/Hebrew root word is consistently translated with the same English word across the entire Bible. This produces a hyper-literal text useful for tracing word usage across testaments, but also produces some unusual English renderings. Knoch's stated aim was to surface the underlying Greek semantic structure without letting English idiom override it.

Publication history: the New Testament appeared in installments from around 1914–1926 (with major revisions in 1931, 1944/International, 1966/Memorial Edition renaming it Concordant Literal New Testament, and 1976 updates); the Old Testament followed later in multi-volume form.

**Source:** [Wikipedia, Concordant Version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concordant_Version); [C-S-S Concordant introduction](https://c-s-s.website/introduction/).

### 7.3 Known biases and limitations

- **Doctrinal package influence.** Universal reconciliation and British-Israelism are not mainstream positions; their influence on translation choices is documented. Specific renderings tied to these doctrines should be flagged. Most prominently, the CLV's rendering of *aionios* (commonly translated "eternal" or "everlasting") often reflects the universalist position that the term denotes an *age* rather than timeless eternity — affecting passages like Matthew 25:46 ("eternal punishment" → "age-during chastisement").
- **Very small audience** — small translation team, niche readership, limited scholarly engagement with the text.
- **Hyper-literal to the point of opacity.** Concordant vocabulary produces English that no other translator uses — comparison against ESV/NASB can be hard.
- **Anti-Trinitarian** in the same general sense as REV but with different doctrinal specifics.
- **Single-author work** (Knoch, with later editorial revisions by Concordant Publishing Concern after his 1965 death).

### 7.4 Where CLV is *strong* for this project

- Concordant vocabulary is genuinely useful for tracing Greek/Hebrew word usage across testaments.
- Hyper-literal rendering surfaces the underlying Greek structure.
- Public domain (Knoch died 1965).

### 7.5 Where CLV is *weak* for this project

- Doctrinal package (universal reconciliation, British-Israelism) shapes translation choices that are not aligned with the project's perspective.
- Very small audience means limited peer review and limited ability to verify translation choices against scholarly consensus.
- Concordant vocabulary makes comparison against other translations cumbersome.
- The *aionios* / *aion* / universalist rendering of "eternal" passages is a doctrinally-driven choice — do not cite CLV's renderings of these passages as evidence about what the text says.

## 8. KJV / NKJV — Excluded

### 8.1 Why excluded

Both translations preserve 17th-century editorial decisions that the modern critical editions (NA28 / BHS) reject as not original to the biblical text:

- **1 John 5:7–8 (the Comma Johanneum)** — "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one." Absent from every known Greek manuscript before the 16th century. Earliest Greek attestation is in a 14th-century Latin manuscript (Codex Ottobianus); the reading appears to have entered the Latin tradition via a marginal gloss in response to Trinitarian controversies. ([Wikipedia, Comma Johanneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma_Johanneum); standard textual criticism literature.)
- **Mark 16:9–20** (longer ending) — bracketed as a later addition by virtually all modern translations.
- **John 7:53–8:11** (Pericope Adulterae) — bracketed as not original to John's Gospel.

### 8.2 The specific Trinity-bias concern

The KJV preserves readings that, in their textual tradition, smooth Trinitarian interpretation:

- The Comma Johanneum is the most explicit case — a Trinitarian proof-text that exists in no manuscript older than the 16th century but is in the KJV.
- *Monogenēs* in John 1:14, 18; 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9 is rendered "only begotten" — preserving the Latin/Vulgate *unigenitus* rendering that carries the Nicene "eternal generation" implication.
- The KJV uses "God" in passages where the underlying text is ambiguous.

**Sources:** [bible-researcher.com/comma-johanneum.html](https://www.bible-researcher.com/comma.html); standard introductions to NT textual criticism (Metzger, *The Text of the New Testament*; Aland & Aland, *The Text of the New Testament*).

### 8.3 Why exclusion rather than inclusion as reference

The project is not excluding the KJV because of theological opposition. It is excluding the KJV because the KJV's textual basis is a 17th-century edition (Textus Receptus in the Elzevir/Stephanus recension) that has been substantially superseded by 19th- and 20th-century manuscript discoveries. For a project whose stated source-constraint is *the Bible alone*, using a textual basis that demonstrably contains later additions that are not in the underlying manuscripts violates that constraint.

The Bible, the original-language manuscripts, are the canonical text. The KJV is one particular English rendering of a 17th-century selection of manuscripts. It is neither the original nor the most accurate available.

## 11. Cross-translation controversy map

The following are 5–8 specific doctrinal/textual questions where the translations disagree in ways that materially affect the project's claims. For each: which translations land on which side; what the underlying Greek/Hebrew says; what the `[E]/[I-band]/[H-band]` tag should be.

### Controversy 1 — *monogenēs* lexical choice (see §10.6)

- **"Only"** (ESV 2016 in John 1:18, ESV 2025 "only Son" revised)
- **"Only-begotten"** (YLT, LSV, Rotherham, REV, NASB 1995, CLV)
- **Underlying Greek**: *monogenēs*; etymology debated — *monos* ("only") + *genos* ("kind") vs *monos* + *gennaō* ("beget")
- **Tag**: `[H]` for the doctrinal implication of eternal generation; `[E]` for the basic sense "one of a kind / unique" attested in extra-biblical Greek

### Controversy 2 — *dikaiōma* in Romans 5:18 (see §10.7)

- **"Act of righteousness"** (ESV, NASB 1995, REV)
- **"Righteous declaration"** / "judicial decision" (YLT, LSV, Rotherham, CLV)
- **Underlying Greek**: *dikaiōma*; can mean "righteous act," "decree," "justification," "ordinance"
- **Tag**: `[I]` for which sense is operative; `[E]` for the substantive claim (one man's act / decree results in justification for many)

### Controversy 3 — *hilastērion* in Romans 3:25 (see §10.8)

- **"Propitiation"** (ESV, NASB 1995, LSV, REV)
- **"Mercy seat"** (YLT)
- **"Propitiatory covering"** / "propitiatory shelter" (Rotherham, CLV)
- **Underlying Greek**: *hilastērion*; LXX word for the kapporet / atonement cover (Exodus 25:17–22; Leviticus 16)
- **Tag**: `[I]` for the typological sense; `[E]` for the substantive claim (Christ is set forth as a mercy-seat / covering through faith in his blood)

### Controversy 4 — *morphē* in Philippians 2:6 (see §10.9)

- **"Form"** (all translations)
- **Underlying Greek**: *morphē*; debated between essential nature (Nicene metaphysical) and outward expression / mode of being
- **Tag**: `[H]` for the metaphysical reading; `[E]` for the basic semantic range ("form," "condition," "mode of being")

### Controversy 5 — Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7–8)

- **Omitted** (ESV, NASB, YLT, LSV, REV, CLV — all modern critical-text translations)
- **Included** (KJV, NKJV — Textus Receptus tradition)
- **Underlying Greek**: Comma appears in no Greek manuscript before the 16th century; Latin-only tradition
- **Tag**: This is a textual-variant question, not an `[E]/[I-band]/[H-band]` claim. The passage as it appears in KJV is not in the original text. **Do not cite.**

### Controversy 6 — *aionios* / *olam* — "eternal" or "age-during"?

- **"Eternal" / "everlasting"** (ESV, NASB, Rotherham — mainstream renderings of *aionios*)
- **"Age-during"** (YLT, LSV, CLV — Restoration Movement / universalist renderings)
- **Underlying Greek/Hebrew**: *aionios* (Greek); *olam* (Hebrew); both terms are debated regarding whether they denote timeless eternity or ages
- **Tag**: `[I]` for the duration question; `[E]` for the substantive claim that *some kind* of lasting state is in view

### Controversy 7 — "Form of a servant" (*morphē doulou*) in Philippians 2:7

- **"Form of a servant"** (ESV, NASB, LSV, REV, CLV)
- **"Form of a servant"** (YLT, Rotherham) — same English but without capitalizing
- **Underlying Greek**: *morphē doulou* — same *morphē* as in verse 6 but applied to servant condition
- **Tag**: `[E]` for the basic claim (took the form of a servant); the metaphysical reading follows from Controversy 4

### Controversy 8 — *kathistēmi* / *kathistamai* in Romans 5:19 (see §10.7)

- **"Made"** (ESV, NASB, REV)
- **"Constituted"** (YLT, LSV, Rotherham, CLV)
- **Underlying Greek**: *kathistēmi* — "to make, appoint, constitute, establish"; carries a legal/positional sense
- **Tag**: `[I]` for whether the sense is declarative ("made") or constitutive ("constituted"); `[E]` for the substantive claim (the many are established as sinners/righteous through the one man's act)

## 12. Relative weight verdict

Per-translation recommendation for how heavily each should be cited in the final manuscript:

| Translation | Recommendation | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **ESV** | **Primary citation**, default | Modern critical-text basis; readable while literal; extensive footnotes; evangelical orthodox frame flagged but doesn't foreclose exegesis |
| **NASB 1995** | **Primary citation**, secondary to ESV | Most literal English available; same critical-text basis as ESV; use 1995 specifically for traditional *adelphoi* rendering |
| **YLT** | **Reference**, heavy use for parsing | Word-for-word surfaces Greek structure; TR/Majority Text readings; **flag *olam* / *aion* renderings as doctrinally driven** |
| **LSV** | **Reference**, secondary to YLT | Modernized YLT-style literalism; CC BY-SA; **inherits YLT's limitations including *olam* / *aion* problem** |
| **Rotherham** | **Reference**, heavy use for parsing | Kohlenberger-endorsed; emphasis marks unique; anti-creedal frame aligns with project; **examine differences from ESV/NASB for editorial-frame influence** |
| **REV** | **Reference, never primary** | Explicit unitarian frame; useful for understanding unitarian-tradition readings; cannot be cited as primary evidence |
| **CLV** | **Reference, never primary** | Concordant vocabulary useful for tracing Greek word usage; doctrinal package (universal reconciliation, British-Israelism) shapes renderings; **do not cite *aionios* renderings** |
| **KJV / NKJV** | **Excluded** | Textual-basis issues; preserves Comma Johanneum and other non-original readings |

### Practical citation guidance

- **Default chapter citation**: ESV (NASB where word-order or syntax arguments matter).
- **When ESV/NASB disagree**: cite both readings; tag the disagreement as `[I]` or `[H]` depending on whether the choice reflects a textual issue (Controversies 5–6) or a lexical/philosophical one (Controversies 1–4).
- **When the underlying Greek/Hebrew is ambiguous**: consult YLT (for surface structure), Rotherham (for apostolic reading), and REV (for unitarian reading); treat the resulting comparison as evidence about the *range of plausible readings* of the underlying text.
- **For *olam* / *aion* / *aionios* passages**: do not rely on YLT/LSV/CLV renderings; consult mainstream translations and the LXX / OT context.
- **For textual variants** (Comma Johanneum, longer Mark, Pericope Adulterae): ESV's brackets / omissions reflect the modern critical text; the omitted text is *not* in the original language manuscripts and cannot be cited as `[E]`.

## 13. Recommended practice when translation differences bear on a claim

When two translations disagree in a passage the project cites:

1. **Quote both readings** (per X.4 in the outline).
2. **Note the textual basis of each translation** if it differs (e.g., TR/Majority Text vs. critical text).
3. **Note the translation committee's stated theological frame** if it may have shaped the choice.
4. **Tag the claim `[H]`** if the divergence reflects an editorial choice rather than a clear reading of the underlying text. Tag `[E]` only when both primary translations agree and the meaning is unambiguous.
5. **Where the disagreement is text-critical** (a passage that exists in one tradition but not another), note this in the manuscript and refer to Appendix D for the full research map.

This discipline lets the project use the entire translation set as evidence about the biblical text *and* about how scholarly traditions have read it, without leaning on any single translation's editorial frame as if it were the underlying text itself.
