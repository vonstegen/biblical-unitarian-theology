# Translation Bias and Limitations Documentation

> **Purpose.** This document records, with sourced citations, the known
> biases, limitations, and editorial decisions of every translation in
> the project's citation set. The project uses these works — none is
> rejected — but each is treated as a *reading with a frame* rather than
> as a transparent window onto the original text. Where a translation's
> choices smooth a passage in a way that bears on the project's
> arguments, the smoothing is named here.
>
> **Source constraint reminder.** All primary research material is the
> Bible text itself. These translation-bias assessments come from
> *secondary* documentation (publishers' prefaces, scholarly reviews,
> textual-criticism literature) — they are not used as *evidence* for any
> theological claim. They are used only to flag where a translation
> editorial frame may have shaped a rendering, so the user can compare
> across translations and weight readings accordingly.
>
> **Canonical citation set:** ESV (primary), NASB (primary); YLT, LSV,
> Rotherham's Emphasized Bible, REV, CLV (reference). KJV/NKJV excluded
> (see §7).

---

## 1. English Standard Version (ESV) — Primary

**Publisher:** Crossway (2001; 2016 revision; 2025 minor update)
**Textual basis (NT):** Nestle-Aland 27/28 (UBS 4/5); critical text, Westcott-Hort family
**Textual basis (OT):** Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia (BHS)
**Translation committee:** ~14 Translation Oversight Committee members chaired by J.I. Packer; Wayne Grudem among advisors; explicit commitment to "historic Christian orthodoxy"

### 1.1 Theological / doctrinal frame

The Translation Oversight Committee is committed to historic Christian orthodoxy. The preface states translators "believe that the Bible is the inerrant Word of God" and that the translation is to serve the church. Translation decisions are made within that frame.

**Implication:** Where the Greek/Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous and a doctrinal commitment favors one reading, the ESV's choice will generally be the orthodox one.

### 1.2 Known lexical choices that bear on this project

The clearest documented case is the rendering of *μονογενής* (*monogenēs*) in John's Gospel and 1 John. The ESV (2001) renders this as "only" or "only Son" (John 1:14, 18; 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9), continuing the RSV's departure from the traditional KJV "only begotten." The rationale:

- Mainstream 20th-century Greek lexicography treats *monogenēs* as primarily meaning "one of a kind / unique / only" (from *monos* + *genos*, "only / offspring"), not from *gennaō* ("beget"). In familial contexts (the 9 NT occurrences) it functions as "only [offspring]."
- The "begotten" rendering reflects Latin Vulgate *unigenitus* and later creedal development (Nicene "begotten, not made"), which the ESV committee judged should not be imported back into the translation of the Greek term itself.
- The 2025 ESV update revised John 1:18 to "God the only Son" — incorporating *some* descent/sonship implication while still not restoring "begotten."

**Sources:** [bible-researcher.com/esv.html](https://www.bible-researcher.com/esv.html); [Crossway ESV update article](https://www.crossway.org/articles/esv-bible-translation-update/); [Jeff Riddle on John 1:18](http://www.jeffriddle.net/2016/08/word-magazine-56-text-note-john-118.html); [Ezra Institute on the textual variant](https://www.ezrainstitute.com/resources/examining-the-textual-variant-in-john-118); [Logos forum discussion](https://community.logos.com/forums/topic/82974-why-does-esv-omit-begotten-in-john-118-316/).

**Implication for this project:** Passages where "only begotten" appears in KJV/NKJV (John 1:14, 18; 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9; Hebrews 11:17) will read as "only Son" or "one and only" in ESV. The lexical question — whether *monogenēs* implies *eternal generation* in the Johannine writings — is exactly the kind of claim the project should tag as `[H]` (hypothesis), not `[E]`, because the lexical evidence goes both ways and the ESV committee's choice does not foreclose the orthodox reading. See Appendix B.A.B.1.

### 1.3 Textual variant choices

The ESV prefers the Westcott-Hort / Nestle-Aland critical text over the Textus Receptus / Byzantine majority tradition. This affects:

- **John 1:18** — prefers *monogenēs theos* ("only God") over Byzantine *ho monogenēs huios* ("the only-begotten Son"). Same verse as the lexical question above; both textual and lexical dimensions matter.
- **Mark 16:9–20** (longer ending) — in brackets as a later addition; many verses are footnoted or absent.
- **John 7:53–8:11** (Pericope Adulterae) — in brackets as not original to John's Gospel.
- **1 John 5:7** (Comma Johanneum, "the three that bear record in heaven") — omitted entirely; not in any known Greek manuscript before the 16th century.

**Sources:** same Crossway/Ezra Institute materials cited above; standard textual criticism literature.

### 1.4 Where ESV is *strong* for this project

- Generally recognized as the most-literal English translation in widespread evangelical use while remaining readable. ([bible-researcher.com review](https://www.bible-researcher.com/esv.html))
- Modern critical-text basis matches the textual mainstream of NT/OT scholarship, which is the right baseline for a "what does the text say" project.
- Crossway publishes the translation notes, alternate readings, and textual-variant footnotes openly — readers can audit every choice.

### 1.5 Where ESV is *weak* for this project

- The translation committee's *evangelical orthodoxy* commitment means lexical choices that bear on Christology (especially the *monogenēs* cluster) have been pre-decided in an orthodox direction. A biblical unitarian reading of the same passages may find the translation choice obscures alternative lexical arguments.
- John 1:18, John 3:16, and 1 John 4:9 are exactly the verses a debt-debtor thesis will want to scrutinize most carefully. Use YLT and LSV (which retain some TR/Majority Text readings) and Rotherham (with its explicit anti-creedal framing) as cross-checks in those passages.

## 2. New American Standard Bible (NASB) — Primary

**Publisher:** The Lockman Foundation (1971; updated 1977, 1995, 2020)
**Textual basis (NT, 1995):** Nestle-Aland 26th edition
**Textual basis (OT, 1995):** Biblia Hebraica
**Translation philosophy:** "Essentially literal" word-for-word, with explicit evangelical commitments

### 2.1 Theological / doctrinal frame

The Lockman Foundation states a "Fourfold Aim" that includes giving "the Lord Jesus Christ His proper place (the place the Word gives Him)." Translators explicitly believe in the inerrancy of Scripture. Translation choices are made within this frame.

**Source:** [Lockman NASB page](https://www.lockman.org/new-american-standard-bible-nasb/); [Theopedia, NASB](https://theopedia.com/new-american-standard-bible); [Wikipedia, NASB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_American_Standard_Bible).

### 2.2 Gender language and the 2020 update

The 1995 NASB retained traditional masculine generics ("man," "brothers," "he who") consistent with formal equivalence and historical English Bible usage. The 2020 update adopted what Lockman calls "gender-accurate" language:

- "Brothers" (*adelphoi*) is rendered "brothers *and sisters*" with the addition in italics when addressing mixed Christian communities.
- Generic "man/men" becomes "person/people/one" in over 1,200 places where the original is generic rather than specifically male.
- Singular "he" is retained for generic singular antecedents (no "singular they" adoption) to preserve the formal equivalence principle.
- Male-specific terms (*anēr*, Hebrew *geber*) are never broadened.

**Source:** [Lockman gender-accurate language statement](https://www.lockman.org/gender-accurate-language-in-the-nasb-2020/); [Passion to Know More discussion](https://www.passiontoknowmore.com/post/2019/02/27/gender-neutral-is-here-to-stay).

**Implication for this project:** Both the 1995 and 2020 NASB are usable; the project should cite the 1995 edition unless specifying otherwise, since the 2020 changes are stylistic rather than affecting any of the doctrinal passages central to the debt-debtor thesis. If the project wants traditional masculine-generic phrasing (which preserves the formal-equivalence argument that "man" can be generic), use the 1995 NASB.

### 2.3 Where NASB is *strong* for this project

- Generally considered the most literally precise English translation widely available. (Multiple reviews, including [Lockman NASB page](https://www.lockman.org/new-american-standard-bible-nasb/))
- Same critical-text basis as ESV — same textual-critical baseline.
- Word-order and syntax are preserved more strictly than ESV, making it ideal for syntax-based arguments (e.g., Romans 5:12–21 word-order observations).

### 2.4 Where NASB is *weak* for this project

- Same evangelical doctrinal frame as ESV (§1.1).
- 1995 edition's archaic phrasing ("the one who believes *into him*") can be awkward; 2020 update improves readability but loses some word-order cues.
- Like ESV, omits Mark 16:9–20 and 1 John 5:7 (Comma Johanneum) as later additions.

## 3. Young's Literal Translation (YLT) — Reference

**Author:** Robert Young (1862; revised 1887, 1898)
**Textual basis:** Textus Receptus (majority / Byzantine text-type for NT); Masoretic Text for OT
**License:** Public domain

### 3.1 Translation philosophy

YLT is the most rigorously word-for-word translation in widespread English use. Young attempted to preserve every Greek/Hebrew tense form, particle, and word-order choice in English. The translator's stated aim (per the 1898 Preface): "a literal and idiomatic rendering of the Original Hebrew and Greek Texts."

**Source:** [CCEL, Young's Preface](https://www.ccel.org/bible/ylt/ylt.htm); [Wikipedia, YLT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%2527s_Literal_Translation); [bible-researcher.com review](https://www.bible-researcher.com/young.html).

### 3.2 Known biases and limitations

- **English is highly unnatural.** The translation's pursuit of literal fidelity produces English that no native speaker would write or speak. Common criticisms: awkward syntax, archaic tense forms ("will" for future, "is" for present), strange English construction. ([bible-researcher.com review](https://www.bible-researcher.com/young.html); [puritanboard.com discussion](https://puritanboard.com/threads/ylt.84192/))
- **Textual basis is Textus Receptus / Majority Text**, not the modern critical text. This means YLT includes passages that NA28/UBS excludes: Mark 16:9–20 (full), John 7:53–8:11 (Pericope Adulterae, footnoted in modern editions), and **the Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7–8)** in some printings. ([Wikipedia, YLT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%2527s_Literal_Translation))
- **No notes, no commentary.** YLT offers no translator's notes or alternate readings — just the rendered text.
- **Archaic English even for its time.** YLT's tense-form system (e.g., "is saying," "will say," "did say") was already unusual in 1898.

### 3.3 Where YLT is *strong* for this project

- Surfaces Greek/Hebrew structure that smoother translations hide. Useful for syntax-based arguments (Romans 5:12–21 word-order; Philippians 2:6–11 prepositional phrases).
- TR/Majority Text readings where they differ from the critical text — gives a perspective on what the majority manuscript tradition says. (For a project that takes the Bible alone as authority and doesn't privilege either text-type, this is genuinely useful information.)
- Public domain — can be quoted freely.

### 3.4 Where YLT is *weak* for this project

- The English is so awkward that readers will misread or dismiss it. Always pair with a smoother primary citation (ESV or NASB).
- TR/Majority Text basis means Comma Johanneum and other disputed passages may appear in the text — readers unfamiliar with textual criticism may take these as "what the Bible says" when they are manuscript-variant readings. Treat as evidence about the majority tradition, not as the canonical text.

## 4. Literal Standard Version (LSV) — Reference

**Publisher:** Covenant Press (2020, 1st edition)
**Textual basis (NT):** Textus Receptus / Majority Text; consults Alexandrian and other traditions in specific instances
**Textual basis (OT):** Masoretic Text; with LXX and Dead Sea Scrolls consulted in specific instances
**License:** Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike (CC BY-SA) — openly licensed
**Author team:** Anonymous; published by Covenant Press, associated with the Covenant Christian Coalition

### 4.1 Translation philosophy

LSV is a major revision of YLT, modernizing the English while preserving the strict word-for-word approach. Uses "YHWH" for the divine name (rather than "LORD" or "Yahweh"), preserves verb tenses, and includes TR/Majority Text readings (longer ending of Mark, Johannine Comma considerations) with brackets and footnotes marking text-critical choices.

**Source:** [LSV Preface](https://www.lsvbible.com/p/the-preface-to-literal-standard-version.html); [Wikipedia, LSV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_Standard_Version); [Covenant Press LSV page](https://www.covenantpress.org/p/literal-standard-version.html).

### 4.2 Known biases and limitations

- **New translation (2020) — limited scholarly review** compared to older versions. Multiple reviewers note that while the translation is impressively literal, the team is small and review by external textual scholars has been limited. ([review summary](https://www.associationmormonletters.org/reviews/older-reviews/the-holy-bible-literal-standard-version-reviewed-by-jeffrey-needle/))
- **Textual basis is TR/Majority Text** — same caveats as YLT (§3.4) regarding Comma Johanneum, longer Mark, Pericope Adulterae. These readings are preserved with footnote indicators but remain in the text.
- **Published by a Christian-nationalist ministry** (Covenant Christian Coalition, per [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_Standard_Version)). The translation choices themselves are not flagged as carrying theological bias, but the publishing context should be noted.
- **Anonymous authorship and small team.** Translation decisions are not transparently attributable to specific scholars.

### 4.3 Where LSV is *strong* for this project

- Modern English while preserving YLT-style word-for-word literalism — easier to read than YLT.
- CC BY-SA licensed — can be quoted and adapted freely, useful for derivative projects.
- TR/Majority Text readings preserved with markers — for a project that wants to engage with the majority manuscript tradition.

### 4.4 Where LSV is *weak* for this project

- Same TR-basis caveats as YLT.
- Limited scholarly review and anonymous team — use as a secondary cross-check rather than as a primary text.
- The publisher's association with a particular Christian-nationalist cultural context is worth flagging but does not by itself indicate translation-level bias.

## 5. Rotherham's Emphasized Bible — Reference

**Author:** Joseph Bryant Rotherham (1902 complete edition; NT first 1897)
**Textual basis (NT):** Westcott-Hort 1892 revision (the same critical-text family that ESV/NASB descend from)
**Textual basis (OT):** Christian David Ginsburg's Masoretico-critical edition (with assured emendations)
**License:** Public domain

### 5.1 Translation philosophy

Rotherham's goal was to place modern readers "in as good a position as that occupied by the reader of the first century for understanding the Apostolic Writings" — explicitly *anti-creedal*, written from a "simple, apostolic viewpoint free of later traditional or mediaeval overlays." The translator was associated with the Churches of Christ (Restoration Movement), which has historically rejected creedal Trinitarianism.

The defining feature is *emphasis marking* — typographical devices (indentations, special symbols, varying type) that mark Hebrew/Greek word order, parallelism, narrative vs. speech, parenthetical phrases, and divine proclamations. This makes Rotherham uniquely useful for parsing difficult passages.

Notable translation choice: *baptizō* is rendered as "immerse" (not "baptize") — reflecting Restoration Movement theology but also the literal Greek sense.

**Source:** [Wikipedia, Rotherham's Emphasized Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotherham%27s_Emphasized_Bible); [bible-researcher.com review](https://www.bible-researcher.com/rotherham.html); [Wikisource, The Emphasised Bible Introduction](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Emphasised_Bible/Introduction).

### 5.2 Known biases and limitations

- **Restoration Movement / non-creedal frame.** Rotherham's stated goal is to read the New Testament "free of later traditional or mediaeval overlays." This is a conscious editorial decision that influences translation choices — particularly where creedal terms or Trinitarian formulations might otherwise have been imported.
- **Westcott-Hort critical text basis** — same as ESV/NASB; same textual-variant choices.
- **Archaic English** from 1902; emphasis marks require some learning to read fluently.
- **"Immerse" for *baptizō*** is a Restoration Movement distinctive. The literal Greek sense ("to immerse, dip") supports the rendering, but the translation choice reflects Rotherham's polemical context.

### 5.3 Where Rotherham is *strong* for this project

- Anti-creedal framing aligns with the project's biblical-unitarian perspective.
- Emphasis marks surface Greek/Hebrew structure better than any other translation in the set.
- Same Westcott-Hort critical text basis as ESV/NASB — textual variants match the project's primary translations.
- Public domain.
- Genuinely literal with maximum word-order preservation.

### 5.4 Where Rotherham is *weak* for this project

- The anti-creedal framing means *some* translation choices may have been *intentionally* chosen to avoid Trinitarian readings, not just to be literal. Where Rotherham and ESV/NASB differ, the difference should be examined: is it Rotherham being more literal, or Rotherham reading his Restoration Movement lens into the text?
- The "immerse" rendering of *baptizō* is a known example of editorial frame shaping a translation choice.

## 6. Revised English Version (REV) — Reference

**Publisher:** Spirit & Truth Fellowship International (revbible.com)
**Translation philosophy:** "Optimal equivalence" — formal-leaning but functional where the Hebrew/Greek form would obscure English sense
**Textual basis (NT):** Nestle-Aland 28th edition (2012)
**Textual basis (OT):** Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia 4th edition (2004)
**First printed NT:** 2014; ongoing revisions

### 6.1 Theological / doctrinal frame

REV is published by Spirit & Truth Fellowship International (STFI), a biblical unitarian ministry. Their stated doctrinal position: the Father (Yahweh) is the one true God; Jesus Christ is God's only begotten human Son and Messiah (not eternally pre-existent as a divine person or co-equal God); holy spirit is a gift from God (not a person); humans have free will; the soul is mortal.

The translation preface acknowledges upfront: *"the theological understanding of the translator always affects the way he or she translates the text. Thus, the degree to which the Bible is accurately translated is dependent upon the degree to which the translator accurately understands what the text of the Bible is saying."*

**Source:** [REV Introduction](https://revbible.com/Information/2/Introduction_to_the_REV_Commentary); [Spirit & Truth REV page](https://stfi.org/revised-english-version/); [REV iOS App Store description](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/rev-bible-app/id1381443899); [REV About page](https://revbible.com/about); [REV Introduction (alternate URL)](https://www.revisedenglishversion.com/info/introduction); [REV John 14:17 commentary example](https://revbible.com/comm/John/14/17).

### 6.2 Translation features

- "Yahweh" for YHWH (not "LORD" or "Jehovah")
- "Holy Spirit" (uppercase) for God; "holy spirit" (lowercase) for the gift — distinguished by context
- Extensive footnotes giving literal Hebrew/Greek readings when the translation is idiomatic
- Italics for words not in the original text
- Brackets for passages highly doubtful as original (e.g., some TR additions)
- No capitalizing of pronouns when antecedent is ambiguous (deliberate to avoid interpretive bias)

### 6.3 Known biases and limitations

- **Explicitly unitarian-affiliated.** Translation choices will systematically smooth Trinitarian readings where the underlying Greek/Hebrew is ambiguous. STFI states this is the point of the project.
- **Very limited scholarly review** — small team, single ministry, recent translation.
- **Same critical-text basis as ESV/NASB** — same textual-variant choices.
- **The editorial frame is the most explicit of any translation in the set.** REV is upfront about it. Where ESV's editorial frame is implicit (committee orthodoxy), REV's is explicit (unitarian). Both need to be flagged; neither is "neutral."

### 6.4 Where REV is *strong* for this project

- Provides a unitarian-aligned reading alongside the orthodox-aligned ESV/NASB readings — useful for understanding how a unitarian scholar reads a passage.
- Same critical text as primary translations — useful for *translating from* even when not cited as primary.
- Extensive footnotes showing literal Hebrew/Greek — excellent for parsing.
- Explicit acknowledgement of translator bias is *more* honest than translations that pretend neutrality.

### 6.5 Where REV is *weak* for this project

- Cannot be cited as primary evidence because of the explicit unitarian frame.
- New translation, limited review, small team.
- The same editorial smoothing that aligns with the project's perspective also makes it impossible to use as a neutral cross-check.

## 7. Concordant Literal Version (CLV) — Reference

**Author/compiler:** Adolph Ernst Knoch (mid-20th century)
**Publisher:** Concordant Publishing Concern (founded 1909)
**Translation philosophy:** Hyper-literal, concordant vocabulary, word-for-word

### 7.1 Theological / doctrinal frame

Concordant Publishing Concern is associated with a doctrinal package that includes universal reconciliation (the view that all will ultimately be saved), dispensational/covenant theology distinctive to the movement, and British-Israelist leanings (the belief that the Anglo-Saxon peoples are descended from the lost tribes of Israel). Knoch's doctrinal commitments shaped his translation choices.

**Source:** [Concordant Publishing Concern, Introduction to the CLNT](https://www.concordant.org/version/intro-to-the-concordant-new-testament/); [Concordant expositions on universal reconciliation](https://www.concordant.org/expositions/human-destiny/the-salvation-of-the-unbeliever/); [historical context](https://www.triumphofmercy.com/blog/distinguishing-biblical-universalism); [Concordant history](https://www.facebook.com/theHistoryofJehovahsWitnesses.NewBook.NY2013/posts/adolph-ernst-knoch-and-the-concordant-publishing-concern-refuge-for-former-witne/1147630491947303/).

### 7.2 Translation philosophy

The CLV's distinguishing feature is *concordant vocabulary*: each Greek/Hebrew root word is consistently translated with the same English word across the entire Bible. This produces a hyper-literal text useful for tracing word usage across testaments, but also produces some unusual English renderings.

### 7.3 Known biases and limitations

- **Doctrinal package influence.** Universal reconciliation and British-Israelism are not mainstream positions; their influence on translation choices is documented. Specific renderings tied to these doctrines should be flagged.
- **Very small audience** — small translation team, niche readership, limited scholarly engagement with the text.
- **Hyper-literal to the point of opacity.** Concordant vocabulary produces English that no other translator uses — comparison against ESV/NASB can be hard.
- **Anti-Trinitarian** in the same general sense as REV but with different doctrinal specifics.

### 7.4 Where CLV is *strong* for this project

- Concordant vocabulary is genuinely useful for tracing Greek/Hebrew word usage across testaments.
- Hyper-literal rendering surfaces the underlying Greek structure.
- Public domain (Knoch died 1965).

### 7.5 Where CLV is *weak* for this project

- Doctrinal package (universal reconciliation, British-Israelism) shapes translation choices that are not aligned with the project's perspective.
- Very small audience means limited peer review and limited ability to verify translation choices against scholarly consensus.
- Concordant vocabulary makes comparison against other translations cumbersome.

## 8. KJV / NKJV — Excluded

### 8.1 Why excluded

Both translations preserve 17th-century editorial decisions that the modern critical editions (NA28 / BHS) reject as not original to the biblical text:

- **1 John 5:7–8 (the Comma Johanneum)** — "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one." Absent from every known Greek manuscript before the 16th century. Earliest Greek attestation is in a 14th-century Latin manuscript (Codex Ottobianus); the reading appears to have entered the Latin tradition via a marginal gloss in response to Trinitarian controversies. ([Wikipedia, Comma Johanneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma_Johanneum); standard textual criticism literature.)
- **Mark 16:9–20** (longer ending) — bracketed as a later addition by virtually all modern translations.
- **John 7:53–8:11** (Pericope Adulterae) — bracketed as not original to John's Gospel.

### 8.2 The specific Trinity-bias concern

The KJV preserves readings that, in their textual tradition, smooth Trinitarian interpretation:

- The Comma Johanneum is the most explicit case — a Trinitarian proof-text that exists in no manuscript older than the 16th century but is in the KJV.
- *Monogenēs* in John 1:14, 18; 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9 is rendered "only begotten" — preserving the Latin/Vulgate *unigenitus* rendering that carries the Nicene "eternal generation" implication.
- The KJV uses "God" in passages where the underlying text is ambiguous (e.g., the Anglican ordinariate's discussion of these passages routinely notes them).

**Sources:** [bible-researcher.com/comma-johanneum.html](https://www.bible-researcher.com/comma.html); standard introductions to NT textual criticism (Metzger, *The Text of the New Testament*; Aland & Aland, *The Text of the New Testament*).

### 8.3 Why exclusion rather than inclusion as reference

The project is not excluding the KJV because of theological opposition. It is excluding the KJV because the KJV's textual basis is a 17th-century edition (Textus Receptus in the Elzevir/Stephanus recension) that has been substantially superseded by 19th- and 20th-century manuscript discoveries. For a project whose stated source-constraint is *the Bible alone*, using a textual basis that demonstrably contains later additions that are not in the underlying manuscripts violates that constraint.

The Bible, the original-language manuscripts, are the canonical text. The KJV is one particular English rendering of a 17th-century selection of manuscripts. It is neither the original nor the most accurate available.

## 10. Recommended practice when translation differences bear on a claim

When two translations disagree in a passage the project cites:

1. **Quote both readings** (per X.4 in the outline).
2. **Note the textual basis of each translation** if it differs (e.g., TR/Majority Text vs. critical text).
3. **Note the translation committee's stated theological frame** if it may have shaped the choice.
4. **Tag the claim `[H]`** if the divergence reflects an editorial choice rather than a clear reading of the underlying text. Tag `[E]` only when both primary translations agree and the meaning is unambiguous.
5. **Where the disagreement is text-critical** (a passage that exists in one tradition but not another), note this in the manuscript and refer to Appendix D for the full research map.

This discipline lets the project use the entire translation set as evidence about the biblical text *and* about how scholarly traditions have read it, without leaning on any single translation's editorial frame as if it were the underlying text itself.
