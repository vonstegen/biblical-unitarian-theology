## 14.1 The question

The central claim of this chapter is simple: the cross is the payment of the death penalty that God Himself established as the judicial consequence of sin. It is not a separate event detached from Christ’s life of obedience. It is the culmination of that obedience — the point at which the perfectly obedient man willingly accepted the penalty that divine justice required.

This claim joins Chapter 12 (the lifelong, willing obedience of Christ) and the present chapter into one inseparable unit. The death on the cross is not an independent transaction; it is the final act of the obedience that began in the incarnation and continued through Gethsemane. The acceptance criterion for the chapter is therefore whether the active obedience of Chapter 12 and the death described here stand as a single, coherent claim rather than two loosely related ideas.

The chapter also examines the New Testament’s own vocabulary for what happened at the cross — propitiation, redemption, bearing sin, blood, ransom — and shows that these terms are drawn from the text itself rather than imposed upon it.

## 14.2 Isaiah 53: the Suffering Servant

The most explicit Old Testament statement of a representative who bears the consequences of others’ sins is found in Isaiah 53.

> “Surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows; yet we esteemed him stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted. But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned — every one — to his own way; and the Lord has laid on him the iniquity of us all.” (Isaiah 53:4–6, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> “Yet it was the will of the Lord to crush him; he has put him to grief; when his soul makes an offering for guilt, he shall see his offspring; he shall prolong his days; the will of the Lord shall prosper in his hand. Out of the anguish of his soul he shall see and be satisfied; by his knowledge shall the righteous one, my servant, make many to be accounted righteous, and he shall bear their iniquities. Therefore I will divide him a portion with the many, and he shall divide the spoil with the strong, because he poured out his soul to death and was numbered with the transgressors; yet he bore the sin of many, and makes intercession for the transgressors.” (Isaiah 53:10–12, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

Several features are load-bearing. The Servant is pierced and crushed for the transgressions and iniquities of others. The Lord lays the iniquity of the many upon him. His soul is poured out to death. He makes an offering for guilt (*ʾāšām*). He bears the sin of many. The language is substitutionary and representative without being mechanical or coercive. The Servant is righteous, yet he accepts the consequences that belong to the unrighteous. This supplies the Old Testament pattern that the New Testament later applies to Jesus. `[E]`

## 14.3 Romans 3:21–26: propitiation by his blood

Paul’s densest single statement of the cross appears in Romans 3.

> “But now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law, although the Law and the Prophets bear witness to it — the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe. For there is no distinction: for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, and are justified by his grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus, whom God put forward as a propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith. This was to show God’s righteousness, because in his divine forbearance he had passed over former sins. It was to show his righteousness at the present time, so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus.” (Romans 3:21–26, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

Two terms dominate the passage. *Redemption* (*apolytrosis*) points to release secured by a price. *Propitiation* (*hilastērion*) is the term the Septuagint uses for the mercy seat (*kapporet*), the place where blood was applied on the Day of Atonement. God puts Christ forward as that propitiation “by his blood.” The purpose clause is explicit: the cross demonstrates that God is both just and the justifier. Justice is not set aside; it is satisfied in the death of the obedient representative. `[E]`

## 14.4 2 Corinthians 5:21: made to be sin

> “For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.” (2 Corinthians 5:21, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The statement is stark. The one who knew no sin is made to be sin *for our sake*. The purpose is that we might become the righteousness of God in him. The verse does not say that Christ became a sinner in character; it says he was made to be sin in a representative and judicial sense. The exchange is real: the sinless one bears what belongs to the sinful, and the sinful receive what belongs to the righteous. This is the language of expiation and identification, not of moral transformation of Christ himself. `[E]`

## 14.5 Galatians 3:13: redeemed from the curse

> “Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us — for it is written, ‘Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree.’” (Galatians 3:13, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

Paul quotes Deuteronomy 21:23 and applies it directly to the cross. The law pronounces a curse on the one hanged on a tree. Christ becomes that curse *for us*, and by that act he redeems (*exēgorasen* — bought out of the marketplace) those under the curse. The mechanism is substitutionary: he takes the position the law assigns to the law-breaker. The payment is his becoming the cursed one in our place. `[E]`

## 14.6 1 Peter 2:24 and 3:18: bore our sins / suffered for sins

> “He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree, that we might die to sin and live to righteousness. By his wounds you have been healed.” (1 Peter 2:24, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> “For Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit.” (1 Peter 3:18, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

Peter is direct. Christ bore our sins *in his body on the tree*. The location is the cross. The purpose is that we might die to sin and live to righteousness. In 3:18 the pattern is the same: the righteous suffered for the unrighteous in order to bring us to God. The suffering is once, and it is representative. The death is the means by which the righteous one stands in the place of the unrighteous. `[E]`

## 14.7 Hebrews 9: blood and once-for-all offering

Hebrews gathers the sacrificial logic of the Old Testament and applies it to Christ.

> “Indeed, under the law almost everything is purified with blood, and without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins.” (Hebrews 9:22, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> “He entered once for all into the holy places, not by means of the blood of goats and calves but by means of his own blood, thus securing an eternal redemption.” (Hebrews 9:12, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> “so Christ, having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time, not to deal with sin but to save those who are eagerly waiting for him.” (Hebrews 9:28, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The principle is stated without qualification: without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness. Christ’s own blood secures an eternal redemption. He is offered once to bear the sins of many. The repeated “once” and “once for all” contrast with the repeated animal sacrifices of the old covenant. The payment is final because the representative is final. `[E]`

## 14.8 The New Testament’s own atonement vocabulary

The New Testament does not leave the meaning of the cross to later systematic categories. It supplies its own terms:

- **Propitiation** (*hilastērion*) — Romans 3:25; the mercy-seat language of the Day of Atonement applied to Christ.
- **Redemption / ransom** (*apolytrosis*, *lytron*, *exagorazō*) — Romans 3:24; Galatians 3:13; Mark 10:45; Matthew 20:28; 1 Timothy 2:6. Release secured by a price.
- **Bearing sin / made to be sin** — Isaiah 53; 2 Corinthians 5:21; 1 Peter 2:24; Hebrews 9:28.
- **Reconciliation** (*katallagē*) — Romans 5:10–11; 2 Corinthians 5:18–20; Colossians 1:20. Enemies brought near by the death of the Son.
- **Lamb of God** — John 1:29. The one who takes away the sin of the world.

These are not later theological inventions. They are the categories the biblical authors themselves chose. The chapter treats them as such. `[E]`

## 14.9 Synthesis: the cross as the culmination of obedience

The texts above converge on a single picture. The cross is the point at which the obedient man accepts the death that justice required. It is not an isolated payment event that could have occurred independently of the life that preceded it. The lifelong obedience described in Chapter 12 reaches its climax when that same obedient man, in Gethsemane and on the cross, willingly takes the penalty established in Eden and confirmed throughout the Old Testament.

The active obedience and the death therefore form one claim, not two. The death is the final act of the obedience. Without the prior obedience the death would not be the death of the righteous representative; without the death the obedience would not have completed the payment that justice demanded.

This reading also prepares the next chapter. The Father’s vindication of the Son (Chapter 16) rests on the fact that the Son’s obedience was real and complete, including the acceptance of death. The resurrection is God’s public declaration that the payment has been accepted and the representative has been found faithful.
