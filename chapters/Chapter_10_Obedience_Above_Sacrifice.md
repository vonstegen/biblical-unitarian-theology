## 10.1 The question

The prophetic critique is not a rejection of sacrifice as an institution. It is a rejection of sacrifice disconnected from the obedience the covenant requires. The acceptance criterion is whether the chapter answers "what kind of obedience does God require?" and supplies the language for Christ's active obedience in Chapter 12.

The Levitical legislation is the sacred institution the prophets are not rejecting. The prophets reject sacrifice that is disconnected from the obedience the covenant is for. The prophetic critique is the kind of obedience the covenant requires — justice, kindness, humility, steadfast love, mercy, knowledge of God, a broken and contrite heart.

This chapter completes the pattern of disobedience Chapter 9 established at the covenant scale: the prophetic critique is the divine response to the pattern, and the pattern the prophets name is *sacrifice without obedience* (Isa 1:13; Amos 5:21; Mic 6:6–8).

## 10.2 The 1 Samuel 15:22–23 reading

> "Has the Lord as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the Lord? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to listen than the fat of rams. For rebellion is as the sin of divination, and presumption is as iniquity and idolatry. Because you have rejected the word of the Lord, he has also rejected you from being king." (1 Samuel 15:22–23, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

This is the foundational explicit textual witness to the obedience-above-sacrifice theme. To obey is better than sacrifice. Rebellion is as the sin of divination. The text establishes that obedience is the priority the covenant requires; sacrifice without obedience is rejected.

**Tagging:** `[E]` for the textual statement. `[I-high]` for the interpretation that the prophetic critique is the kind of obedience the covenant requires.

**Evidence sketch (`[I-high]`):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The verse explicitly states "to obey is better than sacrifice." (b) Rebellion and presumption are equated with divination and idolatry. (c) The rejection of Saul is the consequence of rejecting the word of the Lord.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The verse is in the context of Saul's specific disobedience. (b) The prophets do not reject sacrifice as an institution (the Levitical system is affirmed elsewhere).
- *Band justification:* The priority of obedience is explicit; the application to the covenant's required obedience is the natural reading across the prophetic corpus. `[I-high]` is honest.

## 10.3 The Hosea 6:6 reading

> "For I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings." (Hosea 6:6, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The *ḥesed* / *da'at* pairing carries the theme: God desires steadfast love and the knowledge of God rather than sacrifice. The chapter reads this as the priority of covenant relationship (steadfast love, knowledge of God) over disconnected ritual. Jesus quotes it in Matthew 9:13 and 12:7, applying the same priority to his own controversies.

**Tagging:** `[E]` for the textual statement. `[I-high]` for the interpretation that the prophetic critique supplies the language for the obedience the covenant requires.

**Evidence sketch (`[I-high]`):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The verse explicitly contrasts steadfast love and knowledge of God with sacrifice. (b) Jesus quotes it twice to rebuke those who prioritize ritual over mercy. (c) The same theme appears in Micah 6:8 and other prophets.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The verse is in the context of Israel's unfaithfulness; it does not say sacrifice is never acceptable when paired with obedience. (b) The Levitical system itself requires both sacrifice and obedience.
- *Band justification:* The priority of steadfast love and knowledge of God is explicit; the application to the covenant's required obedience is the natural reading across the prophetic corpus. `[I-high]` is honest.

## 10.4 The Jesus citation (Matthew 9:13 and Matthew 12:7)

> "Go and learn what this means, 'I desire mercy, and not sacrifice.' For I came not to call the righteous, but sinners." (Matthew 9:13, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

> "And if you had known what this means, 'I desire mercy, and not sacrifice,' you would not have condemned the guiltless." (Matthew 12:7, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

Jesus quotes Hosea 6:6 twice. The first is in the context of eating with sinners; the second is in the context of his disciples plucking grain on the Sabbath. In both cases the priority is mercy and the innocence of the guiltless over rigid ritual. The chapter reads this as the Jesus citation applying the prophetic critique to his own controversies: the obedience the covenant requires is mercy, not disconnected sacrifice.

**Tagging:** `[E]` for the quotations. `[I-mid]` for the interpretation that Jesus is re-applying the prophetic priority of mercy rather than merely invoking the verse.

**Evidence sketch (`[I-mid]`):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Both quotations are direct from Hosea 6:6. (b) The contexts (eating with sinners, Sabbath mercy) emphasize mercy over ritual. (c) The prophets' critique is applied to Jesus' own ministry.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The quotations are in specific controversy contexts; they do not form a systematic treatise on sacrifice. (b) Jesus participates in the temple system elsewhere. (c) Whether the citation re-applies or merely invokes the prophetic priority is a reading of intent.
- *Band justification:* The priority of mercy over disconnected ritual is explicit in the quotations and their contexts; the interpretive claim that Jesus is re-applying the critique is a defensible but not transparent reading. `[I-mid]` is honest.

## 10.5 The Isaiah 1:11–17 reading

> "What to me is the multitude of your sacrifices? says the Lord; I have had enough of burnt offerings of rams and the fat of well-fed beasts; I do not delight in the blood of bulls, or of lambs, or of goats. ... Bring no more vain offerings; incense is an abomination to me. New moon and Sabbath and the calling of convocations — I cannot endure iniquity and solemn assembly." (Isaiah 1:11, 13, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The multitude of sacrifices is rejected when accompanied by iniquity. The chapter reads this as the prophetic critique of sacrifice disconnected from obedience: the divine weariness is with the kind of sacrifice that coexists with injustice. The call is to "cease to do evil, learn to do good; seek justice, correct oppression; bring justice to the fatherless, plead the widow's cause" (Isa 1:16–17).

**Tagging:** `[E]` for the textual rejection of vain offerings. `[I-high]` for the interpretation that the prophetic critique is the kind of obedience the covenant requires (justice, care for the vulnerable).

**Evidence sketch (`[I-high]`):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The verse explicitly rejects the multitude of sacrifices when iniquity is present. (b) The call to justice and care for the vulnerable follows immediately.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The text does not say sacrifice is never acceptable when paired with obedience. (b) The Levitical system itself requires both.
- *Band justification:* The rejection of disconnected sacrifice is explicit; the call to justice as the required obedience is the natural reading of the passage. `[I-high]` is honest.

## 10.10 The chapter's argument in summary

The prophetic critique is not a rejection of sacrifice as an institution. It is a rejection of sacrifice disconnected from the obedience the covenant requires. The texts converge on a single picture: the obedience the covenant requires is justice, kindness, humility, steadfast love, mercy, knowledge of God, and a broken and contrite heart. This supplies the language for Christ's active obedience in Chapter 12 — the last Adam who does justice, loves kindness, walks humbly with God, and offers the obedience the prophets called for.

The pattern across the passages is consistent: sacrifice is named in the same breath as the disobedience, and the divine refusal is directed at the *combination*, not at the sacrifice. Saul spared the spoil to sacrifice (1 Sam 15:21); the new moon and Sabbath cannot be endured *with the iniquity* (Isa 1:13); the offerings *will not be accepted* (Amos 5:22); the *what shall I bring* question is met with *do justice* (Mic 6:6–8). The combination is what the prophets reject; the institution is preserved.

The chapter confines itself to the prophetic critique. The Levitical system is the sacred institution the prophets are not rejecting. The sufficiency of one man's obedience is deferred to Chapter 15.
