## 13.1 The question

Chapter 12 argued that Christ's obedience is lifelong — that the cross is the consummation, not the invention, of a willing doing of the Father's will that began in the incarnation. This chapter examines the moment at which that willingness is tested most severely: Gethsemane. The question is whether the willing obedience of Chapter 12 holds at the crisis point, or whether the cross is in fact a coerced suffering that breaks the willing obedience of the rest of the life.

The chapter also examines a related vocabulary question: the New Testament's own "two wills" language — the Father's will and the Son's will — as it appears in the Gethsemane texts and the parallel Father–Son sayings in the Fourth Gospel. The chapter's argument is that the two-wills language is drawn from the text, not imposed upon it. The Son's "not my will, but yours" is the textual anchor; the Johannine parallels show that this is the standing form of the Son's relation to the Father, not an anomaly introduced at the crisis.

The acceptance criterion for the chapter is therefore whether the willing obedience of Chapter 12 is shown by the Gethsemane texts to be unignorable, drawn only from the listed biblical texts and their parallel Father–Son passages.

## 13.2 Matthew 26:36–46 — the first full account

> "Then Jesus went with them to a place called Gethsemane, and he said to his disciples, 'Sit here, while I go over there and pray.' And taking with him Peter and the two sons of Zebedee, he began to be sorrowful and troubled. Then he said to them, 'My soul is very sorrowful, even to death; remain here, and watch with me.' And going a little farther he fell on his face and prayed, saying, 'My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will.' … Again, for the second time, he went away and prayed, 'My Father, if this cannot pass unless I drink it, your will be done.' … So, leaving them again, he went away and prayed for the third time, saying the same words again." (Matthew 26:36–44, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The Matthean account gives the full shape of the prayer:

- The place is named. Gethsemane is not an anonymous spot; the name and the Mount of Olives setting place the prayer in a tradition the disciples could locate.
- The inner circle is selected. Peter, James, and John are taken further in than the others. The pattern of three (the same three at the Transfiguration and elsewhere) signals that what is about to happen has the character of a revelation.
- The emotional register is explicit. "Sorrowful and troubled" (Greek *lupeō* and *adēmoneō* — grief and agitation), "very sorrowful, even to death" (*perilupos*, an unusual doubled grief). This is not the language of calm acceptance; it is the language of one facing what he does not want to face.
- The prayer is addressed as My Father. The address is intimate (no *Kyrie* here), but the content is petitionary. The Father is asked, with full respect, to consider an alternative.
- The cup is named. "Let this cup pass from me" — the cup is something the Son sees it as possible to ask the Father to remove. The petition presupposes the Father's freedom to act otherwise than the cross.
- The willing subordination is named. "Not as I will, but as you will" — the Son's will is real (it is named and set against the Father's will), and the Son wills otherwise than the cross. The submission of the Son's will to the Father's will is the act of obedience the chapter is tracking.
- The petition is repeated three times. The repetition is not for emphasis in the rhetorical sense; it is the shape of sustained willing. The Son does not simply submit once; he re-submits, in the same words, against the same pressure.

The chapter reads Matthew 26:36–46 as the fullest single witness to the willing obedience at its crisis. The Son is grieved; the Son has a will of his own that is set against the cup; the Son wills the Father's will instead. `[E]` for the textual evidence; `[I-high]` for the interpretation of "not as I will" as a real counter-will that is set aside in willing obedience.

**Evidence sketch (`[I-high]`):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Matthew 26:39 names the Son's will and sets it against the Father's — "not as I will, but as you will" — where *thelō* ("I will") is a real act of willing. (b) The petition "let this cup pass from me" (Matthew 26:39) presupposes a genuine desire not to drink the cup; a conventional phrase would not accompany a real petition for removal. (c) John 6:38 places the same counter-will language outside the crisis ("not to do my own will"), confirming that "not my will" names a real standing feature of the Son's relation, not a figure of speech.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) "Not as I will" can be read as a reverential idiom expressing submission rather than reporting a genuine contrary desire. (b) The Gethsemane prayer is prayer, not a doctrinal treatise; its grammar could be read as liturgical rather than psychological.
- *Band justification:* The real counter-will is the natural reading of a real petition plus the Johannine parallel; the idiomatic reading is possible but weaker, so `[I-high]` is honest.

## 13.3 Mark 14:32–42 — the parallel account with the Aramaic address

> "And they went to a place called Gethsemane. And he said to his disciples, 'Sit here while I pray.' And he took with him Peter and James and John, and began to be greatly distressed and troubled. And he said to them, 'My soul is very sorrowful, even to death. Remain here and watch.' And going a little farther, he fell on the ground and prayed that, if it were possible, the hour might pass from him. And he said, 'Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will.'" (Mark 14:32–36, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

Mark's account is shorter but adds two textual features that matter for the chapter:

- *Abba, Father.* The Aramaic *abba* (intimate father) is preserved alongside the Greek *pater*. Mark uses *abba* only here in his Gospel. The combination is unparalleled: the prayer is at once intimate and reverent, and the Aramaic is preserved because it is the prayer language of the Son. The early church preserves the Aramaic for the same reason (Romans 8:15; Galatians 4:6) — the cry of *abba* is the standing cry of the Son, repeated by the sons.
- *All things are possible for you.* Mark makes explicit what is implicit in Matthew: the petition presupposes the Father's omnipotence. The Son is not asking whether the Father can remove the cup; he is asking whether the Father will. The petition is will-petition, not power-petition.

Mark also preserves the warning to the disciples that opens the chapter's most pointed observation about the willing obedience:

> "Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak." (Mark 14:38, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The line is spoken to the disciples, but it states a structural truth that the chapter applies to Jesus himself in the inverse. In the disciples, the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak — there is a real willing, and a real weakness, and the willing does not carry. In Jesus, the willing carries to its goal; the flesh is weak in the sense that the cup is unwelcome, but the will is not overcome by the weakness. The Son's "not what I will, but what you will" is the successful form of the "spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak" pattern. The chapter reads the warning to the disciples as the chapter's most direct evidence that willing and weakness are real and separable in the human case; and that Jesus, in the same form, carries the willing through. `[E]`

## 13.4 Luke 22:39–46 — the unique additions

> "And he came out and went, as was his custom, to the Mount of Olives, and the disciples followed him. … And he withdrew from them about a stone's throw, and knelt down and prayed, saying, 'Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me. Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done.' And there appeared to him an angel from heaven, strengthening him. And being in agony he prayed more earnestly; and his sweat became like great drops of blood falling down to the ground. … And when he rose from prayer, he came to the disciples and found them sleeping for sorrow." (Luke 22:39–46, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

Luke's account preserves two unique details that the chapter treats as load-bearing:

- The angel. "An angel from heaven, strengthening him." The Father does not remove the cup, but he sends strengthening for the one who must drink it. The petition for removal is not granted; the petition for strengthening is. This is consistent with the chapter's reading of willing obedience: the Son accepts the cup, and the Father sustains him in the accepting. The strengthening is the Father's confirmation that the Son's submission is in fact the Father's will.
- The sweat like drops of blood. Luke records an extreme physical response that Matthew and Mark omit. The costliness of the willing submission is not hidden. The Son's obedience is not performed at the level of stoic detachment; it is performed through genuine human cost.

Luke also includes the detail that the disciples' sleep was "for sorrow" — they were not careless but grieved. The Son's distress is not unique to him; the disciples feel the pressure too. The Son, however, does what they cannot: he rises from prayer and confronts what is coming. The chapter reads the contrast as evidence that the Son's obedience succeeds precisely where the disciples' willing fails.

The textual note attached to Luke 22:43–44 — "Some manuscripts omit verses 43 and 44" — is genuine. The verses are absent from some of the earliest manuscripts and are present in others; the ESV includes them as part of the standard text. The chapter treats them as part of Luke's account without making the chapter's central argument depend on the textual question. Whether or not 22:43–44 is original to Luke, the angel/sweat details are consistent with the picture in Matthew and Mark; the chapter's argument does not turn on them.

## 13.5 John 18:11 — the Johannine parallel at the moment of arrest

> "So Jesus said to Peter, 'Put your sword into its sheath; shall I not drink the cup that the Father has given me?'" (John 18:11, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

John does not record Gethsemane. But at the moment of arrest — the immediate consequence of the prayer — the Fourth Gospel gives the same vocabulary in a single sentence: the cup. Three observations are load-bearing:

- Shall I not drink the cup. The question expects the answer yes. The drinking of the cup is the settled will of the Son, expressed not in Gethsemane's "not my will, but yours" but in the post-Gethsemane resolution: the cup will be drunk.
- The cup the Father has given me. The cup is the Father's giving. The petition "let this cup pass from me" was not granted; the cup is given, and the Son receives it. The Father's giving and the Son's drinking are now one act.
- Put your sword into its sheath. The disciples' violent resistance is forbidden. The willing acceptance of the cup is incompatible with any human attempt to prevent it. The Son's obedience is not the result of being overpowered; it is the result of having chosen.

John 18:11 is the chapter's strongest single sentence for the post-Gethsemane resolution. The petition was real; the petition was not granted as asked; the Son accepts what the Father has given; the acceptance is settled. `[E]`

## 13.6 Hebrews 5:7–8 — the epistle writer's reflection

> "In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard because of his reverence. Although he was a son, he learned obedience through what he suffered." (Hebrews 5:7–8, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

Hebrews does not narrate Gethsemane but reflects on it from within a Christian congregation a generation after the events. Three observations are load-bearing:

- In the days of his flesh. The Son's prayer is set in the incarnation. The flesh is real; the prayer is real; the cost is real.
- Loud cries and tears. The emotional register is preserved. Hebrews is not embarrassed by the costliness of the willing obedience; it is part of the picture.
- He was heard because of his reverence. The verb *eulabeia* is rendered "reverence" by the ESV; it carries the sense of pious caution, the careful holding of oneself in the face of danger. The hearing is not the granting of the petition for removal; it is the strengthening (Luke's angel), the sustained capacity to do the Father's will.
- He learned obedience through what he suffered. The developmental claim of Chapter 12 returns here in concentrated form. The Son learned (Greek *emathen*) — not acquired, but acquired through experience — obedience through the path of suffering. Gethsemane is the schoolroom in which that learning happens.

Hebrews' reflection is the chapter's strongest evidence that the early church received Gethsemane not as a deviation from the Son's obedience but as its completion. The Son's obedience reaches its goal in the prayer "not as I will, but as you will." `[E]`

## 13.7 The "two wills" question

The term "two wills" appears in later christological reflection (post-Chalcedonian theology) and was used to frame how the Son's human will and the Father's will can both be real without dividing the person. The chapter does not stake its argument on the later technical vocabulary; it stakes its argument on the textual categories the biblical authors themselves use.

The textual categories are:

- The Father's will. The Father has a will that is named, addressed, and petitioned. Matthew 26:39; Mark 14:36; Luke 22:42 — all three Synoptic accounts frame the prayer as addressed to the Father's will.
- The Son's will. The Son has a will that is named and set against the Father's will. "Not as I will" (Matthew 26:39), "not what I will" (Mark 14:36), "not my will" (Luke 22:42). The Son's will is real and is set aside in the same breath in which it is named.
- The Father's will, done. The Son wills what the Father wills. "Your will be done" (Matthew 26:42; Luke 22:42). The petition for removal is set aside; the petition for the Father's will to be done is the prayer's content.
- The Son's standing will. The willing-subordination is not a one-time accommodation. John 6:38 — "not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me" — places this as the Son's standing posture. Gethsemane is the crisis-instance of a relation that is true at every moment of the Son's ministry.

The chapter treats the "two wills" as the textual categories the biblical authors themselves supply. The categories are `[E]` (the text names both wills). The interpretation of the two-wills pattern as a standing relation of Father and Son, not an anomaly introduced by the crisis, is `[I-high]`.

**Evidence sketch (`[I-high]`):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) John 6:38 states the standing form outside the crisis — "not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me." (b) John 8:29 ("I always do the things that are pleasing to him") uses *pantote* ("always") to make the subordination uninterrupted. (c) John 5:30 ("I seek not my own will but the will of him who sent me") repeats the counter-will language as the Son's settled posture.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) No passage states "the Son's will and the Father's will are two wills standing in relation" in propositional form. (b) The Synoptics record "not my will" only at Gethsemane, so a reader of the Synoptics alone might take the counter-will as a crisis-specific statement.
- *Band justification:* The Johannine parallels make the standing relation the natural reading; the absence of a propositional statement and the Synoptic localization pull against `[E]`, so `[I-high]` is honest.

The chapter therefore does not stake its argument on the Chalcedonian or post-Chalcedonian formulation of two wills; it stakes the argument on the textual categories the Synoptic and Johannine writers themselves supply. The categories are textual; the later systematic synthesis is `[I-mid]` (a defensible but non-biblical label for the textual categories).

**Evidence sketch (`[I-mid]`):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The Synoptics name both the Father's will and the Son's will (Matthew 26:39; Mark 14:36; Luke 22:42), so the label "two wills" maps onto textual data. (b) The label "two wills" imports no foreign content; it is a name for the two named wills the text already distinguishes.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The term "two wills" is never used by any biblical author; it is later systematic vocabulary. (b) The post-Chalcedonian formulation carries metaphysical commitments (how two wills inhere in one person) that the biblical text does not address.
- *Band justification:* The label maps onto the text's two named wills but carries non-biblical baggage; it is a defensible synthesis rather than a textual assertion, so `[I-mid]` is honest.

## 13.8 The Johannine Father–Son cross-check

The Fourth Gospel supplies three standing statements of the Son's relation to the Father that confirm Gethsemane is the crisis-instance, not the exception:

> "I can do nothing on my own. As I hear, I judge, and my judgment is just, because I seek not my own will but the will of him who sent me." (John 5:30, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "For I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me." (John 6:38, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "And he who sent me is with me. He has not left me alone, for I always do the things that are pleasing to him." (John 8:29, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

These three verses are the chapter's strongest evidence that the willing subordination of Gethsemane is the standing form of the Son's relation to the Father. Three observations:

- Not my own will. The same counter-will language as Gethsemane appears here outside the crisis. The Son's "not my own will" is the baseline, not the exception.
- Always. John 8:29's *pantote* (always) is the same word that Chapter 12 used for the lifelong obedience. The Son's "not my own will" is not a moment-by-moment accommodation; it is the unbroken form of his life.
- *As I hear, I judge.* John 5:30 introduces a further dimension: the Son's hearing. The verb is plain *akouō* ("I hear"); it is the hearing word to which the preposition *hupo* ("under") is prefixed to form *hupakouō* ("I obey"), the verb behind the noun *hupakoē* in Romans 5:19 ("obedience"). John's "hearing" and Paul's "hearing-under" share the same root and name the same attentive submission, in different vocabulary. The Son hears and does what he hears. Gethsemane is the moment at which what the Son hears (the cup) is hardest to do, and at which he does it.

The chapter reads John 5:30; 6:38; 8:29 as the standing form of which Gethsemane is the crisis instance. The crisis reveals what the standing relation is. The Son's "not my will, but yours" at Gethsemane is not a deviation; it is the deepest expression of what "not my own will but the will of him who sent me" has always been. `[E]` for the textual data; `[I-high]` for the parallel-reading interpretation.

**Evidence sketch (`[I-high]`):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) John 6:38 and John 5:30 repeat Gethsemane's "not my own will" language outside the crisis, so the Synoptic prayer and the Johannine sayings name the same counter-will. (b) John 8:29's *pantote* ("always") marks the subordination as unbroken, not occasional. (c) John 18:11 ("shall I not drink the cup that the Father has given me?") shows the Johannine Son resolving toward the same cup the Synoptic Son accepts at Gethsemane.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The Fourth Gospel does not narrate Gethsemane; the parallel is a reader's cross-Gospel synthesis, not an explicit statement. (b) The Johannine sayings are formulated in John's own idiom and could be read as John's theology rather than as the same historical posture the Synoptics record.
- *Band justification:* The shared counter-will vocabulary plus John 18:11 make the parallel the natural reading; the absence of a Synoptic–Johannine equation in the text itself prevents `[E]`, so `[I-high]` is honest.

## 13.9 Synthesis: the willing obedience at its crisis

The texts above converge on a single picture. Gethsemane is the crisis at which the willing obedience of Chapter 12 is most severely tested, and at which it holds.

Four propositions follow:

1. The Son has a real will that is set against the cup. "Not as I will" / "not my will" / "let this cup pass from me" all presuppose a will that does not want the cross. The petition for removal is a real petition, not a formal gesture.
2. The Son's will is set aside in willing submission. The same words that name the counter-will name its setting-aside. "Not as I will, but as you will" is the form of the obedience: the real counter-will is held, but the Father's will is done instead.
3. The petition for removal is not granted; the petition for sustaining is granted. The Father sends the strengthening angel (Luke); the cup is not removed but is given to be drunk (John 18:11). The Father's answer is the sustaining, not the removal.
4. The willing subordination is the standing relation, not an exception. John 5:30; 6:38; 8:29 place the "not my own will" as the Son's baseline. Gethsemane is the deepest instance of the standing relation.

The chapter therefore concludes that the willingness of the obedience of Chapter 12 is unignorable. The cross is not a coerced suffering; it is the act of one who, having a will set against the cup, wills the Father's will instead. The obedience is willing, and the willingness holds at the crisis.

The chapter also prepares Chapter 14. The cup that the Son drinks in Gethsemane is the cup of the cross. The willing acceptance of the cup in Gethsemane is the same willing acceptance that takes him to Calvary. The cross is the consummation of the willing obedience.

The chapter's central argument does not depend on the originality of Luke 22:43–44 to Luke's Gospel. The strengthening angel and the sweat-like-blood detail appear only in Luke among the Synoptics. Even if these verses are a later addition to Luke, they do not introduce a new doctrinal claim — the Father sustains the Son in the accepting of the cup is consistent with the Matthean and Markan accounts. The chapter quotes the verses because the ESV includes them and because they are widely received as part of Luke's text, but it does not stake any claim on their removal if a reader follows a shorter text.
