## 0.1 The methodological landscape of biblical theology

Biblical theology, in the sense this manuscript assumes, is the discipline that reads the Bible's own categories in the Bible's own sequence. Its classic articulation is Geerhardus Vos's definition: biblical theology "exhibits the organic progress of supernatural revelation in its historic continuity and multiformity" — the discipline traces what God disclosed at each stage of redemptive history, in that stage's own terms, rather than pressing the whole canon through a later systematic grid. Systematic theology asks what the whole Bible teaches about a topic abstracted from history; biblical theology asks what the text says at each point in the history and how the stages build. The distinction is a division of labor, not a rivalry. This manuscript is biblical theology in that sense: it follows the redemption argument from Eden (Chapters 1–3) through the Fall (4–6), the human condition under the debt (7–10), the last Adam (11–15), and the vindication (16–17), and it lets each stage supply its own vocabulary before the next stage is read.

The twentieth-century Biblical Theology Movement (roughly 1945–1965) made the discipline prominent and then made it suspect. The movement insisted that Scripture's theological claims are carried by its history rather than by proposition lists, and that the church must recover the Bible's own thought-forms. Its critics — most influentially Brevard Childs in *Biblical Theology in Crisis* (1970) — charged that the movement read the text through modern categories as foreign as the old dogmatic ones, and that "theology from the text alone" had become a slogan without a method. The responses to that crisis split into two durable positions: theological interpretation of Scripture (the text read with the church and its rule of faith) and a chastened descriptive biblical theology (the text read with explicit methodological self-restraint, honest about what it can and cannot show). This manuscript belongs to the second position. It does not read with the church's rule of faith; it reads under a self-imposed source constraint with an explicit epistemic audit, and it invites the reader to check every step.

The movement's positive program was substantial: it produced the *Journal of Biblical Theology*, recovered the category of redemptive-historical unity for Old Testament study (G. Ernest Wright's *God Who Acts* is the standard exhibit), and taught a generation to ask what a text meant *then* before asking what it means *now*. Its failure, by its critics' account, was that it never settled what authorizes the history it traced — which is the methodological vacuum this manuscript fills by making its constraint explicit rather than assumed. The post-crisis landscape has three live positions, named here only to locate this project: Childs's canonical approach, which this manuscript overlaps with at one point — it reads the canon as a finished whole, Genesis through Revelation — while declining the rule-of-faith commitments that accompany Childs's later work; theological interpretation of Scripture, which reads the text with the church's creedal tradition and which this manuscript declines, since its argument is precisely that the contested readings must be settled by the text and not by the tradition that produced them; and a renewed descriptive biblical theology (Francis Watson's *Text and Truth* as one exhibit), which reads the text's own patterns and lets them generate the theology. The manuscript stands in the third position, and its distinctive additions to it are the source constraint (§0.2) and the epistemic audit (§0.4).

Three commitments follow, stated here once because every later chapter presupposes them:

1. **The text supplies its own categories.** The manuscript refuses vocabulary the text does not use, and where later systematic terms appear ("covenant of works," "federal headship," "active and passive obedience," "two natures") they are named only to identify what the text is being asked to support, never imported as exegetical premises (the discipline is applied at Chapter 3, Chapter 11, Chapter 12, Chapter 13, and Chapter 15).
2. **The argument is a historical sequence, not a topic survey.** The order of Parts I–V is the argument. A reader who reorders the chapters loses the load-bearing dependencies: Chapter 5 blocks Chapter 6; Chapter 11 is the prior foundation on which Chapter 15's one-for-many mechanism is built (the paired acceptance gates in `01_Architecture_and_Outline.md` encode this).
3. **Confidence is made explicit.** Every claim is tagged with its epistemic status relative to the text, so the reader can always distinguish what the text states from what the argument infers (§0.4).

## 0.2 The Bible-only constraint as a methodological commitment

The source constraint is stated in the Introduction §I.2: the argument draws its evidence only from the text of Scripture; no commentary, systematic theology, creed, church father, or modern scholar is cited as evidence for any theological claim. This section's task is to argue that the constraint is a rigorous methodological commitment — a discipline chosen for the argument's sake — and not an arbitrary restriction, a confession hiding inside a method, or a refusal to engage.

**The constraint's warrant.** The text of Scripture describes itself in terms that license this discipline. "All Scripture is breathed out by God and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16) — `[E]` for the text's self-description: the writing itself, not merely the events behind it, is said to be God-breathed and sufficient for the reader's instruction. If that claim is taken seriously, the most direct test of an argument about what Scripture teaches is whether the text, read carefully, actually says it. The method does not first prove Scripture's authority and then read it; it takes the text's own claim as the warrant for reading the text with maximum care and minimum imported apparatus. The Berean pattern makes the same point from the reader's side: the Bereans were more noble than the Thessalonians because "they received the word with all eagerness, examining the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so" (Acts 17:11) — `[E]` for the practice the text itself commends: receiving a claim and testing it against Scripture. This manuscript is written for that reader: every claim is presented so that a reader with a Bible and no other library can check it.

**The constraint as discipline.** The constraint earns its rigor in three ways:

1. **Auditability.** Because every piece of evidence is a biblical text (or a documented translation choice, §0.3), every claim can be checked by the reader against the same text. A claim supported by a named passage is reproducible; a claim supported by "the tradition has always taught" is not. The constraint converts the argument from assertion into verification.
2. **Boundedness.** The constraint blocks the most common failure mode of theological argument: importing the conclusion as a premise. A systematic term carries its system with it; "federal headship" already assumes a doctrine of representation before a single text is read. The manuscript's practice is to refuse the term until the text supplies the substance, and then to say exactly what the text supplies and what it does not. This is the discipline behind Chapter 11's correspondence principle and Chapter 15's one-for-many mechanism, each argued from Romans 5:12–21 and 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49 before any label is permitted.
3. **Honest limits.** The constraint costs something, and the manuscript states the cost instead of hiding it. Where the text is genuinely ambiguous, the manuscript presents the range of readings rather than resolving by authority (Introduction §I.7; Appendix B). Where a question is not answered in the text, the manuscript leaves it open and says so (Chapter 17 §17.2). A constrained argument is limited in what it can prove, and therefore trustworthy in what it claims.

**What the constraint is not.** Three negations bound the commitment. First, the constraint is not a claim that non-biblical sources are false or worthless; it is a claim about what counts as *evidence* in this argument. Second, it is not a refusal to engage the conversation: Appendix A engages it, and the body names competing positions wherever the text is being pressed to support them. Third, it is not a guarantee of interpretive purity; the reader's categories still enter, which is why the manuscript exposes its inference chain (§0.4) instead of pretending to be category-free. The constraint is a discipline, not a charm.

The discipline works, and the manuscript's own history shows it working. In an early draft, the argument quoted Hosea 6:6 as "I desire mercy and not sacrifice." The Hebrew of the verse is *ḥesed* — steadfast love, covenant loyalty — not *raḥamim*, mercy. The text itself corrected the argument, and the correction is on record in Chapter 1's verification log: the chapter now reads "I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice," and the distinction is load-bearing for the obedience-over-sacrifice spine of Chapters 1, 10, and 12. That is auditability in action: a mistake the reader could have caught with a Hebrew lexicon and a Bible was caught by the method before it reached the page. An argument that rests on the tradition cannot be corrected this way, because the tradition's renderings are not checkable against a single text.

**The precedent and the objection.** The principle is not new. The Reformation's *scriptura sui ipsius interpres* — Scripture is its own interpreter — is the methodological precedent: the text is read in light of the text, harder passages by clearer ones, and no external court decides what the text means. The Enlightenment's challenge to that principle was that "text alone" reading silently smuggles in the reader's own categories, and the challenge is fair: it is the exact failure the Biblical Theology Movement was charged with. This manuscript's answer is not to deny the risk but to make it visible: the translation set is disclosed with its editorial frames (§0.3), the inference chain is exposed at every step (§0.4), and the reader is shown precisely where the text stops and the argument continues. A disclosed frame can be audited; an undisclosed one cannot.

**How the text settles the text.** The constraint does not leave the manuscript without an instrument for ambiguous passages. The instrument is the parallel-passage discipline: a contested reading is tested against the clearer texts that speak to the same matter, and the clearer governs the darker. Two examples show the rule at work. The death-penalty declaration of Genesis 2:16–17 is read against its own sequel, where the sentence is enacted the same day — the curse, the rupture, the expulsion — while physical death is deferred to Genesis 5:5; the manuscript tags the result honestly: `[I-high]` for "the penalty took effect that day," with the idiomatic reading of "in the day" carried in the evidence-against list rather than hidden (Chapter 3). And Ezekiel 18:20 ("the son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father") is read as the limit on direct imputation, complementary to — not contradicting — the transmission language of Romans 5:12, because the canon is one text and its parts are read as mutually interpreting (Chapters 6 and 9). The parallel-passage discipline is the Reformation principle operationalized: Scripture interprets Scripture.

**The dissertation form: Appendix A.** In its dissertation form the manuscript adds one bounded exception. The argument of the main body — this chapter through Chapter 17 — is conducted from the text alone. Appendix A (present in the dissertation form; absent from the base manuscript) engages the secondary literature as the conversation the text's argument has generated: the federal and anti-federal traditions on representation, the two-wills debates, the active-and-passive-obedience distinction, the state-of-the-dead literature, and modern scholarship on Paul. The boundary is absolute and is stated in Appendix A itself: secondary literature is engaged there to locate the contribution and to test it against its rivals, never as evidence for the manuscript's own theological claims. The main body's argument stands or falls on the text alone; Appendix A shows where the argument lands in the conversation. The two belong together — a contribution no one has met cannot be tested by anyone — but they do not trade on each other: no claim in Chapters 1–17 rests on anything Appendix A cites.

## 0.3 Translation and the text

No modern reader has unmediated access to the biblical text: Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek are read through translations, and every translation is an interpretation at the points where the languages differ from English. The method therefore cannot pretend to a neutral text. It must disclose its translations, their editorial frames, and its rules for using them. This section states the policy; `06_Translation_Bias_Documentation.md` documents each translation's biases and limitations with sources.

**The set.** The manuscript cites from two primary translations — the ESV (essentially literal, formal equivalence) as the default citation text, and the NASB (1995; the most literal widely used English translation) as the word-order and syntax cross-check — and consults six reference translations, each with a specific function in the argument:

- **YLT and LSV** surface Greek and Hebrew structure through strictly literal renderings in the Textus Receptus / Majority Text tradition.
- **Rotherham's Emphasized Bible** is a maximal-literalism rendering in the same critical-text family as the primaries, with an explicit anti-creedal translation philosophy.
- **The REV** is a modern unitarian-affiliated translation, consulted as a reference precisely so that the unitarian editorial frame is on the table rather than smuggled in.
- **The CLV/CLNT** is a concordant, hyper-literal rendering used as a parsing aid for Greek word-usage tracing.
- **The AICNT** is an AI-rendered critical text with an embedded apparatus of seven-thousand-plus variants, consulted for variant transparency on the hard texts.

The KJV/NKJV family is deliberately excluded: it preserves editorial decisions the modern critical editions reject as not original (the Comma Johanneum in 1 John 5:7 is the standard case), and its "only begotten" renderings smooth a genuinely contested lexical choice. The exclusion is for source-material fidelity, not theological opposition.

**The rules.** Four citation rules govern every chapter:

1. **Divergence is shown, not hidden.** Where the two primaries differ on a passage significant to the argument, both renderings are presented.
2. **Reference translations are cited for a specific structural or lexical argument** — never as an authority to settle a dispute, only as evidence about the underlying text (the concordant rendering of *kathistēmi* in Romans 5:19; the LSV's preservation of *monogenēs* ambiguities).
3. **Contested terms are cited in the original language with transliteration**, so the reader sees what the debate is about rather than inheriting a contested English choice.
4. **No appeal to consensus.** "What the church has always taught" and "what scholars agree" are not arguments in this manuscript. Where readings differ, the range is presented and the text decides.

The rules are load-bearing in practice. The manuscript's treatment of *monogenēs* (John 1:14, 18; 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9) presents the divergence squarely: the ESV renders "only" where the NASB (1995) renders "only begotten," and the reference translations divide along the same line. The manuscript does not pick a winner; it states the lexical range, notes which structural argument each rendering serves, and leaves the reader the range (Chapter 1; Appendix B). Romans 5:19's *katestathēsan* is handled the same way: the primary rendering "were made sinners" (ESV, NASB) is set beside the literal "were constituted sinners" that the concordant tradition offers, because whether the many were *made* or *positioned* as sinners is exactly the question the evidence sketch must keep visible (Chapters 6 and 15). Divergence is evidence, not embarrassment.

The discipline behind the rules is the Introduction's formula: the project reads the text *through* the translation frames rather than *in deference to* them. A set that includes an anti-creedal rendering, a unitarian rendering, and an explicitly excluded Trinitarian-leaning rendering (with reasons) makes the editorial frames part of the visible evidence. That is the translation-level application of the same auditability principle that governs §0.2.

## 0.4 The tagging system as an epistemic standard

The manuscript's epistemic standard is the three-way tag with a confidence band, defined in the Introduction §I.5. `[E]` marks a claim the text states explicitly and unambiguously in both primary translations. `[I]` marks a strong implication from context, and `[H]` marks a hypothesis that depends on synthesis across passages. The `[I]` and `[H]` claims carry a band — low, mid, or high — set by the enumerable textual evidence for and against, and every such claim carries an evidence sketch: a for-list, an against-list, and a one-sentence band justification, in which every entry is a biblical text or a documented translation choice. The reader can audit any band against their own Bible; nothing in a sketch relies on outside material.

The system is offered here as a methodological contribution, not merely an internal convention. Biblical theology typically asserts its conclusions at a uniform confidence; readers must infer from the surrounding prose how much weight the author puts on a given claim. This manuscript makes confidence an explicit, auditable axis. Two dimensions are held apart on purpose: the *kind* of claim (stated, implied, hypothesized) is one axis, and the *strength of the evidence* (the band) is another. A claim can be strongly evidenced but only implied (`[I-high]`) or weakly evidenced but hypothesized (`[H-low]`); conflating the two axes produces either dogmatism (inference presented as text) or hedging (text presented as guesswork). The tagging system forbids both.

Three rules govern the system's use and prevent its abuse:

1. **Cautious downgrade.** Where a claim could plausibly sit at more than one level, the more cautious level is chosen. An `[E]` claim that could be `[I]` is downgraded; a claim is never promoted without further exegetical work.
2. **Bands are qualitative, not numerical.** The band is a confidence judgment grounded in an enumerable evidence sketch; it deliberately refuses numerical probabilities, because a number would reflect the framer's training-corpus priors more than the text, and the reader could not reproduce it. What the reader *can* reproduce is the sketch: the passages, the translation choices, and the comparative judgment (the argument of Introduction §I.5.5).
3. **The tag is a commitment, not a hedge.** A `[H-mid]` claim is a claim the manuscript makes as a hypothesis worth defending; the tag reports what kind of work has been done, and the manuscript stands behind the assignment.

The rule is not hypothetical. Chapter 15 originally tagged its explanatory-sufficiency claim — that the one-act / many-recipients structure *explains* why one man's obedience suffices — as `[E]`. The structure itself is explicit in Romans 5:18–19 and 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, but the sufficiency explanation is a step beyond the structure, and the acceptance pass reclassified it to `[I-high]`: the text states the one-act / many-recipients shape; the argument infers that this shape is the mechanism of sufficiency. Both claims remain in the chapter; only the confidence report changed. That is what the system is for. The argument is not weakened by the downgrade — a committee that discovers an over-tag before the author does is how defenses are lost.

The system is also, in effect, the dissertation's pre-written defense structure. A defense committee asks of every load-bearing claim, "What supports this?" The tags are the standing answer: `[E]` names the text; `[I]` and `[H]` name the passages for and against and the justification for the band. The argument's strengths and weaknesses are on the page rather than hidden in footnotes — which is exactly the transparency a defense rewards.

The system also differs from the field's standing convention for uncertainty. The usual practice is to assert in the main text and hedge in the footnote; the claim reads confidently, and only the careful reader discovers where the author's confidence thins. The tagging system inverts the order: the claim carries its confidence on its face, and the sketch does the work the footnote would do. The inversion matters for honesty at scale — a reader who must check fifty footnotes to find the weak links will not check; a reader who can see the tag distribution at a glance knows where the argument's weight rests and where to push back.

Two risks are acknowledged. First, the sketches can become formulaic; if every claim wears the same four-bullet uniform, the manuscript reads mechanically. The mitigation (the second-pass deepening in the project roadmap) is to make each sketch its own argument, with the band as its conclusion rather than its starting point. Second, the system can be mistaken for a claim that confidence is the same thing as truth; it is not. The tag reports the state of the evidence as the manuscript has worked it. A claim can be honestly `[I-high]` and still be wrong — but it cannot be *silently* wrong, and that is what the system exists to prevent.

## 0.5 Why this approach serves the debt-debtor thesis

The thesis of this manuscript is a correspondence claim: according to Scripture, humanity incurred a debt to God through Adam's disobedience; God established death as the judicial consequence of sin; and redemption came through a corresponding human representative — the last Adam — whose willing, lifelong obedience culminated in sacrificial death, vindicated by the Father's raising him. The spine verse is Romans 5:19: "For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous" — the parallel structure of which is `[E]` in the text, and the manuscript's interpretation of which is argued with full evidence sketches in Chapters 11 and 15.

An argument of this shape lives or dies on two things: fidelity to the text's own categories, and honest measurement of the inferential distance between what the text states and what the argument concludes. The Bible-only constraint supplies the first; the tagging system measures the second. That is not a coincidence of method and content; the method was chosen for this argument.

**The correspondence argument needs text-fidelity.** The Adam–Christ parallel carries its force only if it is the text's parallel and not a later system's. The comparison is man : man — "For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:21–22, `[E]`; the correspondence principle is argued in Chapter 11). Every step away from the text's own terms weakens the parallel: if the redeemer must be read through a metaphysical category the text does not use, the correspondence is no longer the text's claim but the system's. The constraint forces the argument to carry itself on the text's categories alone — and, because the argument is exegetical rather than dogmatic, that is the only ground on which it can win against the readings it contests.

Walk the chain once, to see the method and the thesis fused. Step one is `[E]`: the text states the parallel structure — "by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners... by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19); "in Adam all die... in Christ shall all be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:22). Step two is `[I-high]`: the same-order correspondence principle — the redeemer answers the debt as a man, because the text's parallel is man : man (Chapter 11). Step three is `[I-high]`: the one-for-many mechanism — the structure the text states is the structure by which one man's obedience reaches the many (Chapter 15). Step four is where the manuscript stops and says so: the precise nature of the "in Adam" / "in Christ" relation, and the scope of the "all" in Romans 5:18, are `[H-mid]` — the text does not settle them, and the manuscript declines to (Chapter 15; Chapter 17 §17.2). Each step is tagged, each band is justified by enumerated texts, and the reader can see exactly how far the text carries the argument and where the argument continues on inference alone.

**The competing readings are historically embedded.** The readings this manuscript contests — satisfaction theologies, penal-substitution systems, two-wills christology, the federal headship tradition — are carried by their own traditions and their own authorities. A text-only argument cannot defeat them by counter-authority, and it should not try. It must out-read them: show, from the same text, that the text's own structure is the one the manuscript presents. The constraint converts a contest between traditions into a contest of readings, which is the only contest a biblical-theology dissertation can win on the merits.

**The unitarian-exegetical framing is the constraint's beneficiary, not its victim.** The manuscript reads the contested christological texts (John 1:1; Philippians 2:6; Colossians 1:15; Hebrews 1) exegetically and draws no conclusion the text does not carry (Introduction §I.3; Appendix B). An argument that declines post-biblical metaphysical scaffolding is weakest exactly where it leans on that scaffolding and strongest where it stands on the text alone. If the thesis holds without any scaffold, it holds; the constraint is the condition under which the argument is strongest, not a handicap imposed on it.

**What the method can and cannot do.** The method can show what the text states and what the text implies, with the evidence for each step enumerated and auditable. It cannot prove the thesis beyond dispute, settle every ambiguity, or claim the tradition as an ally — and it does not pretend to. It deliberately leaves open what the text leaves open (Chapter 17 §17.2). A dissertation committee can ask more of an argument than any method can deliver, but what this method delivers is the thing a committee can actually test: a chain of claims from the text alone, each link tagged, each band justified, each quotation verified.
