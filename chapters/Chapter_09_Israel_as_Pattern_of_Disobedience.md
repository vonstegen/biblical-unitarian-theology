# Chapter 9 — Israel as Pattern of Disobedience

> **Status:** Revised draft (2026-08-06). Per `01_Architecture_and_Outline.md` (v1.3) and the Introduction §I. The third chapter of Part III. ## 9.1 The question

Chapter 8 established that the biblical text presents disobedience as a *recurring pattern* that can intensify across the canonical story. The remaining question is whether Israel's history at the covenant scale is the *visible operation* of that pattern — and whether the individual-accountability language of Ezekiel 18 contradicts the *constitutional transmission* claim of Chapter 6.

The chapter's answer is that Israel's history is the dramatic, covenant-scale witness to the pattern of Chapter 8. The covenant recipient remains bent in the pattern direction even after the covenant is established; the covenant shows the pattern at scale rather than eliminating it. The chapter makes no soteriological claim about whether the covenant is *effective*; the chapter argues only that the *pattern of the recipient* is the *narrative* the Israelite text reports.

The acceptance criterion (A9) is narrative: the chapter sets up Part IV by showing *why a faithful human redeemer is needed* at the narrative level. The individual-accountability question is engaged fairly in §9.10 — the *complement* framework treats the Ezekiel 18 forensic limit as the *complement* of the constitutional transmission (per Chapter 6 §6.8), not its contradiction.

## 9.2 The golden calf: the foundational failure (Exodus 32)

The golden calf is the foundational explicit textual witness to the Israel failure. While Moses is on the mountain, the people gather against Aaron and demand gods who will go before them:

> "When the people saw that Moses delayed to come down from the mountain, the people gathered themselves together to Aaron and said to him, 'Up, make us gods who shall go before us. As for this Moses, the man who brought us up out of the land of Egypt, we do not know what has become of him.'" (Exodus 32:1, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

Aaron fashions a golden calf and the people proclaim:

> "These are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt!" (Exodus 32:4, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

Aaron builds an altar, proclaims a feast to the Lord, and the people offer sacrifices and "sat down to eat and drink and rose up to play" (Exodus 32:5–6, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`.

The episode shows the pattern's character: the people do not *wait* for the mediator, they *demand* a visible mediator, and they *conflate* the visible mediator with the Lord. The play language (Hebrew *ləṣəḥoq*, "to play / make sport / revel") is the worship-becomes-revel language; the *feast to the Lord* is the conflation. The chapter reads the episode as the *foundational* Israel failure — the pattern at the moment the covenant is barely established.

**Tagging:** `[E]` for the textual event. `[I-high]` for the *pattern* reading (see §9.11 evidence sketch).

## 9.3 The Moses reading (Deuteronomy 9:6–7)

Moses's retrospective reading of the wilderness generation frames the pattern explicitly:

> "Know therefore that the Lord your God is not giving you this good land to possess because of your righteousness, for you are a stubborn people. Remember and do not forget how you provoked the Lord your God to wrath in the wilderness. From the day you came out of the land of Egypt until you came to this place, you have been rebellious against the Lord." (Deuteronomy 9:6–7, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

Three textual features matter. First, the *stubborn people* language (Hebrew *ʿam-qəšeh-ʿōrep̄*, "stiff-necked people") is the *people* descriptor — the *kind of being* the nation is, is the resistant kind. Second, the *not because of your righteousness* is the explicit denial of a merit-based grounding — the *kind of being* the covenant is for, is the stubborn kind, not the already-righteous kind. Third, the *rebellious* language is *from the day* language — the rebellion is co-temporal with the covenant's establishment, not subsequent to it.

The chapter reads the passage as Moses's *insider* reading of the pattern: the people who received the covenant are the same kind of being the pattern describes. `[E]` for the textual content; `[I-high]` for the *pattern* reading (see §9.11).

## 9.4 The murmuring (Numbers 14:2–4)

The Numbers passage is the *second* Israel failure of the wilderness period. While Ch 8 §8.5 quotes the same passage in full, the chapter notes a citation correction here: the murmuring speech begins at Numbers 14:2 (the verse-1 clause "Then all the congregation raised a loud cry, and the people wept that night" is the narrative frame, not the murmuring itself).

> "And all the people of Israel grumbled against Moses and Aaron. The whole congregation said to them, 'Would that we had died in the land of Egypt! Or would that we had died in this wilderness! Why is the Lord bringing us into this land, to fall by the sword? Our wives and our little ones will become a prey. Would it not be better for us to go back to Egypt?' And they said to one another, 'Let us choose a leader and go back to Egypt.'" (Numbers 14:2–4, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The *return to Egypt* language is the *known* language — the people prefer the familiar bondage to the promised land. The *choose a leader* language is the *replacement* language — the people propose a leader who fits the *return* direction. The chapter reads the passage as the *complaint* complement to the golden calf: if the calf is the *visible-mediator* demand, the murmuring is the *preferred-leader* demand. Both are the *kind of being* the pattern describes. `[E]` for the textual event; `[I-high]` for the *pattern* reading (see §9.11).

## 9.5 The cyclical failure (Judges 2:10–14)

The Judges passage is the *cyclical* Israel failure — the pattern in narrative form:

> "And all that generation also were gathered to their fathers. And there arose another generation after them who did not know the Lord or the work that he had done for Israel. And the people of Israel did what was evil in the sight of the Lord and served the Baals. And they abandoned the Lord, the God of their fathers, who had brought them out of the land of Egypt. They went after other gods, from among the gods of the peoples who were around them, and bowed down to them. And they provoked the Lord to anger. They abandoned the Lord and served the Baals and the Ashtaroth. So the anger of the Lord was kindled against Israel, and he gave them over to plunderers, who plundered them. And he sold them into the hand of their surrounding enemies, so that they could no longer withstand their enemies." (Judges 2:10–14, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The *generation that did not know* language is the *transmission* language — the forgetting is the *kind of being* the pattern produces across generations. The *did evil / served the Baals / abandoned the Lord* triad is the *idolatry* language — the visible-mediator demand of §9.2 in cycle. The *gave them over* language is the *divine handing-over* language — the consequence of the covenant cycle.

The chapter reads the passage as the *cyclical* Israel failure: the pattern is not a single event but a recurring shape. `[E]` for the textual narrative; `[I-mid]` for the *handing-over=consequence* reading (the verb is grammatically permissive; the consequence-reading is the natural reading but the textual text does not label it as such; see §9.7 evidence sketch for the synthetic sketch).

## 9.6 The exilic failure (2 Kings 17:7–12)

The 2 Kings passage is the *exilic* Israel failure — the pattern culminating in the loss of the land:

> "And this occurred because the people of Israel had sinned against the Lord their God, who had brought them up out of the land of Egypt from under the hand of Pharaoh king of Egypt, and had feared other gods and walked in the customs of the nations whom the Lord drove out before the people of Israel, and in the customs that the kings of Israel had practiced. And the people of Israel did secretly against the Lord their God things that were not right. They built for themselves high places in all their towns, from watchtower to fortified city. They set up for themselves pillars and Asherim on every high hill and under every green tree, and there they made offerings on all the high places, as the nations did whom the Lord carried away before them. And they did wicked things, provoking the Lord to anger, and they served idols, of which the Lord had said to them, 'You shall not do this.'" (2 Kings 17:7–12, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The *sinned against the Lord their God* language is the *covenant* language — the people who have been *brought up out of Egypt* are the same people who now sin. The *feared other gods* and *walked in the customs* language is the *national* language — the pattern is not isolated but collective. The *built high places* and *served idols* language is the *cultic* language — the visible-mediator demand in the *cult* form. The *did wicked things, provoking the Lord to anger* language is the *consequence* language — the divine response follows the pattern.

The chapter reads the passage as the *exilic* Israel failure: the pattern that began at the golden calf reaches its covenant-loss conclusion. `[E]` for the textual narrative; `[I-high]` for the *pattern* reading (see §9.11).

## 9.7 The Asaph reading (Psalm 78:1, 7–11)

The Psalm 78 passage is the *Asaph* reading of the pattern:

> "Give ear, O my people, to my teaching; incline your ears to the words of my mouth! ... so that they should set their hope in God and not forget the works of God, but keep his commandments; and that they should not be like their fathers, a stubborn and rebellious generation, a generation whose heart was not steadfast, whose spirit was not faithful to God. The Ephraimites, armed with the bow, turned back on the day of battle. They did not keep God's covenant, but refused to walk according to his law. They forgot his works and the wonders that he had shown them." (Psalm 78:1, 7–11, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The *stubborn and rebellious generation* language (Hebrew *sōrēr ū-mōreh*, "rebellious and disobedient") is the *generation* descriptor — the *kind of being* the generation is, is the resistant kind. The *heart was not steadfast* language is the *heart* language — the *kind of being* the heart is, is the un-steadfast kind. The *did not keep God's covenant* language is the *covenant-breaking* language — the *kind of being* the covenant-breaking is, completes the triad: the people are *generation* (resistant), *heart* (un-steadfast), and *covenant* (breaking).

The chapter reads the passage as the *Asaph* reading of the pattern: the OT internal reflection on the foundational Israel failure. `[E]` for the textual content; `[I-high]` for the *pattern* reading (see §9.11).

## 9.8 The Hallel reading (Psalm 106)

The Psalm 106 passage is the *Hallel* reading of the pattern, in three movements:

> "Both we and our fathers have sinned; we have committed iniquity; we have done wickedness. Our fathers, when they were in Egypt, did not consider your wondrous works; they did not remember the abundance of your steadfast love, but rebelled by the sea, at the Red Sea." (Psalm 106:6–7, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The *both we and our fathers* language is the *generational* language — the *kind of being* the generation is, is the same kind of being the foregoing generation is. The *rebelled by the sea* language is the *Red Sea* language — the rebellion begins at the *Exodus* itself, not only at the golden calf.

> "They made a calf in Horeb and worshiped a metal image. They exchanged the glory of God for the image of an ox that eats grass. They forgot God, their Savior, who had done great things in Egypt." (Psalm 106:19–21, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The *made a calf in Horeb* and *exchanged the glory* language is the *Horeb* rendering of the golden calf (Psalm 106 calls it *Horeb*; Exodus 32 calls it *Horeb/Sinai*); the *exchanged the glory* language is the *exchange* language — the people trade the *glory* for the *image*.

> "Thus they became unclean by their acts, and played the whore in their deeds. Then the anger of the Lord was kindled against his people, and he abhorred his heritage; he gave them into the hand of the nations, so that those who hated them ruled over them. Their enemies oppressed them, and they were brought into subjection under their power. Many times he delivered them, but they were rebellious in their purposes and were brought low through their iniquity." (Psalm 106:39–43, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The *became unclean* and *played the whore* language is the *cultic* language — the idolatry is also the *uncleanness*, the *playing-the-whore* is the cultic-defection metaphor. The *gave them into the hand of the nations* language is the *handing-over* language — the same divine response as in Judges 2:14.

The chapter reads the passage as the *Hallel* reading: the recapitulation of the pattern with explicit *handing-over* language, ready for the *post-exilic* audience to confess. `[E]` for the textual content; `[I-high]` for the *pattern* reading (see §9.11).

## 9.9 The Stephen reading (Acts 7:39–43)

The Acts 7 passage is the *Stephen* reading of the pattern — the *post-canonical* NT interpretation:

> "Our fathers refused to obey him, but thrust him aside, and in their hearts they turned to Egypt, saying to Aaron, 'Make for us gods who will go before us. As for this Moses who led us out from the land of Egypt, we do not know what has become of him.' And they made a calf in those days, and offered a sacrifice to the idol and were rejoicing in the works of their hands. But God turned away and gave them over to worship the host of heaven, as it is written in the book of the prophets: 'Did you bring to me slain beasts and sacrifices, during the forty years in the wilderness, O house of Israel? You took up the tent of Moloch and the star of your god Rephan, the images that you made to worship; and I will send you into exile beyond Babylon.'" (Acts 7:39–43, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The *refused to obey him* and *thrust him aside* language is the *mediator-rejection* language — the *kind of being* the people are, rejects the mediator. The *turned to Egypt* language is the *return* language — the *in their hearts* is the *desire* direction, the *Egypt* is the *known* (cf. §9.4). The *gave them over to worship the host of heaven* language is the *handing-over* language — same as Judges 2:14 and Psalm 106:41. The *tent of Moloch* and *star of your god Rephan* language is the *exilic* language — the pattern reaches its *exile beyond Babylon* conclusion (the *post-exilic* Stephen reads the *prophetic* indictment forward into the Babylonian exile).

The chapter reads the passage as the *Stephen* reading: the *post-canonical* interpretation that explicitly cites the *prophetic* indictment (Amos 5:25–27) and applies it to the foundational Israel failure. `[E]` for the textual content; `[I-high]` for the *pattern* reading (see §9.11).

## 9.10 The Ezekiel 18:20 limit

The chapter engages Ezekiel 18:20 as the *individual-accountability* limit on the constitutional transmission. The verse says:

> "The soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son. The righteousness of the righteous shall be upon himself, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon himself." (Ezekiel 18:20, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The chapter reads this verse as the *complement* of the constitutional transmission, following Chapter 6 §6.8. The two operate on different axes: the *constitutional transmission* concerns the *ontic* state of the descendants (the *kind of being* the descendants are in Adam); the *Ezekiel 18* limit concerns the *forensic* assignment of punishment (the son is not forensically judged for the father's sin). The axes are distinct and non-contradictory: the son is ontically in Adam's constitution (and inherits the *bent* direction), and the son is forensically accountable only for his own sin.

**Tagging:** `[E]` for the verse (the *son shall not suffer for the father* is explicit). `[I-mid]` for the *complement* interpretation (the axes distinction is the chapter's systematic reading; see Chapter 6 §6.8 evidence sketch for the canonical formulation).

**Evidence sketch — `[I-mid]` "The Ezekiel 18:20 limit is consistent with the constitutional transmission"**

- *Evidence for:* (a) The Ezekiel verb is *suffer* (Hebrew נשׂא, "to carry / bear punishment"), the standard juridical-punishment verb across the OT (Lev 5:1, 17; Num 5:31; Ezek 14:10). The text rules out the *son* bearing the juridical punishment that belongs to the *father*; it does not speak to the *kind of being* the son inherits. (b) The Pauline *in Adam* claim concerns death's universality (Rom 5:12; 1 Cor 15:22) and the kind of being the descendants are; Ezekiel 18:20 concerns the assignment of juridical punishment. The two operate on different axes. (c) The proverb Ezekiel corrects ("the fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge," Ezek 18:2) treats the parental sin as *directly punishing* the child; the correction denies the direct-punishment reading without denying the ontic-bent reading.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) Ezekiel's correction speaks only to *punishment*, not to inherited moral condition; whether the descendants also *inherit* a bent moral condition is a question Ezekiel 18 does not directly address. (b) The Pauline and Ezekiel texts can be read as compatible (one about kind of being, one about juridical punishment) only if the *constitutional / forensic* distinction is honored — a distinction the text itself does not draw. (c) An *integrated* reading in which the descendants both inherit a bent condition *and* bear the juridical guilt of Adam (the *real original sin* position) is consistent with both texts; the text does not adjudicate between this reading and the *ontic-only* reading.
- *Band justification:* The Ezekiel verb (*suffer*) anchors the distinction in the text itself (juridical punishment, not inherited condition); the ontic-reading is one of two compatible positions. The chapter commits to the ontic reading (per Chapter 6 §6.2) and treats the Ezekiel compatibility as `[I-mid]` because the *axes distinction* is not explicit in either text.

## 9.11 The chapter's argument in summary

The chapter's *primary* claim is that Israel's history is the *covenant-scale* witness to the recurring pattern of disobedience that Chapter 8 establishes. The textual evidence is the *Exodus 32* (the golden calf), the *Deuteronomy 9:6–7* (the Moses reading), the *Numbers 14:2–4* (the murmuring), the *Judges 2:10–14* (the cyclical failure), the *2 Kings 17:7–12* (the exilic failure), the *Psalm 78:1, 7–11* (the Asaph reading), the *Psalm 106* (the Hallel reading), and the *Acts 7:39–43* (the Stephen reading).

The chapter's *secondary* claim is that the *pattern* is the *kind of being* the *covenant recipient* is. The chapter commits to the *pattern* reading at `[I-high]` and explicitly notes that the *reading* is the chapter's, not the texts' direct statement.

The chapter's *fair engagement* with Ezekiel 18:20 is that the *individual accountability* is the *complement* of the constitutional transmission (`[I-mid]`). The two are not contradictory; the constitutional transmission is the *kind of being*; the individual accountability is the *forensic* judgment.

The chapter's *forward* reference is *preparation*, not *restoration*: the pattern prepares the question of Chapter 10 (what obedience does the covenant require?) and Chapters 11–14 (what kind of human redeemer can answer the pattern?). The chapter does not claim that the *Last Adam* is the *restoration* of the covenant recipient — that is a *soteriological* claim for Chapters 11–14 to argue from Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15. The chapter's forward reference is a *pointer*, not a *claim*.

**Evidence sketch — `[I-high]` "Israel's history is the *visible operation* of the recurring pattern of disobedience across the covenant scale"**

- *Evidence for:* (a) Exodus 32:1, 4, 5–6 — the foundational Israel failure at the moment the covenant is established; the people reject the mediator and demand a visible one. (b) Deuteronomy 9:6–7 — Moses's *insider* reading: the *stubborn* people are the same kind of being the covenant is for. (c) Numbers 14:2–4 — the second-generation wilderness failure: the *return to Egypt* is the *known* preference. (d) Judges 2:10–14 — the *cyclical* form: the pattern recurs across generations. (e) 2 Kings 17:7–12 — the *exilic* form: the pattern reaches covenant-loss conclusion. (f) Psalm 78:1, 7–11 — the *Asaph* internal reflection: the *stubborn and rebellious generation* is the chapter's *pattern* language. (g) Psalm 106 — the *Hallel* reading: the recapitulation of the pattern with explicit *handing-over* language. (h) Acts 7:39–43 — the *Stephen* reading: the *post-canonical* interpretation that explicitly cites the *prophetic* indictment and applies it to the foundational failure.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The texts describe *events*; the *kind of being* the recipients are, is the chapter's *interpretation* of the events, not a direct statement. (b) The *gave them over* / *gave them into the hand* language in Judges 2:14, Psalm 106:41, and Acts 7:42 is grammatically permissive; the *consequence* reading is the natural reading but the texts do not label it as such. (c) The *chapter* could be reading the pattern in selectively; the texts could be read as discrete events rather than as a pattern.
- *Band justification:* The *events* are explicit across the eight witnesses; the *pattern* is the natural reading of the events taken together. The interpretation is the *chapter's* reading, not a textual statement; the *events* support the reading but do not require it. The chapter rates the *pattern* reading at `[I-high]`, with the explicit acknowledgment that the reading is the *chapter's* synthesis.

**Translation notes:**

1. **Deuteronomy 9:6** "stubborn people" (Hebrew *ʿam-qəšeh-ʿōrep̄*) — the *stubborn* is the *neck* language; the *kind of being* the neck is, is the *resistant* kind. The chapter reads the *stubborn* as the *kind of being* the people are, in the *neck* = *resistance* metaphor.

2. **Psalm 78:8** "stubborn and rebellious generation" (Hebrew *sōrēr ū-mōreh*) — the *stubborn* and *rebellious* are the *generation* language; the *kind of being* the generation is, is the *resistant and rebellious* kind. The chapter reads the *stubborn* and *rebellious* as the *kind of being* the generation is, in the *generation* = *rebellion* descriptor.

3. **Acts 7:42** "God turned away and gave them over to worship the host of heaven" — the *divine* *handing over* is the *consequence* language. The chapter reads the *handing over* as the *divine consequence* of the covenant disobedience — the *kind of being* the *covenant* *recipient* is *handed over* to (the *bent* direction). The *handing-over* reading is `[I-mid]` (the verb is grammatically permissive; the consequence-reading is the natural reading but the text does not label it as such).

4. **Ezekiel 18:20** — the *individual* accountability is the *complement* of the *constitutional* transmission. The chapter treats the *individual* accountability as the *limit* on the *constitutional* transmission — the *son* is *not* forensically accountable for the *father's* sin. The *constitutional* transmission is the *kind of being* the *descendants* are; the *individual* accountability is the *forensic* judgment that each individual *also* faces. The *axes distinction* (ontic vs. forensic) is the chapter's systematic reading; see Chapter 6 §6.8 evidence sketch for the canonical formulation.
