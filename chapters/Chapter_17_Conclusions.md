## 17.1 The 10 research questions mapped to their chapter of resolution

The working thesis from `00_Master_Thesis.md` is that humanity incurred a debt to God through Adam's disobedience. God established death as the penalty for sin. Redemption requires a perfectly obedient human representative whose willing obedience culminated in sacrificial death. Christ's obedient life and death are inseparable aspects of redemption. The 10 research questions are mapped to their chapter of resolution below. All claims are drawn from the biblical text itself.

1. **Who is God according to Scripture, and how does His character define justice?** Chapter 1. God is just, faithful, and immutable (Deuteronomy 32:4; Numbers 23:19; Malachi 3:6; James 1:17). Justice is the formal constraint that makes the debt-debtor framework coherent — corresponding justice, not arbitrary punishment (`[E]` for the textual character; `[I-high]` for the formal constraint).

2. **What was humanity created for in relation to God?** Chapter 2. Humanity was created in God's image for covenantal obedience and dominion (Genesis 1:26–28; 2:15). The image is the structural anchor of the vocation (`[E]` for the textual mandate; `[I-high]` for the covenantal reading).

3. **What command, warning, and relationship defined Adam’s vocation?** Chapter 3. The command was not to eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil; the warning was death on the day of disobedience; the relationship was the unashamed presence in the garden (Genesis 2:16–17; 3:8–10). Death was set as the judicial consequence (`[E]` for the penalty declaration).

4. **What exactly did Adam lose?** Chapter 5. The losses are relational rupture with the divine presence, fear of the divine presence, the curse on the ground, the toil of the ground, the mortality of the body, the pain in childbearing, the rupture in the husband-wife relation, the expulsion from the garden, the blocked access to the tree of life, the creation's futility and groaning, and the hope of the redemption of the body. Each is tagged with evidence sketches; the chapter refrains from over-claiming the mechanism of each loss (`[E]` for the textual losses; `[I-high]` or `[I-mid]` for the interpretation of each as a specific kind of loss).

5. **What passed from Adam to humanity?** Chapter 6. Death passed to all (`[E]` — Romans 5:12; 1 Corinthians 15:22). The mechanism is ontic transmission: Adam's act produced the condition his descendants inherit (`[I-high]`). Forensic imputation of guilt is left open at `[I-mid]`. Ezekiel 18:20 is the limit on direct imputation and is engaged fairly as complementary to the ontic condition.

6. **What role do innocence, children, and moral accountability play?** Chapter 7. Childhood is a real developmental moral category distinct from adult accountability. The text treats the child as a kind of person who is not held accountable in the adult sense. The age of accountability is `[H-mid]` — a plausible systematic inference but not a textual assertion. Innocence is meaningful without committing to a specific age.

7. **Does Scripture describe disobedience becoming habitual or hardened?** Chapter 8. The selected texts depict disobedience as repeatable across individuals and communities and capable of intensifying in social effect and judgment (`[I-mid]`). The pattern is narrative, not a complete theory of human nature. The pattern prepares the question of the obedient redeemer.

8. **How does Israel's history of disobedience prepare for the Messiah?** Chapter 9. Israel's history is the covenant-scale witness to the recurring pattern of disobedience that Chapter 8 establishes. The individual-accountability language of Ezekiel 18 is the complement of the constitutional transmission (Chapter 6), not its contradiction. The pattern prepares the need for a faithful human redeemer at the narrative level.

9. **Does the Old Testament teach that obedience is greater than sacrifice?** Chapter 10. The prophetic critique is the rejection of sacrifice disconnected from the obedience the covenant requires. The obedience the covenant requires is justice, kindness, humility, steadfast love, mercy, knowledge of God, and a broken and contrite heart. This supplies the language for Christ's active obedience in Chapter 12.

10. **Why is one righteous man's obedience sufficient for many?** Chapter 15. The one-man / many structure of Romans 5:12–21 and 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49 establishes federal headship as the scriptural mechanism by which Christ's obedience suffices for the many. The parallel is structural, not biological. Faith is the instrument by which one enters the "in Christ" relation, not the ground of the sufficiency itself (`[I-high]`).

## 17.2 What remains open

The project is Bible-only. Some questions remain open because the text itself does not resolve them with propositional clarity. The open questions are tagged `[H-mid]` or `[I-mid]` in their chapters and are left as hypotheses worth defending rather than as settled claims.

- The precise mechanism of the ontic transmission (imputation, inherited corruption, or both) is `[I-mid]` (Chapter 6).
- The specific age of accountability is `[H-mid]` (Chapter 7).
- The exact nature of the "in Adam" / "in Christ" relation (sphere, organic union, or legal inclusion) is `[H-mid]` (Chapter 15).
- The scope of the "all" in Romans 5:18 (coextensive with the "all" of condemnation or limited to believers) is `[I-high]` (Chapter 15).
- The relationship between the Father's will and the Son's will in Gethsemane is the textual categories the biblical authors supply; the later systematic formulation of "two wills" is `[I-mid]` (Chapter 13).
- The lexical choice for *monogenēs* ("only" or "only begotten") is left open with the range of readings from the translation set (Chapter 1 and Appendix B).

The project stands behind the `[H-mid]` and `[I-high]` claims as hypotheses worth defending from the text. The open questions are not hedges; they are the boundary of what the text itself asserts.

## 17.3 Summary of the debt-debtor thesis

According to Scripture, humanity incurred a debt to God through Adam's disobedience. God established death as the judicial consequence of sin and does not rewrite His own justice. Redemption required a perfectly obedient human representative — the last Adam — whose willing lifelong obedience culminated in sacrificial death. The Old Testament prepares this reading by portraying Israel's repeated covenant disobedience and by teaching that obedience is greater than sacrifice. The New Testament presents Christ as the corresponding human representative who renders the obedience Adam failed to render and willingly accepts the death that divine justice required. The cross is the culmination of that obedience, not a separate transaction. The Father's vindication of the Son in the resurrection is the public declaration that the payment is accepted and the obedience is complete. The new humanity is the restoration of what was lost — the image renewed, the relational rupture healed, the constitutional condition reversed in the last Adam.

The thesis is positive: it shows what Scripture itself says. The argument is exegetical, not dogmatic. The conclusions are what the text says, not what must be true. The tagging system makes the boundary between what the text states and what the argument infers auditable. The project stands behind the `[E]` claims as explicit in the text, the `[I-high]` claims as strong implications from context and parallels, and the `[H-mid]` claims as hypotheses worth defending from the text. What remains open is left open because the text itself does not resolve it with propositional clarity.

The debt-debtor thesis of redemption is the biblical reading of the redemption narrative.
