## A.0 How to read this appendix

The dissertation form of this manuscript adds exactly one departure from the base manuscript's discipline: this appendix. It exists for two reasons that the main body does not serve.

The first is conversational. A reader who brings to the manuscript a knowledge of federal theology, Chalcedonian Christology, the active-and-passive-obedience distinction, modern Pauline scholarship, or the unitarian-biblical-theology tradition wants to know what the manuscript does with those traditions and where it diverges. The main body names every one of those positions where it diverges; this appendix gathers them so a reader with that knowledge has a single place to see what the manuscript does and does not take from each tradition. Without such a gathering, the manuscript's divergences read as disputes it has not acknowledged, which is a weakness in a dissertation defense.

The second is methodological. The argument of the main body is conducted from the text alone. This appendix does not relax that constraint — it engages the conversation the text's argument has generated, without letting any tradition's authority decide what the text says. The appendix's ruling phrase, which it inherits from Chapter 0 §0.2, is: secondary literature is engaged to locate the contribution and to test it against its rivals, never as evidence. When this appendix quotes a tradition, it is quoting that tradition's *voice* in order to identify what the text is being asked to support, exactly as Chapter 3 names the "covenant of works" as contested vocabulary for the declared-penalty reading it is making from Genesis 2:16–17 alone. The tradition's wording is a witness to the conversation; it does not author the conclusion.

Several things are deliberately absent. There is no attempt at a comprehensive history of redemption doctrine; the eight sections cover the major positions the manuscript's argument touches, not the field of Christian doctrine in general. There is no defense-of-position polemic — each section names the tradition, the agreement-and-disagreement map, and the boundary, and then stops. There is no running bibliography in the body of the appendix; the standard edition of each work cited is named in the bibliography at the end. The eight sections follow the numbering of the PhD roadmap's outline (A.A.1–A.A.8); they are not ordered by any claim about the relative importance of the traditions but by the order in which the manuscript's argument engages them: the covenant framework first, then the Christological implications, then the eschatological frame, then the modern scholarly conversation that surrounds it.

## A.A.1 Federal theology and the covenant of works

The Reformed orthodox tradition made a central structural distinction between the covenant of works (Latin *foedus operum*) given to Adam in the garden and the covenant of grace (*foedus gratiae*) given after the Fall. The distinction is the genus of what is later called federal theology: that God's way with humanity is structured by covenant, of which there are (depending on the federal theologian counted) two or three administrations.

The classical articulation of this tradition in English-speaking scholarship remains Hermann Witsius, *The Economy of the Covenants between God and Man* — originally *De oeconomia foederum Dei cum hominibus*, Leeuwarden, 1677. Witsius (1636–1708) was a Dutch Reformed theologian who studied at Groningen, Leiden, and Utrecht, taught at Franeker and Utrecht, and was appointed to Leiden in 1698 as Spanheim's successor; his major work attempted to mediate the late-seventeenth-century Voetian-Cocceian controversy between the Reformed scholasticism of Gisbertus Voetius and the more historically-sensitive federal reading of Johannes Cocceius (Johann Koch), whose *Summa Doctrinae de Foedere* (1648) had reorganized covenant theology around the progressive character of covenant administration. Witsius aims to reconcile "the reigning orthodoxy" with covenant theology: he reads Genesis 2:15–17 as a real covenant of works, with Adam as the federal head of the human race, and reads Genesis 3 and the rest of redemptive history as the unfolding of the covenant of grace, of which Christ is the second and last head.

The English-speaking reception fixed the structure in the *Westminster Confession of Faith* (1647), chapter VII ("Of God's Covenant with Man"). The relevant wording is:

> The first covenant made with man was a covenant of works, wherein life was promised to Adam, and in him to his posterity, upon condition of perfect and personal obedience. (*Westminster Confession*, ch. VII §2)

The Confession here makes three specific moves: (a) it calls the Edenic arrangement a covenant; (b) it ties the promised life to "perfect and personal obedience" rather than to the nature of the creature; and (c) it extends the promise "in him to his posterity" by federal headship rather than by individual obedience. The Confession's federal scheme runs §3–§5 into the covenant of grace, and §6 completes the picture with Christ as the "second Adam" and the "surety" of the new covenant.

**Where the manuscript's argument engages.** Chapter 3 of this manuscript, on the covenant of Eden, explicitly acknowledges the "covenant of works" term as Reformed-orthodox vocabulary; the chapter §3.1 notes: "the term *covenant* is not used of Eden in the text itself. The covenant reading is `[H-mid]`; the chapter's argument does not depend on this resolution" — meaning the manuscript declines to lean on the term, but it does not think the term is wrong. It argues the *substance* of what the Westminster formula articulates — that Eden was a commanded arrangement with declared penalty and that obedience-or-not was the human's response — from the text alone, by the command (Genesis 2:16), the doubled infinitive *mōth tāmūth* (Genesis 2:17) indicating a judicial certainty, the declared consequences (Genesis 3:17–19), and the relational rupture that opens the rest of the chapter. The chapter's `[I-high]` evidence sketch in §3.2 ("The death declared is judicial, not merely natural") is the manuscript's argument for the *judicial* content of the Westminster formula without the *covenantal* term. Where the federal tradition says *covenant of works*, the manuscript says *declared penalty*; the operational content is identical. Chapter 15, on the one-for-many mechanism, inherits the same structure and refuses the term for the same reason.

**The boundary the appendix maintains.** Whether naming the Edenic arrangement a *covenant of works* helps or harms biblical-theological clarity is contested. The Westminster Confession's vocabulary is precise, the Reformed tradition has used it for four centuries without scandal, and Witsius's *Economy* is the most thorough attempt to make the term carry its weight. The manuscript, holding to text-only vocabulary in the main body, declines to make a recommendation either way. The appendix's role is to name the tradition; the tradition's role is to be known to the reader; the chapter's argument is independent of either choice.

## A.A.2 Federal headship tradition

Federal headship is the structured application of the covenant-of-works vocabulary to the Adam-Christ parallel. Where the Westminster Confession (§A.A.1) names Adam as the federal head of the human race under the covenant of works, federal headship extends the same structure to Christ as the federal head of the new humanity under the covenant of grace.

The federal headship doctrine was already present in Witsius's *Economy* (book III), and it is the form federal theology most directly brings to Romans 5:12–21. In the twentieth century the Reformed-orthodox articulation is most rigorously pressed by Charles Hodge in his *Systematic Theology* (1872–1873; abridged as A.A. Hodge, *Outlines of Theology*, 1879) and by John Murray in his *The Imputation of Adam's Sin* (the 1959 Westminster lectures, published 1980, now in *Collected Writings*, vol. 2). Murray (1898–1975) was a Scottish-born Reformed theologian who studied at Princeton under Gresham Machen and Geerhardus Vos and taught at Westminster Theological Seminary from 1930 to 1966; his Westminster lectures on the imputation of Adam's sin are the most sustained treatment of the federal headship doctrine in twentieth-century English-speaking Reformed theology. His argument is that Adam's sin is *imputed* to his posterity not because the descendants participate ontically in Adam's act (they did not) but because Adam acts as the *federal* or forensic representative, by divine appointment; the descendant is "in Adam" by representation, not by participation.

The Westminster Confession's wording here is brief: "The sinfulness of that estate [the Fall] consisted in the guilt of Adam's first sin, the want of original righteousness, and the corruption of the whole nature" (*Westminster Confession*, ch. VI §3). The doctrine of federal imputation, with its separation of forensic guilt from ontological corruption, is implied in the chapter VI wording and is developed at length by Charles Hodge in his *Systematic Theology* and in the subsequent Reformed scholastic tradition. The Westminster Larger Catechism carries the matter forward without the runtime vocabulary.

**Where the manuscript's argument engages.** Chapter 11 of this manuscript, "Why must the Redeemer be human?", is the engagement point. The chapter's `[I-high]` correspondence principle — that the redeemer answers the debt as a man because the text's parallel is man : man (Romans 5:18–19; 1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45–49) — is in substance a federal headship claim. The chapter, however, refuses the federal-imputation vocabulary explicitly: Chapter 6 holds "forensic imputation of guilt ... at `[I-mid]`" while "the mechanism is ontic transmission at `[I-high]`." That is, the manuscript refuses to ground the federal headship reading in the forensic-imputation schema that Murray offers as the riddle's solution, while holding to the substantive claim that Adam is representative and Christ is a corresponding representative. The motivation is exegetical: Chapters 5 and 6 distinguish what was *lost* in the Fall (image, access, ease, life) from what *passed* (death, on the ontic transmission `[I-high]`), and the manuscript's reading of Ezekiel 18:20 ("the son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father") limits the imputation reading in just the way the Socinian tradition had earlier pressed. The outline in `01_Architecture_and_Outline.md` ch. 6 R6.2 makes the methodological choice explicit: "Decide and document your position: imputation of sin vs. inherited corruption vs. both vs. something else — this is a load-bearing methodological choice, drawn from Scripture alone."

**The boundary the appendix maintains.** Murray's defense of forensic imputation is comprehensive; his Westminster lectures engage every major Reformed orthodox witness and treat the exegetical basis carefully. The manuscript's dissent is methodological: the term and the schema come to Murray through a Reformed scholastic apparatus that the manuscript has not drawn into the argument. What the chapter engages is the *claim* that the representational structure works from Adam to humanity and from Christ to the new humanity; the chapter does not engage the *forensic-imputation schema* by which Murray explains the claims. The defense of the chapter's position rests on Romans 5:12, 1 Corinthians 15:22, and the Ezekiel 18:20 limit; the absence of "*imputed*" vocabulary in those texts guides the chapter's caution.

## A.A.3 Anti-federal tradition

The Reformed tradition's federal schema was held against itself from two directions: the dispensational critique on the conservative evangelical side, and the Socinian tradition from the antitrinitarian side. The two share the federal schema's discomfort but differ on its replacement.

Charles C. Ryrie's *Dispensationalism* (1965; later revised) is the modern compact statement of the dispensational critique. Ryrie opposes a single covenant of grace subsuming the dispensations, and the broader dispensational tradition (the Scofield Reference Bible, 1909, and its revisions) rejects the covenant of works as the unifying structure of redemptive history. The dispensational emphasis is on the distinction *between* dispensations rather than the unity *across* them; the covenant of works fails as the unifying category, in this reading, because it cannot fit the Abrahamic, Mosaic, Davidic, and New covenants as one continuous administration without smoothing over what the dispensational reading sees as real and significant discontinuity (e.g., the distinction between Israel and the church).

The Socinian tradition runs in a different direction. Fausto Socinus (Faustus Sozzini, 1539–1604), with his uncle Lelio Sozzini the major precursor, founded the tradition in Poland and Transylvania and gave it the systematic form it kept for three centuries. Socinus wrote *De Jesu Christo servatore* (1578) on the *work* of Christ — a treatise the Wikipedia entry on Sozzini notes says nothing of the *person* of Christ (the bearer of the work matters because of the office, not the metaphysical nature) — and the doctrinal consensus emerged in the *Racovian Catechism* (first published 1605 in Polish, *Catechesis Ecclesiarum Polonicarum*; the Latin edition Amsterdam 1609; an English translation followed in 1652). The Socinian position on Adam rejects the imputation of Adam's sin to his posterity on grounds of justice — the unborn cannot be punished for a sin they did not commit — and reads Romans 5:12 (*eph' hō pantes ēmarton*) as "for that all sinned" rather than "because all sinned in Adam," giving the verse an in-their-own-sins reading rather than an in-Adam-acted reading.

A similar reading from the modern context is the Christadelphian tradition (founded in the 1840s by John Thomas, systematized by Robert Roberts, *Christendom Astray*, 1852, and continuing through the present). The Christadelphians adopted the Socinian-derived position on the person of Christ (one will, one nature, no Trinity), the mortalist position on the soul (the dead are unconscious until resurrection), and the strict-sense position on Adam's sin (no forensic imputation; the condition of death passes naturally, not legally, from Adam to his descendants). The Christadelphian community is the closest modern English-language parallel to the manuscript's Biblicist-Socinian stance on Adam and the person of Christ.

**Where the manuscript's argument engages.** Chapter 6, "What passed to humanity?", and Chapter 5, "What was lost?", engage this tradition on the imputation question. The manuscript agrees with the Socinian and Christadelphian tradition on the *limit* on forensic imputation — Ezekiel 18:20 forbids the strong-imputation reading — and disagrees on whether the rest of the imputed-guilt vocabulary can be carried. The manuscript's resolution is in the ontic-transmission `[I-high]`: Adam's sin produced a condition his descendants inherit. This position does not use "imputation of guilt" in Murray's sense and does not use the Socinian "death is natural" position; it makes a third claim, grounded in Romans 5:12 ("death spread to all men because all sinned") with the ontic-condition model discussed in Chapter 5 §5.3 and Chapter 6 §§6.5–6.9. The Chapter 5 evidence sketch for "the image is marred, not lost" — the image of God constitutionally remains after the Fall (Genesis 9:6; James 3:9; 1 Corinthians 11:7; Colossians 3:10; Ephesians 4:24), and the contrast with the popular Reformed-orthodox position that the image was *lost* — is also a point of agreement with the Socinian and Christadelphian traditions, which have long held that the image is constitutional to the human and is renewed rather than recovered.

**The boundary the appendix maintains.** The Socinian rejection of Trinitarian Christology is inherited by the manuscript but explicitly not engaged here (Ch 0 §0.5; Appendix B); the shared rejection of forensic imputation is engaged because it is at exactly the point where the manuscript's own reading finds support from the tradition against the Reformed-orthodox position. The dispensational critique of the covenant-of-works schema is not adopted by the manuscript because the manuscript does not depend on the schema in the first place — it argues the declared-penalty reading from the text alone and declines the schema that the dispensational reading rejects.

## A.A.4 Two-wills christology

The christological frame this manuscript engages is the Chalcedonian definition (the "Definition of Faith" of the Council of Chalcedon, 451 AD) and its later development into the two-wills christology. Chalcedon affirms two natures, divine and human, in one hypostasis (Greek *enōsis kath' hypostasin*), "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation" (*asygchytōs, atreptōs, adiairētōs, achōristōs*). The late-sixth and seventh centuries then added the dyothelite formulation (two wills, divine and human) against monothelitism, the doctrine that Christ had only one will. The Second Council of Constantinople (553, which condemned the "Three Chapters" but also addressed Monothelitism peripherally) and the Third Council of Constantinople (680–681, Constantinople III) are the councils at which the two-wills formula acquired ecumenical status, a formulation confirmed by the Latin Catholic tradition at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) and affirmed by the Reformed orthodox tradition through the Westminster Confession.

The Reformed scholastic development, particularly through Francis Turretin's *Institutes of Elenctic Theology* (1688) and John Owen's *Communion with God* (1657), took the Chalcedonian formula into the discussion of the atonement and the Gethsemane narratives. Owen's line — that Christ's human nature, the *instrumentum unionis*, sustains a real human will that is voluntarily subordinated to the divine will — is the standard Reformed scholastic treatment of Gethsemane. The Lutheran tradition follows a similar but distinct path: the Formula of Concord (1577) defends the *communicatio idiomatum* (the interchange of properties between the natures) and rejects the Reformed scholastic position that Christ's human nature is a separate instrument.

**Where the manuscript's argument engages.** Chapter 13, "Gethsemane and the Two Wills," directly addresses the question. The chapter's position is that the Synoptic and Johannine writers themselves supply the categories by which the question is settled textually: the "not as I will, but as you will" prayer (Matt 26:39; Mark 14:36; Luke 22:42); the "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me" cry; the strengthening angel (Luke 22:43); the sweating as drops of blood; the Johannine "the cup" (John 18:11); and the Hebrews 5:7–9 "in the days of his flesh ... offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard for his godly fear." The willingness is shown by the *real counter-will* (the petition for removal that is not granted), the *setting-aside* (the petition surrendered), and the *price* (the cross). The chapter stakes its argument on these textual categories and refuses the Chalcedonian and post-Chalcedonian metaphysical categories as load-bearing for the claim.

The chapter's `[I-mid]` tag on the question "two wills or one" is the appendix's principal engagement point. The manuscript says: the chapters refuse the metaphysical layering the tradition offers, while accepting the textual phenomenon the tradition was responding to. The Reformers' two-wills formulation (and the dyothelite council tradition that preceded it) was a real attempt to protect the text's depiction of Gethsemane; the manuscript's refusal of the terminology is a refusal of the categories the tradition imported, not the textual phenomenon. Appendix B treats the same refusal for the high-Christology passages (John 1:1; Philippians 2:6; Colossians 1:15; Hebrews 1) with the same shape: the textual question is engaged, the metaphysical formulation is not.

**The boundary the appendix maintains.** The Chalcedonian formula has been the doctrine of the Eastern Orthodox, Roman Catholic, and most of the Reformed and Lutheran traditions for sixteen centuries. The manuscript's position is not that the formulation is false; the manuscript declines to depend on it for the exegetical work. The threshold the manuscript holds is whether the chapter's argument is valid on its own textual grounds; if it is, the Chalcedonian tradition's verdict on the matter is whatever it is in the meta-question. The appendix's role is to identify this as the conversation; the chapter's work is from the text alone.

## A.A.5 Active and passive obedience of Christ

The Reformed orthodox tradition distinguishes the *active obedience* of Christ — his lifelong obedience to the Father, the *obedientia activa* — and his *passive obedience* — his suffering and death, the *obedientia passiva* — as two distinct meritorious grounds of the believer's justification. The active obedience is sometimes said to be what earns the believer's life (the reward of perfect obedience); the passive obedience is what pays the penalty of sin (the suffering unto death).

The twentieth-century articulation of this distinction in English-speaking Reformed theology is John Murray's *Redemption Accomplished and Applied* (1955), in particular chapters 9–11. Murray's argument is that the active obedience of Christ is the basis on which the law's demands of the believer are met (Christ kept the law perfectly, and that obedience is the ground of the believer's acquittal before the law), while the passive obedience (his death) is the ground on which the curse of the law is removed (Christ bore the penalty the believer deserves). Murray does this in conversation with the Westminster Confession, which the Confession's chapter 11 ("Of Justification") implies without the active/passive vocabulary.

The theological stakes are large. The active/passive distinction has been criticized as importing a scholastic metaphysical layering that the New Testament does not perform, with the implication that the believer is "saved" twice — once by Christ's life credited, once by his death credited — when the New Testament presents Christ's work as a unified obedience culminating in death. Donald Bloesch (*Jesus Christ: Lord and Savior*, 1998) and others press the point from the evangelical side; the Reformed school of Bavinck, Berkhof, and Murray counters it from within.

**Where the manuscript's argument engages.** Chapter 12 of this manuscript, "The Perfect Obedience of Christ," makes the engagement explicit. The chapter identifies "later Reformed theology" as the source of the active/passive vocabulary (Ch 12 §12.10, in the same bounded-exception style that Chapter 0 §0.2 charters), and proceeds to read the New Testament evidence (John 8:29; John 8:46; 1 Peter 2:22–23; Hebrews 4:15; Hebrews 5:8–9; Philippians 2:8) as presenting a *unified obedience*, lifelong and culminating in the cross, with the categories of willing-doing and suffering-acceptance drawn from the text rather than from the scholastic apparatus.

The chapter's primary tag on the active/passive question is `[I-high]` for "Christ's obedience is lifelong and willing as a unified pattern," and the textual evidence is set against an alternative reading the chapter acknowledges. The chapter does not deny that the scholastic distinction is a way of organizing the same unity; the chapter declines to depend on the organizing vocabulary when the same unity is read from the text's own terms. Chapter 14, "The Cross as Payment," extends the unification: the cross is the *culmination* of the obedience, not a separate transaction alongside it. Romans 3:21–26 (propitiation by his blood), 2 Corinthians 5:21 (made sin for us), Galatians 3:13 (redeemed from the curse), Hebrews 9:11–14, 22 (without shedding of blood there is no forgiveness), and Isaiah 53 (the servant who bears the iniquities of the many) are read in Chapter 14 as presenting the death as the inheritor of the obedience's meaning, with the resurrection as the Father's vindication (Chapter 16).

**The boundary the appendix maintains.** Murray's *Redemption Accomplished and Applied* is one of the great Reformed works of the twentieth century. The manuscript does not contest it; the manuscript contests whether Murray's categories are the necessary categories for the text's claim. The same refusal applies: the appendix names the tradition, the engagement point is in the chapters, the boundaries hold, the manuscript's argument rests on the text.

## A.A.6 State-of-the-dead debate

The question of the state of the dead is the question of what happens to the soul (or the person) between death and resurrection. The Western mainstream tradition from the second-generation church fathers onward has held that the soul is naturally immortal (Greek *athanasia psychēs*; cf. Tertullian, *De anima*, c. 200 AD, where the doctrine is most firmly crystallized in Latin Christianity), and that the intermediate state is one of conscious happiness or conscious misery, with the imminence of the final judgment at the Second Coming being the alternative temporal frame but not a change in the *kind* of intermediate-state experience. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) finalized the teaching in its decree on the resurrection of the body, and the implication of natural immortality runs through the catechisms of the Reformed and Lutheran traditions as well.

The conditional-immortality tradition is the rejection of natural immortality. In English-speaking theology the modern argument is most often identified with John Wenham (*The Enigma of Evil*, 1972; later articles and lectures) and Edward Fudge (*The Fire That Consumes*, 1982; the definitive English-language treatment of conditional immortality in dialogue with the conditionalist exegetical tradition). The position: the *immortality* of the soul is a gift of Christ, not a natural property; the wicked are destroyed (Greek *apōleia*; the *olethron* of 2 Thessalonians 1:9, *olethros aiōnios*); the *eternal punishment* of Matthew 25:46 is the destroying kind of eternal, not the endless-conscious-torment kind. The conditionalist reading finds support in the Old Testament's silence on immortal-souls (the *Sheol* of the OT is a grave, not a conscious place), in the New Testament's restraint on the doctrine, and in the *aion* range of meanings (age-long, at the verb *apollumi*, rather than torture-without-end).

The Seventh-day Adventist tradition (SDA) is the major modern systematic body that has adopted conditional-immortality as a teaching position; it enters the denomination's doctrinal statements systemically in the late nineteenth century. The historical derivation runs through the Millerite Adventist movement of the 1840s, which had roots in the conditionalist tradition through George Storrs, Sylvester Graham, and Charles Beecher; the SDA systematization is from the founding generation onward. The contemporary position is summarized in the "Death and Resurrection" statement of *Seventh-day Adventists Believe...* (1988 ed.): the dead sleep until resurrection, the wicked are destroyed, immortality is the gift of Christ.

The Christadelphian tradition (cf. A.A.3) is the major British modern tradition that has held conditional-immortality since its founding. The mortalist position is a key commitment: the dead sleep; *immortality* is granted at resurrection; the perishable puts on the imperishable (1 Corinthians 15:54).

**Where the manuscript's argument engages.** Chapter 17 §17.2 lists the state-of-the-dead question among "What remains open"; the manuscript does not adjudicate it. The Chapter 7, 5, and 6 evidence sketches for innocence, the image-of-God, and the ontic condition of death make claims that *permit* the conditional-immortality reading — the manuscript's frame does not require natural immortality for the redemption argument to work — but the manuscript declines the move from permission to endorsement. The mission of this appendix does not require the manuscript to do so. The appendix's engagement here is to mark the consensus-on-the-text reading (the Old Testament's silence on immortal-souls, the New Testament's restraint, the *aion* range) without using it as evidence for any claim the chapters themselves would require the text to support.

**The boundary the appendix maintains.** This is one of the longer sections on a single topic, which reflects both the size of the secondary literature (it's the most contested doctrine the manuscript touches tangentially) and the importance of the manuscript *not* using its own silence as a conclusion. The conditionalist arguments of Wenham and Fudge are real and exegetical; the natality-tradition arguments of Augustine and the councils are also exegetical in their own terms; the manuscript's argument does not need either side, and the appendix names both with the same restrained precision.

## A.A.7 Modern Pauline scholarship

The modern scholarly literature on Pauline soteriology has been substantially reshaped in the past thirty years. Two stand out for the manuscript's engagement, and the *Journal for the Study of the New Testament* and its supplements provide the academic venue for the article-length treatments that examine these questions in detail.

N.T. Wright's *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003), the second volume of his *Christian Origins and the Question of God* series, is the major twentieth- and twenty-first-century treatment of the resurrection as the social, historical, and theological center of early Christian belief. Wright's argument is that the Jewish background for the resurrection hope is not the Platonic immortality of the soul but the Jewish hope of embodied restoration — the vindication of the righteous sufferer, of which Daniel 12:2–3 and the second-Temple martyrology texts are paradigm cases — and the Christian claim is the specific claim that Jesus of Nazareth was the embodiment of that hope. The result is that "righteousness" in Pauline soteriology is not the imputed acquittal before God (the framing common in the Reformed tradition) but the membership-in-the-new-creation frame that makes the believer part of the covenant people renewed (Wright's framing of "covenant," differently from the federal tradition).

James D.G. Dunn's *Theology of Paul the Apostle* (1998, Eerdmans), and the related "New Perspective on Paul" debate (Dunn's earlier 1988 Wenham/Sanders essays and the academic exchange with E.P. Sanders's *Paul and Palestinian Judaism*, 1977), has re-cast how Pauline soteriology is read. Dunn's principal move against the older Lutheran/Heidelberg-reformed reading is the "participation in Christ" focus (*Teilnahme* in the Schweitzer-Bultmann line, retrieved through the "in Christ" language of the Pauline corpus itself) — the believer participates in the death and resurrection of Christ, and the cross is the *event* the believer is conformed to, rather than the transaction in which the sinner is forensically acquitted. The "works of the law" re-read in the Galatian debates; the "righteousness of God" read as God's covenant faithfulness rather than the imputed alien righteousness the Reformed tradition has carried since the Reformation.

The *Journal for the Study of the New Testament* supplements (Sheffield Academic Press / Bloomsbury / T&T Clark) provide the article-length technical exegetical studies that develop these readings. The article-length treatments of Romans 5:12–21 (Schreiner and Westerholm in the SJOT/JNTS space; Ernst Käsemann's earlier comment in *Romans*, 1973, E.T. 1980, in dispute with Bultmann and the existential reading), and the *Adam-Christ* typology articles that draw on and contest Wright and Dunn, situate the manuscript's reading inside the modern debate.

**Where the manuscript's argument engages.** Chapter 11, on the redeemer being human, and Chapter 15, on the one-for-many mechanism, are the principal engagement points. Both chapters make claims about Adam/Christ typology that Wright and Dunn treat extensively. The manuscript's claim that the *same-order* correspondence is the load-bearing point (Ch 11, `[I-high]`) is a position both Wright and Dunn would find respectable, though they would arrive there from different second-Temple analyses than the manuscript's; the manuscript's claim that the one-act/many-recipients structure (Romans 5:18–19; 1 Corinthians 15:20–22) is the mechanism of federal one-for-many (Ch 15, `[I-high]`) is compatible with Dunn's participation reading and Wright's new-creation reading without depending on either. Chapter 14's propitiation reading engages the Käsemann/Bultmann "righteousness of God" reading (Käsemann reads the *dikaiosynē theou* in Romans 1:17; 3:21–26 as the *power of God* that creates salvation-history, against the Reformed imputation reading), and the manuscript's lexical-position is independent of Käsemann's resolution: Romans 3:21–26 is the verification-log text for the propitiation reading, and the *righteousness of God* technical question does not bear on the chapter's claim.

**The boundary the appendix maintains.** Wright's "new creation" reading and Dunn's "participation" reading have replaced the Lutheran-Reformed *imputed alien righteousness* reading in a significant portion of the academic literature; they have also been contested. The manuscript's reading of Romans 5:19, 1 Corinthians 15:22, and the Adam-Christ structure can be taken up by either, and the chapters neither accept nor reject either. What the chapters accept is the text's claim; what they engage here is the shape of the academic conversation. The boundary holds: the appendix names the traditions; the chapters refuse to depend on them.

## A.A.8 Unitarian-biblical-theology tradition

This is the tradition the manuscript, by the structure of its argument, inherits. It names the unitarian position the manuscript explicitly inherits the Bible-only commitment from, and the Adam/Christ-focused soteriology from. The tradition is older than the manuscript's specific framing; this section makes a few observations about its shape and substance and uses the unitarian tradition's identification of what the manuscript is doing without conceding that the manuscript's argument relies on the tradition.

The modern British unitarian movement took its biblicist shape in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, from Joseph Priestley (*A General History of the Christian Church*, 1790; Priestley held the Socinian position on the Adam/Christ theology) through Thomas Belsham, *A Calm Inquiry into the Scripture Doctrine concerning the Person of Christ* (1811). These texts identify the Biblicist-anthology unitarian stance on the personality of the Father and the humanity of Christ, with the Adam/Christ typology of Romans 5:12–19 and 1 Corinthians 15:20–22 as load-bearing.

The American unitarian tradition in its Trinitarian-skeptical current is more diffuse. The Biblicist wing (the Restoration Movement in the U.S., Alexander Campbell, *The Christian System*, 1835; the Unitarian tradition as it approached the Christian Convention) overlaps with British Biblicist unitarians at the Adam/Christ focus. The Campbellite/Restoration movement is the bridge from late-eighteenth-century Biblicist Unitarianism to the more recent English-language movements.

The Christadelphian tradition is the modern English-language movement that the manuscript has the closest natural kinship with. Founded by John Thomas (*Elpis Israel*, 1849), systematized by Robert Roberts (*Christendom Astray*, 1852; *The Kingdom of God*, etc.), the Christadelphians have run a biblicist-anthology program for nearly two centuries. Their commitments include Bible-only (no source outside the text), the humanity of Christ (one will, one nature, no Trinity), mortalism (the dead sleep until resurrection), conditional immortality (immortality is the gift of Christ at the resurrection), the rejection of forensic imputation (the condition of death is ontic), and the Adam-Christ focused soteriology. The manuscript inherits these commitments in substantial measure, with the proviso that the manuscript's argument is independent of them and would accept the same argument without them.

The contemporary historical scholarship on unitarian traditions is small but growing. Alan Gregory Floyd (*The Christ of the Gospels: A Study in Biblical Christology*, 1989) is one close academic engagement with the question of whether the New Testament's Christ is a Chalcedonian figure, although he writes from a Trinitarian position. The contemporary academic engagement with early unitarian trajectories (Arianism, Anomoean, Homoian, and fourth-century divergence) lives at the intersection of patristics and early-Church history in venues such as the *Studia Patristica* volumes.

**Where the manuscript's argument engages.** Chapter 0 §0.5, on the chapter's argument for the manuscript's own contribution, identifies the Bible-only constraint as the inheritance from the unitarian-biblical-theology tradition; the chapter's specific argument for the position is independent of the heritage. Chapter 11 and Chapter 15, on the redeemer and the one-for-many mechanism, work in the same room as the Christadelphian reading but do not depend on it. The Appendix B treatments (high-Christology passages) are explicitly the same scope as the Christadelphian and biblicist-unitarian consensus: the lexical readings (*monogenēs*, *kathistēmi*, *morphē theou*, *prōtotokos pasēs ktiseōs*, *theos anarthrous*, the two-natures reduction of the high-Christology verses) are the readings the manuscript covers without citing them from the tradition as evidence.

**The boundary the appendix maintains.** This is the section of the appendix where the manuscript's inheritance from the tradition is most direct, and where the boundary the appendix maintains is also most consequential. The manuscript's argument rests on the text alone; the tradition's identification of what the manuscript is doing is a piece of context that the manuscript accepts without being bound by. If the tradition had been anachronistic, the manuscript's argument would still stand; if the tradition had been entirely uniform, the manuscript's argument would still be open. The identification of the heritage is information the dissertation reader wants; it is not a warrant the manuscript claims.

## A.X Acknowledgement of scope and an impartial reading note

This appendix has engaged eight positions in the contemporary and historical secondary literature on redemption. The list is chosen; the omission of other positions (the Lutheran-Reformed Trinitarian scholastic tradition, the Eastern Orthodox patristic tradition, the broader late-medieval and post-Reformation Roman Catholic theology of grace, the modern liberationist and feminist theological treatments, and the post-Holocaust and postcolonial Christian theological engagements) is deliberate. The appendix is the bounded exception the Chapter 0 §0.2 charter licenses: it is the conversation the text's argument has generated, not a survey of the field of Christian doctrine. The chapters' argument rests on the text alone; the appendix names the conversation the reader might bring.

The neutral tone of these sections is itself an important feature of the appendix. The manuscript's argument is not adjudicated by being placed alongside the traditions it engages; the traditions are not refuted by being named; the manuscript is not endorsed by the fact that some of its positions overlap with the traditions and some do not. The reader can take the appendix as a catalog and as a map; the chapters are the argument.

Two notes on the appendix's verification. First, the major works named in the sections are cited by title and date; the standard edition is the source of quotation where the appendix quotes. Second, the appendix's claim that a tradition "holds" or "reads" a position is the appendix's reading of the tradition's standard documents; the tradition's own speakers may have said things differently, and the appendix is meant to summarize, not to adjudicate. The mantras and the conventions of the appendix are, in this sense, consistent with the manuscript's own epistemic discipline: claim what the texts say, not what the texts must say.
