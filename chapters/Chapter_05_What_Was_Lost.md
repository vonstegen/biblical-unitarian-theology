## 5.1 The question

The chapter's question is the inventory of what Adam's act actually lost. The inventory matters because the kind of loss determines the kind of restoration that addresses it (Chapters 16–17). The chapter confines itself to what the text says — the pre-Fall condition, the post-Fall consequences, and the explicit lists of what was forfeited. The chapter does not attempt to enumerate the speculative physics of the pre-Fall world (the immortal Adam body, the biological sinlessness, the climate of Eden); it confines itself to the textual evidence.

The chapter's structure is *not* a *locus* of all possible losses but a *bounded inventory* of the losses that the text explicitly identifies. The accept criterion is that the list be *explicit* and *tagged* — each loss identified, the evidence for the loss enumerated, and the band's confidence recorded. The chapter refrains from over-claiming in two specific ways: (a) it does not list any loss that the text does not identify, and (b) it does not infer the *mechanism* of any loss beyond what the text directly states.

These losses are the *ground* of the next chapter's claim (Chapter 6): what *passed to humanity* is the *consequence* of these losses — the kind of consequence Adam's act incurred that propagates to the descendants.

## 5.2 The pre-Fall condition (Genesis 1–2)

The pre-Fall condition is described by a small number of textual statements. The chapter inventories them in order.

**First, the creation was "very good."** "And God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good. And there was evening and there was morning, the sixth day" (Genesis 1:31, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The Hebrew phrase *tōv mə'ōd* ("very good") is the *culminating* comment of the creation account. The phrase is not merely descriptive; it is *constitutive* — the creation is declared *good* in a way that has force. The goodness is the *basis* of the divine presence in the garden (Genesis 3:8) and the *basis* of the human vocation (Genesis 1:26–28). The post-Fall condition is a *departure* from this declared goodness.

**Second, Adam and his wife were naked and were not ashamed.** "And the man and his wife were both naked and were not ashamed" (Genesis 2:25, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The verse is structurally important because it is the *last* verse of Genesis 2, immediately preceding the temptation narrative. The "not ashamed" is not a *psychological* detail; it is a *relational* state. The absence of shame is the *natural* condition of pre-Fall humanity — the absence of the rupture that shame presumes. The post-Fall condition is the *inversion* of this state: the eyes-open, the know-of-nakedness, the covering of the loin-cloths (Genesis 3:7), the hiding from the divine presence (Genesis 3:8). The single verse of Genesis 2:25 is the *negative* against which the post-Fall rupture is measured.

**Third, the human is the image of God.** "Then God said, 'Let us make man in our image, after our likeness'" (Genesis 1:26, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The image is the *constitutive* feature of the human being (see Chapter 2 §2.4). The image is the structural anchor of the dominion mandate (Genesis 1:28) and the basis for the *kind* of treatment the human is due (Genesis 9:6; James 3:9). The image is *not lost* in the fall (see §5.4 below for the textual evidence that the image persists); the image is the *constitutive* feature of the human that the fall *disrupts* but does not *annihilate*.

These three statements — the goodness of creation, the unashamed nakedness, the image of God — are the *positive* pre-Fall condition. The chapter's inventory of the losses is the *negative* of these positives.

## 5.3 The post-Fall rupture (Genesis 3:7–19)

The post-Fall rupture is described in two interlocking sequences: the *immediate* effects (Genesis 3:7–10) and the *declared* consequences (Genesis 3:16–19, 22–24). The chapter inventories them in order.

**Loss 1: The relational rupture with the divine presence.** "And they heard the sound of the Lord God walking in the garden in the cool of the day, and the man and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the Lord God among the trees of the garden" (Genesis 3:8, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The hiding is the *first* post-Fall action. The pre-Fall condition was the *unashamed* presence (Genesis 2:25); the post-Fall condition is the *hiding* from the divine presence. The hiding is not merely physical (the human hides from God); the hiding is relational — the human is now *unable* to stand in the divine presence without the rupture of the act. The chapter tags this as **Loss 1: The relational intimacy with the divine presence** — `[E]` for the existence of the hiding; the *interpretation* as *rupture* is `[I-high]`.

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "The hiding is a relational rupture, not merely physical"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Genesis 3:8 says they hid "from the presence of the Lord God" — the object of the hiding is God's *presence*, not a physical threat. (b) The pre-Fall state was unashamed nakedness in God's presence (Genesis 2:25), so the hiding inverts a *relational* state. (c) The fear named in Genesis 3:10 ("I was afraid… because I was naked") is a *relational* fear — shame before God, not danger.
- *Evidence against:* (a) "Hid themselves among the trees" is a *physical* action, so the verse could be read as physical flight rather than a relational rupture. (b) The text does not say the relation is *broken* — it says they hid, which is a reversible action.
- *Band justification:* The object of the hiding (God's presence) and its inversion of the unashamed state make the relational-rupture reading the most natural; the physical-hiding reading pulls against. `[I-high]` is honest.

**Loss 2: The fear of the divine presence.** "And he said, 'I heard the sound of you in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked, and I hid myself'" (Genesis 3:10, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The fear is the *affective* dimension of the rupture. The pre-Fall condition was the *unashamed* presence; the post-Fall condition is the *afraid* presence. The fear is the *psychological* symptom of the *relational* rupture; the two are not the same — the *fear* presupposes the *rupture*. The chapter tags this as **Loss 2: The fear of the divine presence** — `[E]` for the existence of the fear; the *interpretation* as *symptom* of the relational rupture is `[I-high]`.

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "Fear is the affective symptom of the relational rupture"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Genesis 3:10 joins fear and nakedness ("I was afraid, because I was naked") — the fear is about shame before God, not danger. (b) The pre-Fall state was "not ashamed" (Genesis 2:25), so fear is the *inversion* of the pre-Fall affect. (c) The fear follows the hiding (Genesis 3:8), tying it to the rupture.
- *Evidence against:* (a) Fear after sin could be a *natural* dread of punishment rather than a distinct "symptom" category. (b) The text states the fear directly without calling it a symptom of a rupture.
- *Band justification:* The joining of fear with nakedness and its inversion of the unashamed state make the symptom reading the most natural; the direct-statement reading pulls against. `[I-high]` is honest.

**Loss 3: The curse on the ground.** "And to Adam he said, 'Because you have listened to the voice of your wife and have eaten of the tree of which I commanded you, You shall not eat of it, cursed is the ground because of you; in pain you shall eat of it all the days of your life; thorns and thistles it shall bring forth for you; and you shall eat the plants of the field. By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread, till you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; for you are dust, and to dust you shall return'" (Genesis 3:17–19, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The curse is the *consequence* on the created order. The pre-Fall condition was the *uncursed* ground (Genesis 2:15 — Adam was *placed* in the garden to work it and keep it; the ground was *good*); the post-Fall condition is the *cursed* ground. The chapter tags this as **Loss 3: The curse on the ground** — `[E]` for the existence of the curse; the *interpretation* as *consequence* is `[I-high]`.

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "The curse is a judicial consequence on the created order"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Genesis 3:17 introduces the curse with "because you have listened to the voice of your wife and have eaten" — the curse is the *because* of the act, a consequence. (b) The curse is spoken *to Adam* ("And to Adam he said"), as a sentence. (c) The curse changes the ground from the pre-Fall good (Genesis 1:31) to a cursed state — an *imposed* alteration.
- *Evidence against:* (a) "Cursed is the ground" could be read as *descriptive* (the ground is now experienced as cursed) rather than a distinct judicial act. (b) The text does not use courtroom vocabulary.
- *Band justification:* The "because… you have eaten" structure and the sentence-form make the consequence reading the most natural; the descriptive reading pulls against. `[I-high]` is honest.

**Loss 4: The toil of the ground.** "By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread" (Genesis 3:19, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The toil is the *consequence* on the human's labor. The pre-Fall condition was the *work* of the garden (Genesis 2:15 — "to work it and keep it") — work as the *vocation* of the image-bearer; the post-Fall condition is the *toil* of the ground — labor as the *compelled* extraction of existence from the now-cursed ground. The chapter tags this as **Loss 4: The toil of the labor** — `[E]` for the existence of the toil; the *interpretation* as *loss* (the *kind* of labor is *changed* from vocation to toil) is `[I-high]`.

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "Toil is a changed kind of labor, from vocation to compelled toil"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Genesis 2:15 gives Adam the garden "to work it and keep it" — labor before the fall. (b) Genesis 3:17–19 re-describes the labor as "in pain," "by the sweat of your face," "thorns and thistles" — the labor is now compelled and painful. (c) The text's own sequence contrasts the pre-Fall *vocation* with the post-Fall *toil*.
- *Evidence against:* (a) The text says the ground is cursed, not that Adam's *labor itself* changed in kind. (b) "Toil" could be read as the *same* work now made difficult by the curse, rather than a change in the kind of work.
- *Band justification:* The before/after contrast between the garden-work (2:15) and the sweat-toil (3:17–19) makes the changed-kind reading the most natural; the same-work-made-difficult reading pulls against. `[I-high]` is honest.

**Loss 5: The mortality of the body.** "For you are dust, and to dust you shall return" (Genesis 3:19, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The mortality is the *operational definition* of the death-declaration in Genesis 2:17. The pre-Fall condition was the *uncursed* body formed from the dust (Genesis 2:7); the post-Fall condition is the *return to the dust*. The chapter treats the mortality as the *physical* aspect of the *death* declared in Genesis 2:17 (the chapter's argument in §3.2 was that the *death* includes the relational rupture, the ground-cursing, the toil, and the physical return). The chapter tags this as **Loss 5: The mortality of the body** — `[E]` for the existence of the mortality; the *interpretation* as the *physical* aspect of the death is `[I-high]`.

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "Mortality is the physical aspect of the death declared in 2:17"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Genesis 2:17 declared "you shall surely die." (b) Genesis 3:19 gives the operational content — "to dust you shall return" — matching the death to the dust-formation of Genesis 2:7. (c) Genesis 5:5 records Adam's physical death as the fulfillment.
- *Evidence against:* (a) "In the day that you eat" cannot refer to the physical death (which came centuries later), so the death of 2:17 might be broader than the physical. (b) The death could be primarily relational/spiritual, with the physical return as a secondary aspect.
- *Band justification:* The dust-to-dust wording and its match with the dust-formation make the physical-aspect reading the most natural; the deferred physical death pulls against a purely-physical reading. `[I-high]` is honest.

**Loss 6: The pain in childbearing.** "To the woman he said, 'I will surely multiply your pain in childbearing; in pain you shall bring forth children. Your desire shall be for your husband, and he shall rule over you'" (Genesis 3:16, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The pain is the *consequence* on the woman's domain. The pre-Fall condition was the *vocational* fruitfulness (Genesis 1:28 — "be fruitful and multiply"); the post-Fall condition is the *painful* fruitfulness. The chapter tags this as **Loss 6: The pain in childbearing** — `[E]` for the existence of the pain; the *interpretation* as the *kind* of fruitfulness being changed from painless to painful is `[I-high]`.

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "Childbearing changed from painless to painful fruitfulness"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Genesis 1:28 gives the command "be fruitful and multiply" — fruitfulness is the pre-Fall vocation. (b) Genesis 3:16 says "I will surely multiply your pain in childbearing" — the pain is *added* to the fruitfulness. (c) The verb "multiply" (*rabah*) links the two — fruitfulness multiplied, and pain multiplied.
- *Evidence against:* (a) The text does not say childbearing was *painless* before the fall — it only says pain is now multiplied. (b) "Multiply your pain" could be read as *intensifying* an existing pain rather than *introducing* pain.
- *Band justification:* The multiplication-of-pain language applied to the fruitfulness command makes the painless-to-painful reading the most natural; the intensify-existing-pain reading pulls against. `[I-high]` is honest.

**Loss 7: The relational rupture with the husband-wife relation.** "Your desire shall be for your husband, and he shall rule over you" (Genesis 3:16, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The ruling is the *consequence* on the husband-wife relation. The pre-Fall condition was the *united* pair (Genesis 2:18–24 — the woman was made as the *helper* corresponding to the man); the post-Fall condition is the *rule* of the husband over the wife. The chapter tags this as **Loss 7: The rupture in the husband-wife relation** — `[E]` for the existence of the rule; the *interpretation* as *rupture* (rather than *mere ordering*) is `[I-mid]` (see evidence sketch in §5.6).

**Loss 8: The expulsion from the garden.** "Therefore the Lord God sent him out from the garden of Eden to work the ground from which he was taken. He drove out the man, and at the east of the garden of Eden he placed the cherubim and a flaming sword that turned every way to guard the way to the tree of life" (Genesis 3:23–24, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The expulsion is the *consequence* on access to the divine presence. The pre-Fall condition was the *place* of the divine presence (Genesis 3:8 — the Lord God *walking in the garden*); the post-Fall condition is the *expulsion* from the place. The cherubim with the flaming sword are the *guardians* of the way back — the *forensic* lock on the garden. The chapter tags this as **Loss 8: The expulsion from the garden** — `[E]` for the existence of the expulsion; the *interpretation* as *loss* is `[I-high]`.

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "The expulsion is a loss of access to the divine presence"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Genesis 3:8 places the divine presence in the garden (the Lord walking there). (b) Genesis 3:23–24 drives the man out of the garden — the place of that presence — and sets guardians on the way back. (c) The cherubim later guard the place of divine-human meeting (Exodus 25:18–22; 1 Kings 6:23–28), so expulsion is loss of access to God.
- *Evidence against:* (a) The text states the expulsion as a *preventive* measure ("lest he… take also of the tree of life and eat"), so it could be read as protection rather than loss. (b) The loss of access to the tree of life is listed separately (Loss 9), so the expulsion's distinct loss is not explicit.
- *Band justification:* The expulsion from the place of the divine presence makes the loss-of-access reading the most natural; the preventive/protective reading pulls against. `[I-high]` is honest.

**Loss 9: The blocked access to the tree of life.** "He drove out the man, and at the east of the garden of Eden he placed the cherubim and a flaming sword that turned every way to guard the way to the tree of life" (Genesis 3:24, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The blockage is the *consequence* on the source of life. The pre-Fall condition was the *access* to the tree of life (Genesis 2:9 — "the tree of life was in the midst of the garden"); the post-Fall condition is the *blocked access* to the tree of life. The chapter tags this as **Loss 9: The blocked access to the tree of life** — `[E]` for the existence of the blockage.

This is the inventory of the *nine* losses the text explicitly identifies. The chapter tags each one with `[E]` for the textual fact; the *interpretation* of each loss (the *kind* of loss it is) is `[I-high]` or `[I-mid]` depending on the strength of the inference from the textual evidence.

## 5.4 The image of God is not lost

The chapter's *negative* claim — that the image of God is *not lost* in the fall — is based on the textual evidence that the image is *still* the basis for the human's *kind* of treatment after the fall.

> "Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed, for God made man in his own image." (Genesis 9:6, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The verse is *post-Flood* (the Noahic covenant). The image is the *post-Fall* basis for the *forensic* protection of the human — the human *still* bears the image that grounds the sanction of Genesis 9:6. The image is *not* lost.

> "For a man ought not to cover his head, since he is the image and glory of God, but woman is the glory of man." (1 Corinthians 11:7, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The verse is *post-Pentecost* (the Corinthian church). The image is the *post-Fall* basis for the *liturgical* distinction between man and woman in the Christian assembly. The image is *not* lost.

> "With it we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we curse people who are made in the likeness of God." (James 3:9, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The verse is *post-Pentecost* (the Jerusalem church). The image is the *post-Fall* basis for the *moral* restraint that the same mouth cannot bless God and curse the human. The image is *not* lost.

These three passages bracket the canonical arc — the image is the basis for the human's *forensic* (Genesis 9:6), *liturgical* (1 Corinthians 11:7), and *moral* (James 3:9) treatment *after* the fall. The image is *constitutive* of the human being and is *not* lost in the fall.

The chapter distinguishes three possible readings of the *post-Fall* status of the image:

- **Reading 1: Image is lost.** The image is *annihilated*; the post-Fall human is *not* the image of God; the constitution is *replaced*. *Ruled out* by Genesis 9:6, 1 Corinthians 11:7, James 3:9, all `[E]`. The image is *still* the basis for the human's treatment.
- **Reading 2: Image is *marred*.** The image is *constitutionally present* but *functionally disrupted*. The fall introduces a *dysfunction* in the exercise of the image: the image itself is preserved, but the dominion it was made to exercise (Genesis 1:28) is *disrupted* by the fallen condition. The chapter tags this as **the image is *marred* but not lost** — `[E]` for the textual evidence (the image is still the basis for treatment); the *interpretation* as *marred* is `[I-high]` (the textual evidence supports the *constitutional* presence; the *functional* disruption is inferred from the broader Fall narrative).

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "The image is constitutionally present but functionally disrupted"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Genesis 9:6 (post-Flood) grounds protection of human life in the image, so the image persists after the fall. (b) James 3:9 and 1 Corinthians 11:7 (post-Pentecost) still call humans the image/likeness of God. (c) The fall narrative (Genesis 3) shows the dominion exercise corrupted (the ground cursed, the pair-relation ruptured) even while the image-bearing constitution remains.
- *Evidence against:* (a) The text never says the image is "marred" — it only shows the image persisting and the consequences of sin. (b) "Marred" is a synthesis category, not a textual term.
- *Band justification:* The persistence of the image across the canon makes the constitutional-presence reading the most natural; the absence of an explicit "marred" term pulls against. `[I-high]` is honest.
- **Reading 3: Image is *restored* in Christ.** 2 Corinthians 3:18 — "And we all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another. For this comes from the Lord who is the Spirit" (ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. Colossians 3:10 — "and have put on the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator" (ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. Ephesians 4:24 — "and to put on the new self, created after the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness" (ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The image is *restored* (or *being restored*) in the Christian life. The chapter tags this as **the image is *being restored* in Christ** — `[E]` for the textual evidence; the *interpretation* as *being restored* is `[I-high]` (the textual evidence supports the *constitutional* presence and the *functional* restoration in Christ).

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "The image is being restored in Christ"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) 2 Corinthians 3:18 says believers "are being transformed into the same image." (b) Colossians 3:10 says the new self "is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator." (c) Ephesians 4:24 says the new self is "created after the likeness of God" — the same image/likeness vocabulary of Genesis 1:26–27 now applied to renewal.
- *Evidence against:* (a) These verses describe the *believer's* renewal, not a universal restoration of the image. (b) "Being transformed/renewed" is progressive, so "restored" overstates a process that is still underway.
- *Band justification:* The threefold reuse of the image/likeness vocabulary for the believer's renewal makes the being-restored reading the most natural; the progressive, believers-only scope pulls against. `[I-high]` is honest.

The chapter's *qualified* reading: the image is *constitutionally* present throughout the human condition (Genesis 9:6; 1 Corinthians 11:7; James 3:9) and is *functionally* being restored in the Christian life (2 Corinthians 3:18; Colossians 3:10; Ephesians 4:24). The image is *not lost* in the fall; the image is *being* *restored* in Christ. The two are compatible: the constitutional *kind* is preserved (the image is *not* lost), and the functional *exercise* is being *recovered* (the image is *being* *restored*).

## 5.5 The creation's groaning (Romans 8:18–25)

The post-Fall condition is not limited to the human. The Pauline text *extends* the consequence to the *creation itself*.

> "For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us. For the creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God. For the creation was subjected to futility, not willingly, but because of him who subjected it, in hope that the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to corruption and obtain the freedom of the glory of the children of God. For we know that the whole creation has been groaning together in the pains of childbirth until now. And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies. For in this hope we were saved. Now hope that is seen is not hope. For who hopes for what he sees? But if we hope for what we do not see, we wait for it with patience." (Romans 8:18–25, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The Pauline text identifies the *creation itself* as subjected to futility and groaning in the pains of childbirth. The "creation" (Greek *ktisis*) is the *non-human* creation — the ground, the animals, the cosmos. The futility is *not* the futility of the human but the *futility* of the *creation* that *humanity*'s fall introduced (cf. Genesis 3:17 — the curse on the *ground*). The Pauline text identifies the *hope* of the creation as the *redemption* of the children of God — the cosmic scope of the redemption (developed in Chapter 16).

The chapter tags this as **Loss 10: The creation's futility and groaning** — `[E]` for the textual evidence. The Pauline framework is *not* invented; it is the *reading* of the Genesis 3:17–19 curse on the ground as the *first* of the cosmic consequences of the fall.

**Loss 11: The hope of the *redemption of the body*.** "And not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`. The redemption of the body is the *Pauline* eschatological hope — the *future* redemption that the *present* groaning awaits. The chapter tags this as the *future* restoration that the *present* losses await. The *kind* of restoration (the redemption of the body) is the *kind* of restoration that addresses the *mortality* of the body (Loss 5). The chapter treats this as the *forward* reference to Chapter 16 — the Father's vindication and the new humanity. `[E]` for the textual evidence; the *interpretation* as the *forward* reference is `[I-high]`.

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "The redemption of the body is the forward reference to Chapter 16"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Romans 8:23 places "the redemption of our bodies" in the future ("we wait eagerly for… the redemption of our bodies"). (b) The body's redemption directly reverses the mortality of Loss 5 ("to dust you shall return"). (c) The chapter's inventory is the *losses*; the redemption of the body is the *restoration* that the later Vindication chapters (16–17) develop.
- *Evidence against:* (a) Romans 8:23 is Paul's eschatology, not an explicit cross-reference to a later chapter of this paper. (b) The "redemption of the body" is a hope of *believers* ("we ourselves"), not a universal reversal of all the losses.
- *Band justification:* The future-tense redemption and its reversal of Loss 5 make the forward-reference reading the most natural; the believers-only scope pulls against. `[I-high]` is honest.

## 5.6 The Genesis 3:16 translation question

The verse "Your desire shall be for your husband, and he shall rule over you" (Genesis 3:16) is a *translation* question. The Hebrew *'el* is the preposition that the ESV translates as "for" (your desire shall be *for* your husband). The same Hebrew preposition appears in Genesis 4:7 ("its desire is for you, and you must rule over it") — the Cain-and-sin passage. The Genesis 4:7 passage is unambiguous in the tradition: the *desire* is the *desire-for-mastery* (sin desires to master Cain), and the *rule* is Cain's *rule* over sin.
