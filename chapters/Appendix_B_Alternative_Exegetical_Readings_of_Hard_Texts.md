> **Sources:** Bible only for all theological claims. Secondary literature is used only to document translation biases and limitations (in `06_Translation_Bias_Documentation.md`), not to support theological claims. The sole authority and source material for theological claims is Scripture.

## B.1 The purpose of this appendix

The project is Bible-only for all theological claims (Introduction §I.2). Where the text is genuinely ambiguous, the project presents the range of readings from the translation set rather than choosing one on external authority. This appendix collects the hard texts that the main argument engages exegetically but does not resolve dogmatically. The readings are presented fairly with tags; the reader is left to evaluate. No reading is used as evidence for a theological claim. The appendix is `[H-mid]` for the specific interpretations; the ambiguity of the text is `[E]`.

The hard texts are the high-Christology passages that the church has read Trinitarianly for two millennia (John 1:1; Philippians 2:6; Colossians 1:15; Hebrews 1; John 20:28; Titus 2:13; 2 Peter 1:1). The project engages them exegetically but does not draw the Trinitarian conclusion. The unitarian reading is noted but not imposed as the only possible reading. The appendix is the place where the project is most transparent about the boundary between what the text says and what the argument infers.

## B.2 John 1:1 — "the Word was God"

> "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The verse is explicit: the Word was with God and the Word was God. The Greek *theos* is anarthrous (without the article) in the third clause. The translation set renders it "the Word was God" (ESV, NASB, LSV, Rotherham, REV) or "the Word was a god" (some unitarian translations). The anarthrous *theos* can be qualitative ("the Word was divine") or indefinite ("a god"). The chapter does not resolve the lexical question; it presents the range of readings from the translation set. The project treats the ambiguity as `[E]` for the textual fact; the *interpretation* as "the Word was the same God as the Father" is `[H-mid]` (the immediate context does not decide the question; the range of readings is real).

**Evidence sketch (`[H-mid]` for the Trinitarian reading):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The Word is called *theos* in the same verse as the Father is called *theos*. (b) The prologue's high Christology (the Word creates all things, is the light, becomes flesh) supports the reading that the Word is divine in the same sense as the Father.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The anarthrous *theos* can be qualitative or indefinite; the ESV footnote acknowledges the range. (b) The distinction "the Word was with God" suggests two persons. (c) The unitarian reading ("the Word was a god" or "the Word was divine") is consistent with the grammar.
- *Band justification:* The text calls the Word *theos*; the specific meaning (same God as the Father, a god, or divine in a lesser sense) is ambiguous. The Trinitarian reading is one plausible interpretation among others. `[H-mid]`.

The project notes the range of readings from the translation set and leaves the reader to evaluate. The verse is engaged exegetically but not used as evidence for a theological claim.

## B.3 Philippians 2:6 — "form of God" and "equality with God"

> "Who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped." (Philippians 2:6, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The verse is explicit: the one who was in the form of God did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped. The Greek *morphē theou* ("form of God") and *isa theō* ("equality with God") are the key terms. The translation set renders it "form of God" and "equality with God" (ESV, NASB, LSV, Rotherham, REV). The project treats the ambiguity as `[E]` for the textual fact; the *interpretation* as "the preexistent Son did not grasp at equality with the Father" is `[H-mid]` (the immediate context does not decide the question; the range of readings is real).

**Evidence sketch (`[H-mid]` for the preexistence reading):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The contrast with "taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men" suggests a prior state. (b) The self-humbling is from "form of God" to "form of a servant."
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The "form of God" could be read as the image of God in which all humans are created (Genesis 1:26–28). (b) The "equality with God" could be read as the temptation Adam faced (Genesis 3:5). (c) The unitarian reading ("the man Christ Jesus did not grasp at equality with God") is consistent with the grammar and the context of the hymn as an example for the church (Philippians 2:5).
- *Band justification:* The text contrasts "form of God" with "form of a servant"; the specific meaning (preexistent divine nature or the image of God in which the man Christ Jesus was created) is ambiguous. The preexistence reading is one plausible interpretation among others. `[H-mid]`.

The project notes the range of readings from the translation set and leaves the reader to evaluate. The verse is engaged exegetically but not used as evidence for a theological claim.

## B.4 Colossians 1:15 — "the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation"

> "He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation." (Colossians 1:15, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The verse is explicit: the one who is the image of the invisible God is the firstborn of all creation. The Greek *eikōn tou theou tou aoratou* ("image of the invisible God") and *prōtotokos pasēs ktiseōs* ("firstborn of all creation") are the key terms. The translation set renders it "the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation" (ESV, NASB, LSV, Rotherham, REV). The project treats the ambiguity as `[E]` for the textual fact; the *interpretation* as "the eternal Son is the image of the Father and the firstborn in the sense of eternal generation" is `[H-mid]` (the immediate context does not decide the question; the range of readings is real).

**Evidence sketch (`[H-mid]` for the eternal Son reading):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The "image of the invisible God" is the same language used for the Son in Hebrews 1:3 ("the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature"). (b) The "firstborn of all creation" is followed by "for by him all things were created" (Colossians 1:16), suggesting the Son is the agent of creation.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) "Firstborn of all creation" can be read as the first created being or the one who inherits the creation (the Davidic king is "firstborn" in Psalm 89:27). (b) The "image of the invisible God" can be read as the man Christ Jesus bearing the image of God (Genesis 1:26–28; 2 Corinthians 4:4). (c) The unitarian reading ("the man Christ Jesus is the image of the invisible God and the firstborn of all creation in the sense of the heir") is consistent with the grammar and the context of the hymn as a celebration of Christ's supremacy.
- *Band justification:* The text calls the one in view the image of the invisible God and the firstborn of all creation; the specific meaning (eternal Son or the man Christ Jesus as the image and heir) is ambiguous. The eternal Son reading is one plausible interpretation among others. `[H-mid]`.

The project notes the range of readings from the translation set and leaves the reader to evaluate. The verse is engaged exegetically but not used as evidence for a theological claim.

## B.5 Hebrews 1:1–3 — "the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature"

He is the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of his nature, and he upholds the universe by the word of his power. After making purification for sins, he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high." (Hebrews 1:1–3, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The passage is explicit: God has spoken by his Son, whom he appointed the heir of all things, through whom he created the world. The Greek *apaugasma tēs doxēs* ("radiance of the glory") and *charaktēr tēs hypostaseōs* ("exact imprint of his nature") are the key terms. **Evidence sketch (`[H-mid]` for the eternal Son reading):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The Son is the agent of creation ("through whom also he created the world"). (b) The "radiance of the glory" and "exact imprint of his nature" suggest a shared divine nature.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The "heir of all things" and "making purification for sins" are the work of the incarnate Son. (b) The "exact imprint" can be read as the man Christ Jesus bearing the image of God (Genesis 1:26–28; 2 Corinthians 4:4). (c) The unitarian reading ("the man Christ Jesus is the radiance of the glory and the exact imprint of God's nature in his exalted state") is consistent with the grammar and the context of the chapter as a celebration of Christ's supremacy.

## B.6 John 20:28 — "My Lord and my God"

> "Thomas answered him, 'My Lord and my God!'" (John 20:28, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The verse is explicit: Thomas says to Jesus "My Lord and my God." The Greek *ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou* is the same phrase used for the Father in the LXX (Psalm 35:23; Jeremiah 38:18). The translation set renders it "My Lord and my God" (ESV, NASB, LSV, Rotherham, REV). The project treats the ambiguity as `[E]` for the textual fact; the *interpretation* as "Thomas confesses Jesus as the same God as the Father" is `[H-mid]` (the immediate context does not decide the question; the range of readings is real).

**Evidence sketch (`[H-mid]` for the Trinitarian reading):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The phrase *ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou* is the standard address to the Father in the LXX. (b) The context is the resurrection appearance; Thomas's confession is the climax of the Gospel (John 20:31 — "that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God").
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The phrase could be an exclamation of astonishment ("My Lord and my God!" as in "Oh my God!"). (b) The unitarian reading ("Thomas addresses the Father through the risen Jesus" or "Thomas confesses Jesus as his lord and god in the sense of delegated authority") is consistent with the grammar and the context of the Gospel as presenting Jesus as the sent Son.
- *Band justification:* The phrase is the standard address to the Father; the specific meaning (Thomas confesses Jesus as the same God as the Father or as his delegated lord and god) is ambiguous. The Trinitarian reading is one plausible interpretation among others. `[H-mid]`.

The project notes the range of readings from the translation set and leaves the reader to evaluate. The verse is engaged exegetically but not used as evidence for a theological claim.

## B.7 Titus 2:13 and 2 Peter 1:1 — "our God and Savior Jesus Christ"

> "Waiting for our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ." (Titus 2:13, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "To those who have obtained a faith of equal standing with ours by the righteousness of our God and Savior Jesus Christ." (2 Peter 1:1, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The Greek *tou megalou theou kai sōtēros hēmōn Iēsou Christou* (Titus 2:13) and *tou theou hēmōn kai sōtēros Iēsou Christou* (2 Peter 1:1) are Granville Sharp constructions — one article governing two nouns. The translation set renders it "our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (ESV, NASB, LSV, Rotherham, REV). The project treats the ambiguity as `[E]` for the textual fact; the *interpretation* as "Jesus Christ is our great God and Savior" is `[H-mid]` (the immediate context does not decide the question; the range of readings is real).

**Evidence sketch (`[H-mid]` for the Trinitarian reading):**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The Granville Sharp construction typically refers to one person. (b) The context is the appearing of the glory of the one described as God and Savior.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The construction can be read as two persons in some contexts. (b) The unitarian reading ("the glory of our great God and of our Savior Jesus Christ") is consistent with the grammar and the context of the epistle as distinguishing God and Christ (Titus 1:4; 2 Peter 1:2).
- *Band justification:* The Granville Sharp construction is the standard reading; the specific meaning (one person or two) is ambiguous. The Trinitarian reading is one plausible interpretation among others. `[H-mid]`.

The project notes the range of readings from the translation set and leaves the reader to evaluate. The verses are engaged exegetically but not used as evidence for a theological claim.
