## 11.1 The question

Chapters 4–6 established the *debt-debtor* structure that runs through this paper: Adam's disobedience incurred a debt before God; death was set in Eden as the judicial consequence of disobedience; what Adam lost in the constitutional event passed to his descendants (Ch 6). Chapter 7 added that the debtor class passes through a *developmental* arc — from the constitutional inheritance received *in Adam* to the *forensic* accountability of the adult — without specifying the age at which the latter arrives. The remaining question is what *kind of being* can be the *corresponding representative* that pays the debt the first debtor incurred.

The correspondence principle — that the redeemer must stand in the same order as the debtor — is the chapter's claim. The chapter draws the correspondence principle from the Pauline Adam-Christ parallel and from the explicit New Testament statements about Christ's humanity. The redeemer is the kind of being the debtor is: the same order of human existence. The acceptance criterion (A11) is whether the chapter answers *why not a God-man?* via the correspondence principle, drawn from the texts themselves, with no appeal to Nicene or Chalcedonian categories from outside.

## 11.2 The Adam-Christ parallel: same order, opposite acts

The foundational text is Romans 5:18–19:

> "Therefore, as one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men. For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous." (Romans 5:18–19, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]` — E11.1

The structure is explicit. *One man* — *one trespass* — condemnation. *One man* — *one act of righteousness* — justification. The Greek construction *di' henos anthropou* ("through one man") names Adam in the first clause and Christ in the inverse. The correspondence runs along two axes simultaneously: the *numerical* axis (one and one) and the *categorical* axis (man and man). Both sides of the parallel are named by their humanity. `[E]`

Paul extends the parallel in 1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45, 47:

> "For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:21–22, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

> "Thus it is written, 'The first man Adam became a living being'; the last Adam became a life-giving spirit... The first man was from the earth, a man of dust; the second man is from heaven." (1 Corinthians 15:45, 47, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]` — E11.2

The titles are decisive. Adam is *the first man*; Christ is *the last Adam* and *the second man*. The counterpart to the first man is another *man*. A representative of a different order would break the parallel Paul constructs. The parallel is *man : man*, not *man : composite*. `[E]` for the *man / man* structure and the *last Adam* / *second man* titles; `[I-high]` for the inference that the parallel itself demands sameness of order.

**Evidence sketch — `[I-high]` "The Adam-Christ parallel demands a redeemer of the same order as the debtor"**

- *Evidence for:* (a) Romans 5:18–19 — the same Greek construction *di' henos anthropou* ("through one man") names both Adam and Christ; the parallel's *numerical* axis (one and one) is reinforced by a *categorical* axis (man and man). (b) Romans 5:14 — Adam is *a type of the one who was to come* (Greek *tupos*); the typological correspondence requires that the antitype share the kind of being that the type is. (c) 1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45, 47 — the titles *last Adam* and *second man* name Christ as *man*, not as a hybrid category; the Greek *deuteros anthrōpos* ("second man") is categorical.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) 1 Corinthians 15:47 calls Christ *the second man is from heaven*; the *from heaven* qualifier does not appear on the Adam side and could be read as a non-terrestrial origin. (b) The *last Adam became a life-giving spirit* (15:45) names Christ as *spirit* in a way Adam is not named; the *spirit* could be read as a categorical asymmetry.
- *Band justification:* The parallel's primary structure is *man / man* and *last Adam / second man*; the *from heaven* and *life-giving spirit* qualifiers name Christ's *origin* and *eschatological role*, not his ontological category. The chapter reads the parallel as demanding sameness of order, with the *origin* qualifier left in its textual place. The reading is `[I-high]`: strong implication from immediate context plus clear parallels; would be `[E]` if Paul asserted the sameness in propositional form ("the redeemer must be a man").

## 11.3 The parallel runs in both directions

The one-man / one-man structure of the Adam-Christ parallel is symmetrical, and the symmetrical reading constrains both sides. Paul names the first figure *the first man Adam* and *a man of dust*; he names the second *the last Adam* and *the second man* (1 Corinthians 15:45, 47). The titles use the same noun — *man* — for both. A representative of a different or mixed order on the second side would require the parallel's first side to be of that different or mixed order as well, or the one-for-one correspondence Paul constructs collapses.

Scripture never describes Adam as anything other than a man. His name (Hebrew *adam*) means *man* / *human*; in Genesis 2–3 the figure who receives the command, disobeys, and incurs the death-penalty is the first human *adam* formed from the ground. Romans 5 calls him *the one man* whose trespass condemned the many; 1 Corinthians 15 calls him *the first man*, *a man of dust*. The first side of the parallel is, by explicit textual testimony, plainly human. The second side must be the same.

This is the strict logic of sameness of order. If the Redeemer were a hybrid of two orders (the later *God-man* of the Chalcedonian formula), the parallel would require Adam to have been that hybrid as well. Adam was simply a man. Therefore the Redeemer is simply a man, occupying the same order Adam occupied. The debt was incurred by a man under the terms given to a man; the payment must be rendered by a man standing in the relation the parallel specifies. The negative case (*why not a God-man?*) becomes sharper for this reason: the parallel runs in both directions, and neither side can step outside the human order without breaking the structure. `[I-high]`

**Evidence sketch — `[I-high]` "The symmetrical structure of the parallel requires sameness of order to apply to both sides"**

- *Evidence for:* (a) 1 Corinthians 15:45, 47 — the titles *first man* and *second man* name both figures by the same noun (*anthrōpos*, man); the structural analogy requires categorical sameness. (b) Genesis 2–3 — the Hebrew name *adam* is the standard Hebrew word for *man* / *human*; Adam's narrative is the first human receiving and breaking the divine command, with no qualifiers that place him outside the human order. (c) Romans 5:12, 15, 17 — Adam is consistently *the one man* (*henos anthropou*); the structural unit of the parallel is *man*, with no second-nature framing.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* (a) The *from heaven* in 1 Corinthians 15:47 appears only on Christ's side and could be read as marking a categorical asymmetry between the two figures. (b) The *life-giving spirit* of 1 Corinthians 15:45 could be read as placing Christ in a non-human category. (c) A reader could argue that the *man : man* structure admits qualitative secondary differences within the human order; the chapter reads Paul's *man / man* titles as a categorical sameness that the secondary differences do not dissolve.
- *Band justification:* The parallel's primary structure — *man : man*, *first man : second man*, *man of dust : man from heaven* — names both figures by *man*. The *from heaven* and *life-giving spirit* qualifiers are about origin and eschatological role, not about the categorical kind the parallel specifies. The bidirectionality follows from the symmetry: Adam is plainly a man by textual testimony; Christ is plainly a man by parallel demand. The reading is `[I-high]`: strong implication from the structural symmetry; would be `[E]` if Paul had asserted the bidirectionality in propositional form.

## 11.4 "The man Christ Jesus" as mediator

> "For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all..." (1 Timothy 2:5–6, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]` — E11.3

The verse is explicit. *One mediator*. *The man Christ Jesus*. The mediation that secures the ransom is performed by the man. The text names the mediator by his humanity. The *one God* is distinguished from the *one mediator*; the mediator is identified as the man. There is no composite figure named here — there is the one God on one side and the mediator-man who gave himself as a ransom on the other. The Greek *anthrōpos Christos Iēsous* ("man Christ Jesus") is the subject of the mediation, not merely an adjective appended to it. `[E]`

## 11.5 Shared flesh and blood (Hebrews 2:14–17)

> "Since therefore the children share in flesh and blood, he himself likewise partook of the same things, that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil, and deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery. For surely it is not angels that he helps, but he helps the offspring of Abraham. Therefore he had to be made like his brothers in every respect, so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest in the service of God, to make propitiation for the sins of the people." (Hebrews 2:14–17, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]` — E11.4

The argument is causal. *The children share in flesh and blood; therefore he himself likewise partook of the same things.* The purpose clauses are explicit: *that through death* he might destroy the one who has the power of death, and *so that* he might become a merciful and faithful high priest. *He had to be made like his brothers in every respect.* The *had to be made* language is *necessity* — the high-priestly work could not be performed otherwise.

The text rules out angelic mediation (*not angels that he helps, but he helps the offspring of Abraham*) and grounds both the death and the high-priestly work in shared human nature. A being who did not share flesh and blood could neither die the death that frees those enslaved by fear of death, nor stand as the merciful high priest who makes propitiation for the people. The Greek *koinōnein* ("to share in / partake of") and the *tōn autōn* ("the same things") name a real participation, not a nominal one. `[E]`

## 11.6 Born of woman, born under the law (Galatians 4:4–5)

> "But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons." (Galatians 4:4–5, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The Son is *sent*, *born of woman*, and *born under the law*. The purpose is to redeem those under the law. The Redeemer enters the same condition the debtors occupy — human birth and subjection to the law — in order to redeem them from it. The Greek *genomenon ek gynaikos* ("born of woman") and *genomenon hypo nomon* ("born under law") name the two structural conditions of entry into the human order. Correspondence is again structural: the redeemer must be born of woman for the redemption of those born of woman; the redeemer must be under the law for the redemption of those under the law. The *hina exagorase* ("to redeem") names the *redemption* direction of the work — the Redeemer's birth under the law is for the redemption of those held under it. `[E]`

## 11.7 Descended from David according to the flesh (Romans 1:3–4)

> "Concerning his Son, who was descended from David according to the flesh and was declared to be the Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness by his resurrection from the dead, Jesus Christ our Lord." (Romans 1:3–4, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

Paul distinguishes two things. *According to the flesh* — descended from David. *According to the Spirit of holiness* — declared Son of God in power by the resurrection. The human lineage is real and is stated first; the *flesh* category is named before the *Spirit of holiness* category, and the Redeemer's identity is given first in the Davidic flesh he inherits. The Redeemer stands inside Israel's history as a man of Davidic descent. The two clauses do not name two natures; they name two *registers* in which the one Redeemer is identified — *according to the flesh* (his lineage), *according to the Spirit of holiness* (his resurrection-vindication). The Redeemer is flesh — Davidic flesh — and his vindication is by resurrection from the dead. The lineage is the correspondence; the resurrection declares what the lineage already accomplished. `[E]`

## 11.8 Form of a servant, likeness of men (Philippians 2:6–8)

> "Who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men. And being found in human form, he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross." (Philippians 2:6–8, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The movement is from *form of God* to *form of a servant*, *likeness of men*, and *human form*, culminating in *obedient to the point of death*. The prior *form of God* belongs to the wider register of the passage; the chapter's correspondence claim is grounded in the *human* side of the description. The incarnate path the text describes is the path of one who is *born in the likeness of men* and *found in human form*. The obedience that reaches the cross is the obedience of that human form.

Two lexical choices in the Greek anchor the correspondence. *homoiōmati anthrōpōn* ("likeness of men") is the human category Paul uses in this sentence — the Redeemer is *in the likeness of men*, sharing the human kind. *schēmati hōs anthrōpos* ("in human form / found in human form") is the *found* language — the Redeemer is *seen* (*heuristhē*, Greek aorist passive) as one *in human form*, that is, as visibly human. The Greek *schēma* names a *configuration*; the *human form* is a configuration the Redeemer occupies and is recognized by. Both terms describe the Redeemer's path *into* the human order, not a partial or nominal entry. The *obedient to the point of death* then names what the human form performs: the obedience of one who occupies that form, which is the obedience the debt-debtor structure requires. `[E]`

## 11.9 Tempted as we are; learned obedience through suffering (Hebrews 4:15; 5:7–9)

> "For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin." (Hebrews 4:15, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

> "In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard because of his reverence. Although he was a son, he learned obedience through what he suffered. And being made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him." (Hebrews 5:7–9, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The high priest is able to sympathize because he has been *tempted in every respect as we are*. He learned obedience through what he suffered. These statements presuppose a genuine human experience of testing, suffering, and growth in obedience. The Greek *peirazein* ("to tempt / test") used in 4:15 is the same verb used in the *temptation* of Jesus (Matt 4; Luke 4) — the *in every respect* (*kata panta*) names a *complete* correspondence of testing, not a partial one.

The *in the days of his flesh* (5:7) frames the obedience within the human order. A figure who did not share that order of existence could not be said to have learned obedience through suffering or to have been tempted as we are. The Greek *emathen hupakoēn* ("learned obedience") names a developmental progression; the *through what he suffered* (*aph' hōn epathen*) names the mechanism. The *yet without sin* (Heb 4:15) is the *qualification* — the obedience is real obedience rather than simulated obedience; the sinlessness is the *match* to the requirement, not the *substitution* of one order for another.

This is also where the chapter's argument integrates with Ch 7. Chapter 7 observed that the human being is constitutionally bent under Adam but is *developmentally* worked through — the kind of being who is tested, who learns, who grows. The Redeemer the correspondence principle requires is the kind of being who occupies that developmental arc in its strength: not one who bypasses testing and learning (that would be a different order), but one who *fully* enters it, in *every respect*, *yet without sin*. The sinlessness is what makes the Redeemer qualified to be the source of salvation; the human testing-and-learning is the order in which the obedience is performed. `[E]`

## 11.10 Synthesis: correspondence requires full humanity

The texts converge on a single structural requirement.

- The first man brought death; the counterpart is also a *man* (Romans 5:18–19; 1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45, 47).
- The one mediator between God and men is *the man Christ Jesus* (1 Timothy 2:5–6).
- He partook of the *same flesh and blood* as the children, *that through death* he might deliver them and so that he might become a merciful high priest (Hebrews 2:14–17).
- He was *born of woman*, *born under the law* — to redeem those under the law (Galatians 4:4–5).
- He is *descended from David according to the flesh* (Romans 1:3–4).
- He *took the form of a servant*, was *born in the likeness of men*, and was *found in human form* (Philippians 2:6–8).
- He was *tempted in every respect as we are* and *learned obedience through what he suffered* (Hebrews 4:15; 5:7–9).

From these statements the correspondence principle follows. The debtor is human; the debt is the debt of human disobedience under the terms God established for humanity. The one who repays must stand in that same order. A representative of a different order — a composite God-man figure in the later classical sense — would not occupy the place the parallel requires, would not share the flesh and blood through which death is destroyed, would not stand under the law as those he redeems stand under it, and could not learn the obedience the texts describe as the path of the Redeemer.

The parallel runs in both directions (§11.3). The symmetry of *first man : second man* and *one man's trespass : one man's obedience* is not a one-way label affixed to Christ alone; it is a structural identification Adam carries too, by name and by his narrative. Adam's plain humanity — by the consistent *one man* / *anthrōpos* language, by his place as the first human who received and broke the command, by the Hebrew name *adam* that means *man* / *human* — is itself the correspondence claim applied to the first side. The Redeemer is the kind of being Adam is, *because* Adam is the kind of being the Redeemer is. The negative case therefore cannot be evaded by appeal to the second figure's uniqueness — that uniqueness, in the *one man / many* parallel, is exactly what binds the Redeemer to Adam's order rather than to a different one.

The New Testament does not present the Redeemer as a hybrid figure whose non-human nature performs the decisive work. It presents him as the man who is the last Adam, the second man, the mediator, the one made like his brothers in every respect, the one who in the days of his flesh learned obedience and became the source of salvation. That is the correspondence the debt-debtor structure requires.

**Translation notes:**

1. **Romans 5:18–19** — *one trespass* (Greek *paraptōma*, "trespass / false step") and *one act of righteousness* (Greek *dikaiōma*, "righteous act / justification"). The ESV's *act of righteousness* renders *dikaiōma* as a specific act (Christ's obedience); other translations render it *righteous decree* or *justification*. The parallel's *categorical* structure (one man / one trespass :: one man / one act of righteousness) holds across the readings; the chapter uses *act of righteousness* in the sense of Christ's single representative obedience.

2. **Philippians 2:6** — *form of God* (Greek *morphē theou*). The *form* (Greek *morphē*) is rendered *form* across the standard translations; the precise sense — whether *morphē* names the *essential nature* or the *external manifestation* — is a point of wider discussion. The chapter does not adjudicate the question here; the present claim is grounded on the *form of a servant* / *likeness of men* / *human form* side of the contrast in 2:7–8.

3. **Philippians 2:7** — *emptied himself* (Greek *heauton ekenōsen*, "he emptied himself"). The verb *kenoō* is the *self-emptying* language; the *emptying* is the path from *form of God* to *form of a servant* / *likeness of men*. The chapter reads the *emptying* as the path the incarnate Redeemer takes into the human order, not as a categorical reduction in nature.

4. **Hebrews 2:16** — *he helps the offspring of Abraham* (Greek *epilambanetai spermatōn Abraam*). The verb *epilambanomai* means *to take hold of, to help, to assist*; the noun *sperma* means *offspring* / *descendants*. The text rules out angelic assistance and locates the Redeemer's help on the Abrahamic-descendant side.

5. **Hebrews 5:8** — *learned obedience through what he suffered* (Greek *emathen aph' hōn epathen tēn hupakoēn*). The verb *manthanō* (*learn*) names the *developmental* character of the obedience; the *through what he suffered* specifies the *mechanism*. The chapter reads the *learned obedience* as the *developmental* obedience of one who lives *in the days of his flesh* (5:7), not as a metaphor for what is in fact innate.

6. **Genesis 2–3** — *adam* (Hebrew אָדָם) is the standard Hebrew word for *man* / *human* / *mankind*; it is also the personal name of the first man. The figure who receives the command (Gen 2:16–17) and disobeys (Gen 3:6) is the first human *adam*, formed from the *adamah* (Hebrew אֲדָמָה, "ground"; Gen 2:7). The chapter reads *adam* in its generic-and-proper-noun sense both: the figure is named *adam* because he is drawn from the *adamah*, and the personal name anchors the *every-adam* reading the rest of Scripture carries (Gen 5:1–2; Rom 5:12, 18–19; 1 Cor 15:22, 45–47).
