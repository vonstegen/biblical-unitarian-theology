## 3.1 The question

Chapter 2 established that Adam was made in God's image, given dominion over the earth, and placed in the garden to work it and keep it. The picture is nearly complete. The missing piece is the limit — the one tree of the knowledge of good and evil, the prohibition, and the announced consequence of eating from it. The text handles all three in two verses (Genesis 2:16–17). The present chapter opens with those two verses and reads them as the constitutional declaration of the Edenic covenant.

The chapter does not assume the term *covenant of works* or the systematic-theological synthesis that the Reformed tradition builds on Eden. It confines itself to what the text says: God spoke to Adam, gave a command, attached a penalty, and then — once the command was broken — declared the consequences in detail. The covenant reading is treated as `[H-mid]` with an explicit evidence sketch; the death-penalty declaration is `[E]`.

## 3.2 The command and the penalty (Genesis 2:16–17)

The two central verses are unambiguous and load-bearing for the entire paper:

> "And the Lord God commanded the man, saying, 'You may surely eat of every tree of the garden, but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die.'" (Genesis 2:16–17, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

Three elements are here, and every later chapter in this paper depends on them.

**The command.** The verb *wayytsav* (וַיְצַו) is the standard Hebrew root for *command* — the same verb used in 1 Kings 5:30 for Solomon's command, in 2 Kings 17:35 for the divine commands given through Moses, and in Genesis 18:19 for God's command to Abraham. In every case the verb implies binding authority — what is commanded is not optional. The Hebrew infinitive absolute form *mōth tāmūth* (מוֹת תָּמוּת) on the verb *die* reinforces the gravity: "you shall surely die" is the standard Hebrew idiom for a certain, emphatic death, not a conditional or hypothetical one. The construction is "the same form used in Genesis 15:16 ('in the fourth generation they shall come back here') and in 1 Samuel 2:16 ('as they shall surely burn [the fat] today') — a biblical Hebrew idiom for a certain, emphatic outcome wherever it appears.

**The penalty.** "You shall surely die" is the declared consequence. The Hebrew *mōth tāmūth* is a doubled infinitive absolute — "dying you shall die" — which is the most emphatic form available to express the certainty of the consequence. The penalty is not a probability; it is a stated outcome. The death is judicial, not merely natural: it is announced by the one who has authority to set the penalty, before the act that would incur it. The whole debt-debtor framework of this paper rests on this prior declaration. `[E]` for the textual evidence; the interpretation of the death as judicial (rather than merely natural mortality) is `[I-high]` and is developed in the later chapters.

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "The death declared is judicial, not merely natural"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The penalty is declared *before* the act (Genesis 2:17) by the one who has authority to command — the command-then-consequence structure is judicial, not natural. (b) The form *mōth tāmūth* ("dying you shall die") is the emphatic idiom for a certain, imposed outcome, not a process description. (c) The execution in Genesis 3:17–19 is a *declared sentence* ("cursed is the ground… to dust you shall return"), spoken as judgment and matching the earlier declaration.
- *Evidence against:* (a) The text does not use a courtroom term (e.g. *mishpat*, "judgment") in Genesis 2:17; "death" could be read as the *natural* consequence of eating a forbidden tree. (b) "In the day that you eat of it" could be read as natural mortality being *initiated* rather than *imposed*. (c) The physical death occurs much later (Genesis 5:5), which a purely natural reading accommodates.
- *Band justification:* The declared-before-the-act structure and the emphatic idiom make the judicial reading the most natural; the absence of explicit courtroom vocabulary pulls against. `[I-high]` is honest.

**The day.** "In the day that you eat of it" — the consequence is immediate in declaration. The actual sequence of events in Genesis 3 — the eating, the eyes-opening, the hiding, the divine interrogation, the curse, the expulsion — plays out over the course of a single narrative day. The physical death of Adam happens much later (Genesis 5:5). The exegetical question of what the day of death refers to is genuine. The chapter treats it as `[I-high]` (the immediate judgment is real and the physical death is required; the precise referent of "the day" is debated). The most natural reading is that the penalty took effect that day — that the relation between Adam and God was ruptured, that the ground was cursed, that the expulsion from the garden happened that day.

**`[I-high]` evidence sketch — "The penalty took effect that day"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Genesis 2:17 binds the consequence to "the day that you eat of it" — the declaration ties the penalty to that day. (b) The immediate sequence in Genesis 3 (eyes opened, hiding, interrogation, curse, expulsion) unfolds within the single narrative day, so the judicial consequences are enacted that day. (c) The relational rupture and the curse are themselves the death *begun* — the chapter's §3.2 reading of death as more than physical.
- *Evidence against:* (a) The physical death is deferred to Genesis 5:5, so "the day" cannot refer to physical death literally. (b) "In the day" may be read as the idiom "when" (a temporal conjunction) rather than a literal same-day claim. (c) The referent of "the day" is debated — immediate spiritual death, the onset of mortality, or the day of the sentence.
- *Band justification:* The same-day enactment of the rupture, curse, and expulsion makes the "took effect that day" reading the most natural; the deferred physical death and the idiomatic reading of "in the day" pull against. `[I-high]` is honest.

## 3.3 The temptation and the transgression (Genesis 3:1–7)

The actual sequence of events is recorded in detail. The structure of the temptation mirrors the structure of the prohibition: the serpent raises the question of what God said (Genesis 3:1), the woman reports the prohibition (Genesis 3:2–3), the serpent denies the consequence (Genesis 3:4), the serpent promises autonomous godlikeness (Genesis 3:5), and the act follows (Genesis 3:6):

> "Now the serpent was more crafty than any other beast of the field that the Lord God had made. He said to the woman, 'Did God actually say, You shall not eat of any tree in the garden?'" (Genesis 3:1, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "And the woman said to the serpent, 'We may eat of the fruit of the trees in the garden, but God said, You shall not eat of the fruit of the tree that is in the midst of the garden, neither shall you touch it, lest you die.'" (Genesis 3:2–3, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "But the serpent said to the woman, 'You will not surely die. For God knows that when you eat of it your eyes will be opened, and you will be like God, knowing good and evil.'" (Genesis 3:4–5, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "So when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes, and that the tree was to be desired to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate, and she also gave some to her husband who was with her, and he ate." (Genesis 3:6, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "Then the eyes of both were opened, and they knew that they were naked. And they sewed fig leaves together and made themselves loincloths." (Genesis 3:7, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

Two observations are immediate and load-bearing.

**First**, the strategy of the temptation is to deny the declared consequence. The serpent attacks not the command itself but the penalty — "you will not surely die" (Genesis 3:4). The penalty-declaration of Genesis 2:17 is the very thing the serpent contests. The text's structure of the temptation is itself a proof that the penalty declaration was real — it was contestable, and it was contested.

**Second**, the act of transgression is shared. The woman gave to her husband, who was with her (Genesis 3:6). Adam was present at the temptation. The text is explicit that Adam was not deceived (1 Timothy 2:14 — "Adam was not deceived, but the woman was deceived"); the transgression is Adam's free act in the face of a clear command. The representation structure that the rest of the paper develops (especially Chapter 15) presupposes that Adam's transgression was an act of responsible obedience-or-not, not a failure of information.

## 3.4 The divine confrontation and the consequences (Genesis 3:8–19)

Once the act is done, the divine confrontation follows. The pattern is forensic: God seeks out the human, the human hides, the human's account of the act is given, and the consequences are declared. The structure is strikingly legal.

> "And they heard the sound of the Lord God walking in the garden in the cool of the day, and the man and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the Lord God among the trees of the garden." (Genesis 3:8, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "But the Lord God called to the man and said to him, 'Where are you?'" (Genesis 3:9, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "And he said, 'I heard the sound of you in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked, and I hid myself.'" (Genesis 3:10, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "He said, 'Who told you that you were naked? Have you eaten of the tree of which I commanded you not to eat?'" (Genesis 3:11, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "The man said, 'The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me fruit of the tree, and I ate.'" (Genesis 3:12, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

> "Then the Lord God said to the woman, 'What is this that you have done?' The woman said, 'The serpent deceived me, and I ate.'" (Genesis 3:13, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The pattern is unmistakable: a summons (verse 9), an accusation (verse 11), an excuse (verses 12, 13). The text is presenting a forensic-judicial scene, not a therapeutic conversation. The divine question is not seeking information; it is granting the hearing — the same kind of granting the hearing that the psalmist describes in Psalm 9:4 and 35:23. The person is given the opportunity to confess, to blame, or to remain silent; the consequence follows the answer.

The consequences are then declared in detail (Genesis 3:14–19). The serpent is cursed (verses 14–15); the protoevangelion — "he shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel" (verse 15) — is the first gospel announcement; the woman is told of pain in childbearing and the tension of her desire with her husband's rule (verse 16); the ground is cursed and toil is mandated (verses 17–18); and Adam is told that he will return to the ground from which he was taken (verse 19):

> "And to Adam he said, 'Because you have listened to the voice of your wife and have eaten of the tree of which I commanded you, You shall not eat of it, cursed is the ground because of you; in pain you shall eat of it all the days of your life; thorns and thistles it shall bring forth for you; and you shall eat the plants of the field. By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread, till you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; for you are dust, and to dust you shall return.'" (Genesis 3:17–19, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The phrase "to dust you shall return" is the operational definition of the death announced in Genesis 2:17. The penalty is not merely expulsion; it is the return to dust — the dissolution of the image-bearing creature back into the ground from which the human was formed. The forensic announcement in 2:17 and the forensic execution in 3:19 are the same event described from two sides. The chapters that follow build on this.

## 3.5 The expulsion and the locked garden (Genesis 3:22–24)

The chapter closes with the expulsion:

> "Then the Lord God said, 'Behold, the man has become like one of us in knowing good and evil. Now, lest he reach out his hand and take also of the tree of life and eat, and live forever —' therefore the Lord God sent him out from the garden of Eden to work the ground from which he was taken. He drove out the man, and at the east of the garden of Eden he placed the cherubim and a flaming sword that turned every way to guard the way to the tree of life." (Genesis 3:22–24, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07) `[E]`

The expulsion is the human consequence; the cherubim with the flaming sword are the guardians of the way to the tree of life. The garden is no longer the place where humanity can freely eat of the tree of life; the state of innocence that would have made eating from it appropriate is gone. The lock is forensic — the cherubim are preventing the man from re-entering, not preventing the tree from fruiting. The image of the cherubim is later used in the tabernacle (Exodus 25:18–22) and the temple (1 Kings 6:23–28) at the mercy seat — the same creatures that guard the garden later guard the place of divine-human meeting in the tabernacle. The chapter treats this verbal parallel as `[E]` (the cherubim are explicitly named in both places) and the systematic identification of the garden as proto-sanctuary as `[H-high]` (the parallels are convergent but the text does not call the garden a sanctuary; see evidence sketch in Chapter 2 §2.7).

The chapter's argument does not depend on the typological reading. What it depends on is the fact of the expulsion: Adam is removed from the place where the tree of life was accessible. The relation between Adam and the source of life is now mediated by the cherubim's sword. The penalty declared in 2:17 has taken effect.

## 3.6 The covenant reading

The chapter has so far stayed close to the text. The remaining question is whether the Edenic relation is properly called a covenant. The outline asks for this question to be answered carefully, with the `[H]` tag.

The word *covenant* (Hebrew *berit*) is absent from Genesis 1–3. The first appearance of the word in the canonical order is in Genesis 6:18 ("But I will establish my covenant with you") — *after* the fall, in the context of Noah. The word *covenant* is then used frequently for the Noahic covenant (Genesis 9:9–17), the Abrahamic covenant (Genesis 15:18; 17:1–11), the Mosaic covenant (Exodus 19:5; 24:7–8; 31:13), the Davidic covenant (2 Samuel 7:12–14), and the new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31–33). The Edenic relation is not described by the term *covenant* in the canonical text.

Three structural features of the Edenic relation, however, parallel the form of later covenants:

1. A declared relationship. Adam is set in the garden, given dominion, and addressed by God. The pattern is covenantal in the broader sense of a divine-human relation with stipulations.
2. A stipulated command. The prohibition of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil is a stipulation — a condition of the relation. The pattern is parallel to the stipulations of the Mosaic covenant (Exodus 19:5–6: "if you will indeed obey my voice and keep my covenant, you shall be my treasured possession among all peoples").
3. A declared consequence. The death-declaration of Genesis 2:17 is a forfeit clause — the consequence of failing the stipulation. The pattern is parallel to the blessings and curses of the Mosaic covenant (Leviticus 26; Deuteronomy 28).

The form is covenantal by structural analogy. Whether the term *covenant* is properly applied to Eden is a question of systematic categorization, not of textual vocabulary. `[E]` for the structural parallels; the proper application of the term *covenant* to the Edenic relation is `[H-mid]` (see evidence sketch below).

**`[H-mid]` evidence sketch — "The Edenic relation is properly described as a covenant"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) The structural parallels with later covenants (declared relationship, stipulated command, declared consequence). (b) The Hosea 6:7 reference — "But like Adam they transgressed the covenant" — if read as the proper noun *Adam* (the first man), implies that the Edenic relation was covenantal (see evidence sketch in Chapter 2 §2.5). (c) The Pauline reading of Adam as a type of Christ (Romans 5:12–21) frequently presupposes the covenant-of-creation framework as the original Adamic relation. *(d) The chapter notes that various post-canonical traditions have called the Edenic relation the "covenant of works" (a synthesis label), but such naming is a categorization the text itself does not impose; the chapter's argument does not rest on that naming.*
- *Evidence against:* (a) The word *berit* (covenant) is absent from Genesis 1–3. (b) The first covenant in the canonical text is the Noahic covenant in Genesis 6:18; the covenant terminology is not retroactively applied to Eden by the text of Genesis 1–3 itself. (c) The Pauline reading of Adam as a type of Christ (Romans 5) does not require the Edenic relation to be a covenant in the formal sense — the type relation works on the basis of representation, not formal covenant terminology. (d) The Hosea 6:7 reference is ambiguous between the proper-noun reading (Adam) and the common-noun reading (man), and the LXX takes the common-noun reading (see Chapter 2 §2.5 evidence sketch).
- *Band justification:* The structural parallels are convergent from the biblical text alone; the chapter treats the covenant application as `[H-mid]` — the most natural reading of the structure, but not the only possible reading. The chapter's argument does not depend on this resolution; the death-penalty declaration is `[E]` regardless of whether the term *covenant* is applied. The chapter does *not* appeal to any post-canonical tradition (Reformed, Roman Catholic, etc.) as evidence for the covenant reading; the chapter's argument is drawn from the biblical text alone.

## 3.7 The death-penalty as the load-bearing citation

The chapter's contribution to the spine of the paper is the declaration of the death-penalty in Genesis 2:17, executed in Genesis 3:17–19 and 3:22–24. This is the citation that every later chapter in the paper presupposes.

- Chapter 4 (The Nature of Adam's Sin) presupposes that the act of Genesis 3:6 is a covenantal transgression, not a moral lapse; the declared penalty is what makes the act covenantal.
- Chapter 5 (What Was Lost?) presupposes that the loss is the consequence of the declared penalty, not a separate metaphysical event.
- Chapter 6 (What Passed to Humanity?) presupposes that the passing of consequences to Adam's descendants is the execution of the declared penalty across generations.
- Chapter 11 (Why Must the Redeemer Be Human?) presupposes that the declared penalty falls on the human, and so the redeemer must be human.
- Chapter 14 (The Cross as Payment) presupposes that the cross is the place where the declared penalty is paid — not waived, not merely declared forgiven, but paid.
- Chapter 15 (Why One Man for Many) presupposes that the one-for-many structure works because the one is the representative who bears the declared penalty on behalf of the many.

The covenant of Eden is the constitutional foundation of the whole paper. The penalty-declaration is the load-bearing citation. The text is unambiguous; the systematic implications are developed in the chapters that follow.

## 3.8 The chapter's argument in summary

**Three propositions are made explicit in the text:**

1. God gave Adam a command with a declared consequence (Genesis 2:16–17). The command is binding; the consequence is certain. `[E]`
2. Adam transgressed the command (Genesis 3:6). The transgression was a free act of responsible obedience-or-not, not a failure of information. `[E]`
3. God declared the consequences in detail (Genesis 3:8–19, 22–24). The consequences include the curse on the serpent, the woman's pain in childbearing, the curse on the ground, the sweat of labor, the return to dust, and the expulsion from the garden. The death-penalty declared in 2:17 is operationalized in 3:19 as "to dust you shall return." `[E]`

The Edenic relation is structurally covenantal — declaration, stipulation, consequence — but the term *covenant* is not used of Eden in the text itself. The covenant reading is `[H-mid]`; the chapter's argument does not depend on this resolution.

The chapter establishes the load-bearing citation for the rest of the paper: death was set as the judicial consequence of Adam's transgression. The redemption that the rest of the paper develops must address this declared consequence.

**Translation notes:**
- The Hebrew *mōth tāmūth* (מוֹת תָּמוּת) in Genesis 2:17 is a doubled infinitive absolute — the most emphatic form available in Hebrew for a certain, decisive outcome. The ESV's "you shall surely die" captures the emphatic form. The same construction appears in Genesis 15:16 ("in the fourth generation they shall come back here"), 1 Samuel 2:16 ("as they shall surely burn the fat today"), and 1 Samuel 14:44 ("you shall surely die, Saul"). The chapter's reading of the death-penalty as certain and judicial is grounded in this grammatical form.
- Genesis 3:15 — "he shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel" — the protoevangelion (first gospel announcement). The Hebrew *šāp̄at* is rendered "bruise" by the ESV; KJV/NKJV use "bruise" as well. The LXX renders it *τηρήσει* (will keep/watch); the Vulgate renders it *conteret* (will crush).
