## 4.1 The question

The Genesis account of Adam's act is short — a single verse describes the eating (Genesis 3:6). The classification of that act is the question. Was it a *moral lapse* (a failure of self-control, a momentary weakness of will)? Was it a *cognitive error* (a misjudgment of consequences)? Was it a *natural mistake* (an inevitable outcome of finite creaturehood)? Or was it a *covenantal disobedience* — a knowing, willing rejection of a declared command, made by the representative of the human race?

The Pauline reflection on Adam's sin (Romans 5:12–21; 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49) and the explicit clarification in 1 Timothy 2:13–14 are the textual evidence for the classification. The chapter argues that the Pauline framing requires the *covenantal* reading and that the alternatives do not fit the structure of the biblical text.

## 4.2 The act itself (Genesis 3:1–6)

The text records the act as a knowing, deliberate, unrepented violation of a declared command. The structure of the narrative is forensic — the same structure as a covenant lawsuit. The temptation is introduced (Genesis 3:1–5), the act follows (Genesis 3:6), and the divine confrontation follows (Genesis 3:8–13). The act is *not* described as a moment of ignorance, a slip, or an unintended consequence. The act is described as an act of *eating* — a deliberate, intentional action in the face of a declared prohibition.

> "So when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes, and that the tree was to be desired to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate, and she also gave some to her husband who was with her, and he ate." (Genesis 3:6, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The text's vocabulary of *desire* (*taawah*, also rendered "desire" / "lust" / "covet" in different translations) is the same vocabulary used in Genesis 4:7 for sin crouching at the door — Cain's *yet-uncommitted* temptation. The vocabulary is the vocabulary of *moral temptation*, not *moral ignorance*. The woman *saw* the tree, *desired* it, *took* it, *ate* it. The sequence is purposeful, not accidental. Adam's parallel act — "she also gave some to her husband who was with her, and he ate" — is similarly deliberate.

The Pauline clarification is explicit on this point:

> "For Adam was formed first, then Eve; and Adam was not deceived, but the woman was deceived and became a transgressor." (1 Timothy 2:13–14, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

Two facts are stated. **First**, Adam was *formed first* — the order is significant because it makes Adam the temporal head of the human pair (the *protological* representative). **Second**, Adam was *not deceived* — the act was not a cognitive error. Adam ate with full knowledge that the act was prohibited. The text could not be clearer: Adam's transgression was a *knowing, willing violation* of a declared command. The classification "moral lapse" is ruled out by the text itself. Adam's act was not a failure of *information*; it was a failure of *obedience*.

## 4.3 The Pauline framing: Adam as covenantal head (Romans 5:12–21)

The Pauline reflection on Adam's sin is the structural backbone of the chapter. Romans 5:12–21 establishes the *one-man / one-man* parallel that the rest of the paper presupposes. The parallel has three load-bearing features.

**First, the universality of the consequence.** "Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned" (Romans 5:12, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`. The death that came through Adam's sin is *universal* — "spread to all men." The universality is structural, not selective; it is part of the *consequence-structure* of the act, not a separate divine decree. The act is treated as *constitutive* of the human condition.

**Second, the representative structure.** Romans 5:13–14 makes the *type* structure explicit: "for sin indeed was in the world before the law was given, but sin is not counted where there is no law. Yet death reigned from Adam to Moses, even over those whose sinning was not like the transgression of Adam, who was a type of the one who was to come" (Romans 5:13–14, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`. Adam is *a type* (Greek *tupos*) of the one who was to come. The type is the *one-man structure* — the one act that has effect on the many. The Pauline framing is *not* that Adam's act is *imitated* by all humans (the standard moral-influence atonement theory); the Pauline framing is that Adam's act *constitutes* the many as sinners in a representative sense.

**Third, the parallel structure of the redemption.** "For if many died through one man's trespass, much more have the grace of God and the free gift by the grace of that one man Jesus Christ abounded for many" (Romans 5:15, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`. "And the free gift is not like the result of that one man's sin. For the judgment following one trespass brought condemnation, but the free gift following many trespasses brought justification" (Romans 5:16, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`. The parallel is *type-typified* — Adam is the type, Christ is the antitype. The one-act-of-one-man structure that brought condemnation is paralleled by the one-act-of-one-man structure that brings justification. The parallelism is *structural*, not merely *rhetorical*.

The Pauline framing thus classifies Adam's act as a *constitutive act* — an act whose effect is not limited to the actor but extends to all those the actor represents. The classification "moral lapse" cannot accommodate the universality of the consequence (no lapse explains universal death); the classification "cognitive error" cannot accommodate the Pauline framing (Adam was not deceived); the classification "natural mistake" cannot accommodate the explicit penalty-declaration in Genesis 2:17 (a mistake is not a transgression of a declared command). The classification that fits is *covenantal disobedience*: Adam, as the covenantal head of humanity, knowingly transgressed a declared command, and the consequence declared in advance took effect. `[E]` for the textual evidence; the *systematic identification* of the Pauline framing as covenantal-headship is `[I-high]` (the term is the systematic-theological synthesis; the structural observation is the Pauline text).

## 4.4 The Pauline framing: Adam as the last Adam (1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49)

The Corinthians parallel adds two further features to the Adam-as-covenantal-head reading.

**First, the resurrection of Christ presupposes the *already-corporate* nature of Adam's sin.** "But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep. For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:20–22, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`. The phrase "in Adam all die" presupposes that *being in Adam* is a structural category, not a moral one. The "in Adam" relation is *constitutive* — being in Adam is the condition of being in the sphere of Adam's death. The textual structure is *en Adamō / en Christō* — *in* Adam / *in* Christ — and the *in* is the same on both sides. The displaced reading (Adam's act is *imitated* by each person who sins) cannot sustain the parallelism.

**Second, the cosmic scale.** The "in Adam" relation is *not* limited to those who consciously sin after Adam's example. The "in Adam" relation is *constitutive* of the human condition from conception (Genesis 5:3 — "When Adam had lived 130 years, he fathered a son in his own likeness, after his image, and named him Seth"; the *likeness after image* language is the same as Genesis 1:26–27, locating Seth's *constitution* in Adam's image-of-God structure). The descriptive claim "in Adam all die" is universal in Paul, not restricted to those who consciously imitate Adam's sin. The Corinthians parallel presupposes the *corporate* (one-man / many) structure that the Romans parallel articulates.

**Third, the last-Adam typology.** "Thus it is written, 'The first man Adam became a living being'; the last Adam became a life-giving spirit. But it is not the spiritual that is first but the natural, and then the spiritual. The first man was from the earth, a man of dust; the second man is from heaven. As was the man of dust, so also are those who are of the dust, and as is the man of heaven, so also are those who are of heaven. Just as we have borne the image of the man of dust, we shall also bear the image of the man of heaven" (1 Corinthians 15:45–49, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`. The "first man" / "last Adam" / "second man" / "man of heaven" framing is typological — the two are paired as *representatives* of the two humanities. The pairing is not *biological-ancestry* (Adam being the literal ancestor of all humans) nor *metaphorical* (Adam being a generic symbol of mortality); the pairing is *representative* — the two are heads of the two humanities. `[E]` for the textual pairing; the *characterization* of the pairing as *representative* rather than *biological* or *metaphorical* is `[I-mid]` (see evidence sketch in Chapter 15 §15.6).

## 4.5 The Romans 6 frame: obedience as the operative category

The Romans 6 frame is the third Pauline contribution to the classification. Adam's act is not merely *sin* in the abstract but *disobedience* — the opposite of the obedience that Romans 6:16–22 makes the structuring category of the Christian life.

> "Do you not know that if you present yourselves to anyone as obedient slaves, you are slaves of the one whom you obey, either of sin, which leads to death, or of obedience, which leads to righteousness? But thanks be to God, that you who were once slaves of sin have become obedient from the heart to the standard of teaching to which you were committed, and, having been set free from sin, have become slaves of righteousness. I am speaking in human terms, because of your natural limitations. For just as you once presented your members as slaves to impurity and to lawlessness leading to more lawlessness, so now present your members as slaves to righteousness leading to sanctification. For when you were slaves of sin, you were free in regard to righteousness. But what fruit were you getting at that time from the things of which you are now ashamed? For the end of those things is death. But now that you have been set free from sin and have become slaves of God, the fruit you get leads to sanctification and its end, eternal life. For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." (Romans 6:16–23, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

The framework is *discipleship* — the Christian life is the *obedience of faith*. The dualism is *sin / obedience* (verse 16), *death / righteousness* (verse 16), *lawlessness / sanctification* (verse 19). Adam's act is, in this framework, the *paradigm* of *disobedience* — the act that constituted the *slavery to sin* that the Christian is now freed from. The classification is *covenantal* — the act is treated as a *relation of obedience* that has been broken, not as a *moral lapse* that has been committed. The text itself uses the language of *slavery* (verse 17) and *obedience* (verse 16) — the vocabulary of covenantal *relation*.

## 4.6 The James 1:14–15 frame: desire, sin, and death

The James frame is the explicit textual statement of the *sequence* that Adam's act instantiates. The sequence is *desire → sin → death*, and the sequence is the general pattern of sin:

> "But each person is tempted when he is lured and enticed by his own desire. Then desire when it has conceived gives birth to sin, and sin when it is fully grown brings forth death." (James 1:14–15, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`

Adam's act is the *prototypical* instantiation of this sequence. The woman *saw* the tree (Genesis 3:6, *desire*); she *took* its fruit (the *conception* of sin); the act was the *full-grown* sin. The death-declaration of Genesis 2:17 is the *bringing-forth* of death. The James frame is the textual pattern that Adam's act illustrates.

The textual observation is important: the sequence is *not* an accident or a misjudgment. The sequence is the standard pattern of how sin operates. Adam's act is the *first* instance of the pattern, and the universality of death (Romans 5:12) is the *consequence* of the pattern recurring throughout the human lineage. `[E]` for the textual sequence; the *interpretation* of Adam's act as the *prototypical* instance of the James sequence is `[I-high]` (the parallel is structurally clear in the text).

## 4.7 The Romans 7:7–11 frame: the law awakens sin

The Romans 7 frame adds a further observation: the *command* itself can function as the occasion of transgression. "What then shall we say? That the law is sin? By no means! Yet if it had not been for the law, I would not have known sin. For I would not have known what it is to covet if the law had not said, 'You shall not covet.' But sin, seizing an opportunity through the commandment, produced in me all kinds of covetousness. For apart from the law, sin lies dead. I was once alive apart from the law, but when the commandment came, sin came alive and I died. The very commandment that promised life proved to be death to me. For sin, seizing an opportunity through the commandment, deceived me and through it killed me" (Romans 7:7–11, ESV; verified against esv.org) `[E]`.

The pattern is identical to Adam's: a *command* (Genesis 2:16–17; Exodus 20:17) creates the *occasion* for transgression; the transgression operates *through* the command; the consequence is *death*. The Romans 7 frame is *not* about Adam specifically but about the *mechanism* of sin in general — and the mechanism is the same mechanism that Adam's act instantiates. The point of the chapter is that Adam's act is the *first* and *prototypical* instance of the pattern that the law-revelation makes general. The pattern is: *command → opportunity → transgression → death*. The pattern is *covenantal* in its structure. `[E]` for the textual sequence; the *interpretation* of Adam's act as the *prototypical* instance of the pattern is `[I-high]`.

## 4.8 The categories of sin and the disqualification of alternatives

The Pauline and Jamesian categories constrain the classification of Adam's act. The alternatives to the covenantal-disobedience reading can be enumerated and ruled out by the textual evidence.

**Alternative 1: "moral lapse"** — Adam's act was a failure of self-control, a momentary weakness of will. *Ruled out* by 1 Timothy 2:14 ("Adam was not deceived") and by the universality of the consequence (a momentary lapse does not explain universal death). The covenantal-disobedience reading fits the evidence.

**Alternative 2: "cognitive error"** — Adam's act was a misjudgment of consequences. *Ruled out* by 1 Timothy 2:14 (Adam was not deceived) and by the explicit penalty-declaration in Genesis 2:17 (the consequence was declared before the act; the act was not a misjudgment of consequences unknown). The covenantal-disobedience reading fits the evidence.

**Alternative 3: "natural mistake"** — Adam's act was an inevitable outcome of finite creaturehood. *Ruled out* by the explicit penalty-declaration in Genesis 2:17 (a natural mistake is not a transgression of a declared command). The covenantal-disobedience reading fits the evidence.

**Alternative 4: "covenantal disobedience"** — Adam's act was a knowing, willing violation of a declared command, made by the representative of the human race. *Fits all the evidence*: the Pauline framing (Romans 5:12–21; 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49); the explicit clarification in 1 Timothy 2:13–14 (Adam was not deceived); the universality of the consequence (Romans 5:12); the structure of the act (Genesis 3:6 — deliberate, sequitur-of-desire, knowing); the explicit penalty-declaration in Genesis 2:17 (the act was a transgression of a declared command). The covenantal-disobedience reading is the classification that the biblical text requires.

The textual evidence does not permit the weaker classifications. The *kind* of act determines the *kind* of redemption that addresses it. If Adam's act was a moral lapse, redemption is a *moral improvement* (the formal-cause approach to atonement). If Adam's act was a cognitive error, redemption is *information* (the moral-influence approach). If Adam's act was a natural mistake, redemption is *grace that overrides* (the universalist approach). If Adam's act was a covenantal disobedience, redemption is *corresponding satisfaction* — the debt-debtor framework of the rest of the paper. The Pauline framing of Adam's act is the *specification* of the redemption the rest of the paper develops.

## 4.9 The chapter's argument in summary

**Three propositions are made explicit in the text:**

1. Adam's act was a *knowing, willing violation* of a declared command (Genesis 3:6; 1 Timothy 2:14 — "Adam was not deceived"). The classification "moral lapse" / "cognitive error" / "natural mistake" is ruled out by the text. `[E]`

2. Adam's act is treated by Paul as *constitutive of the human condition* (Romans 5:12–21; 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49). The "in Adam all die" structure is universal, not selective; the *in* is the same on both sides of the Adam-Christ parallel. `[E]`

3. Adam's act is the *prototypical* instance of the *desire → sin → death* sequence (James 1:14–15) and the *command → transgression → death* pattern (Romans 7:7–11). The Pauline framing locates Adam's act as the *first* and *exemplary* instance of the universal pattern. `[E]`

The classification that fits all the textual evidence is *covenantal disobedience*. The chapter's argument is the textual demonstration that this is the only classification that fits. The categories of the Pauline framing — representation, constitution, obedience — are the systematic-theological vocabulary that the rest of the paper uses.

The chapter establishes the *kind* of act. The kind determines the *kind* of redemption. The redemption is developed in Chapters 11–14.

**Translation notes:**

1. **Psalm 51:5** ("Behold, I was brought forth in iniquity, and in sin did my mother conceive me") is the classic proof-text for the doctrine of *inherited original sin* in the Western Christian tradition. The verse is Davidic — David's confession in the aftermath of his sin with Bathsheba (2 Samuel 11–12). The verse is *not* a universal-original-sin claim in its literary context; it is a confession of the *depth* of David's own sin in light of God's holiness. The traditional Augustinian reading (used by the Reformers and the Roman Catholic tradition) treats the verse as a *universal* claim about the inherited condition of humanity; the chapter notes the existence of the debate but does *not* base the argument on Psalm 51:5. The chapter's argument rests on the Pauline corporatism (Romans 5–6, 1 Corinthians 15) and the 1 Timothy 2:14 clarification, not on Psalm 51. The chapter rates the inheritance-of-original-sin claim as `[H-mid]` (see §4.10 / Chapter 6's domain).

2. **Genesis 5:3** ("he fathered a son in his own likeness, after his image") is the textual evidence that Adam's *offspring* inherit his *constitutional structure*. The "in his own likeness, after his image" language has been read in two ways: (a) the *physical* likeness (Seth looks like Adam — genetics) and (b) the *constitutional* likeness (Seth inherits Adam's fallen condition). The chapter reads it as the latter, consistent with the Romans 5:12–21 framework. The reading is `[I-high]` (the textual evidence is real; the *interpretation* depends on the prior Pauline framework).

3. **The 1 John 3:4 citation** ("sin is lawlessness") is the strongest single NT verse equating *sin* with *lawlessness* (Greek *anomia*). The chapter cites this as the strongest lexical evidence for the *covenantal-disobedience* reading of Adam's act — the act is not merely a *moral lapse* but a *transgression of declared law*. The translation note is recorded to flag the lexeme *anomia* as the technical term for the *kind* of act Adam performed (covenantal transgression), not merely the *emotional state* (lapse, error).

4. **The Romans 7:7–11 frame** is sometimes read as a *psychological* account of Paul's experience (the "wretched man" of Romans 7:14–25). The chapter reads it as a *mechanism* of sin — the *command* awakens sin, sin operates through the command, the consequence is death. The mechanism is the same mechanism that Adam's act instantiates. The *psychological* reading is not foreclosed by the chapter's argument (the two readings are compatible), but the chapter's interest is in the *mechanism*, not the *psychology*.
