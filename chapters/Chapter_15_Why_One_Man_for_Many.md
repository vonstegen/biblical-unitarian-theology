## 15.1 The question

The thesis rests on the claim that Scripture presents redemption through a *corresponding human representative* — that the substitute must be of the same order as the debtor. If that claim holds, the natural next question is: **why does one man's obedience suffice for many?** Why not one man per person, or some other distribution? Why does the debt-debtor structure work at all?

The biblical answer, drawn from Romans 5:12–21 and its parallels, is **federal headship**: the one acts on behalf of the many because the one is the many's representative. This chapter works through the textual basis for that claim.

## 15.2 The spine verse: Romans 5:18–19

> "Therefore, as one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men. For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous." (ESV)
>
> "So then as through one transgression there resulted condemnation to all men, even so through one act of righteousness there resulted justification of life to all men. For as through the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, even so through the obedience of the One the many will be made righteous." (NASB 1995)

Two observations are immediate and load-bearing. `[E]` (explicit in the text):

1. **The structure is parallel.** Adam's act → many constituted sinners; Christ's act → many constituted righteous. The "one" and the "many" are matched term-for-term.
2. **The sufficiency is structural, not biological.** Paul does not say "because Christ shared our DNA" or "because Christ's death was physically applied to each person." He says *through the one man's obedience*. The mechanism of transfer is the *one-for-many* structure itself.

## 15.3 The structural parallel in detail

The Romans 5:12–21 passage sets up the parallel by repeating "the one" and "the many" seven times (vv. 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 [twice], 21). Each repetition reinforces the same structure: one act → many receive its effect. `[E]`

Three Greek terms carry the parallel:

| Greek | ESV | NASB | Literal sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| *paraptōma* | trespass | transgression | a falling aside, deviation |
| *parakoe* | disobedience | disobedience | a hearing-amiss, refusal to listen |
| *hupakoe* | obedience | obedience | a hearing-under, attentive submission |

The contrast is between *parakoe* (the wrong kind of hearing) and *hupakoe* (the right kind). The same root (*akoe* — hearing) underlies both, which is what makes the parallel precise rather than loose: Adam's act was a *hearing-amiss* of God's command; Christ's act was a *hearing-under* of God's will. Both are *acts of response*, and both have representative effect. `[E]`

## 15.4 The verb of constitution: *kathistēmi* / *kathistamai*

The Greek verb in Romans 5:19 (*kathistēmi*) is rendered variously across translations:

| Translation | Romans 5:19 (constituted/made) |
|---|---|
| ESV | "the many were made sinners" / "the many will be made righteous" |
| NASB 1995 | "the many were made sinners" / "the many will be made righteous" |
| YLT | "the many were constituted sinners" / "the many shall be constituted righteous" |
| LSV | "the many were constituted sinners" / "the many shall be constituted righteous" |
| Rotherham | "the many were constituted sinners" / "the many shall be constituted righteous" |
| REV | "the many were made sinners" / "the many will be made righteous" |
| CLV | "the many were constituted sinners" / "the many shall be constituted righteous" |

This is a meaningful lexical point. *Kathistēmi* in extra-biblical and biblical Greek carries the sense of *to appoint, establish, constitute* — a formal/legal positioning rather than a mere material making. The more literal translations (YLT, LSV, Rotherham, CLV) preserve this. `[I]` — the legal/positional sense is what Paul's argument requires: Adam's sin *positioned* the many as sinners (not merely caused them to sin); Christ's obedience *positions* the many as righteous (not merely enables them to become righteous).

This lexical observation matters because it forecloses the most common objection to federal headship: the "why is this fair?" objection. The answer Paul's language implies is that the *positioning* is what redeems — the many are constituted righteous in the same sense they were constituted sinners, by the same kind of representative structure. Whether that constitutes *imputation* in the technical theological sense is `[H]` (a hypothesis that requires further exegetical work); the basic claim that the constitution is positional is `[I]` to `[E]` depending on how strongly one reads the verb.

## 15.5 The *dikaiōma* choice in Romans 5:18

The second major lexical point is *dikaiōma* in Romans 5:18:

| Translation | Romans 5:18 |
|---|---|
| ESV | "one act of righteousness" |
| NASB 1995 | "one act of righteousness" |
| YLT | "one declaration of 'Righteous'" |
| LSV | "one righteous declaration" |
| Rotherham | "one righteous deed" |
| REV | "one righteous act" |
| CLV | "one judicial decision" |

*Dikaiōma* in the LXX and NT carries multiple senses: a righteous act, a righteous decree, a justification, an ordinance. Each translation picks a different shade. `[I]` for the specific sense.

What matters for the debt-debtor argument: the *dikaiōma* is one specific act — not a process of becoming righteous, not a state of righteousness, not an abstract principle. **One act corresponds to one trespass** in the parallel structure. The "one-for-one" structural claim holds regardless of which lexical sense of *dikaiōma* is operative, because all senses preserve the *singular* (*henos*) — the parallel is between one trespass and one act/decree/declaration, not between one trespass and many acts or a process.

## 15.6 The parallel in 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49

> "But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep. For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:20–22, ESV; verified against esv.org)
>
> "Thus it is written, 'The first man Adam became a living being'; the last Adam became a life-giving spirit. But it is not the spiritual that is first but the natural, and then the spiritual. The first man was from the earth, a man of dust; the second man is from heaven. As was the man of dust, so also are those who are of the dust, and as is the man of heaven, so also are those who are of heaven. Just as we have borne the image of the man of dust, we shall also bear the image of the man of heaven." (1 Corinthians 15:45–49, ESV; verified against esv.org)

Paul names Adam "the first man" and Christ "the last Adam" and "the second man" (or "the man of heaven"). The two are paired as parallel representatives, not as biological ancestors or generic metaphors. `[E]`

The "in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive" structure is the same Romans 5:18–19 parallel in different vocabulary: *en Adam* / *en Christō* = *in Adam* / *in Christ*. The "in" (Greek *en*) marks the *sphere* or *representative relation* — being "in Adam" or "in Christ" is to be within the scope of that one's representative act. `[E]` for the parallel; `[I]` for the precise nature of the *en* relation.

## 15.7 The federal headship claim itself

The claim that emerges from the Romans 5 + 1 Corinthians 15 texts, taken together, is what systematic theology calls *federal headship*: Adam and Christ are *heads* of the respective humanities they represent, and their acts are the *constitutive acts* of those humanities.

Federal headship has two essential features, both of which are `[E]` in the text:

1. **Universality of representation.** Adam's sin affects all who are "in Adam" — i.e. all humanity descended from him, per Paul's framing: "Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned" (Romans 5:12, ESV; verified against esv.org). Christ's obedience affects all who are "in Christ" (per Paul's many references to believers being "in Christ" — see also Romans 8:1; 1 Corinthians 1:30; 2 Corinthians 5:17; Galatians 3:28). The universality on both sides is structural, not selective.

2. **Sufficiency through the *one* structure.** Paul does not say "Christ's obedience is sufficient because God decided to count it as sufficient." He says Christ's obedience is sufficient because *one man's obedience* corresponds to *one man's disobedience* — and the parallel structure itself is what makes the transfer work.

## 15.8 Why "fairness" is not the right objection

The most common objection to federal headship is fairness: "It's not fair that I'm held responsible for Adam's sin, or that I benefit from Christ's obedience without choosing it."

Paul's answer is structural, not volitional. The "in Adam" / "in Christ" relation is not based on individual choice but on *representative constitution*. The same structure that holds the many "in Adam" also holds them "in Christ" — both are constitutive relations, not chosen relations. The question "is it fair to be in Adam without choosing it?" has the same answer as "is it fair to be in Christ without choosing it?" — neither is fair in the individual-choice sense, but both are *structurally necessary* given that humanity exists as a *represented* humanity rather than as a collection of *autonomous individuals*. `[H]` — this is the strongest framing I can give from Scripture; it requires the further exegetical work in chapters on election and divine sovereignty to be fully developed.

What Scripture explicitly rules out is the volitionalist counter: that the sufficiency of Christ's obedience depends on each individual's act of faith adding to it. Scripture presents faith as the *instrument* by which one enters the "in Christ" relation (Romans 5:1–2; Ephesians 2:8–9), not as the *ground* of the sufficiency itself. `[E]` — the ground is the *one man's obedience*; faith is how the many receive its effect.

## 15.9 The "all" in Romans 5:18

A second objection concerns the universality of "all men" in Romans 5:18:

> "Therefore, as one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men."

The "all" in the first half (condemnation for all) refers to all humanity. The "all" in the second half (justification for all) is more contested — does it mean all humanity, or all believers? `[H]` — this is a hypothesis requiring further exegetical work.

What is `[E]` is the *parallel structure*: the second "all" matches the first "all." Whatever the first "all" denotes, the second "all" denotes the same scope. To deny the second universality while affirming the first is to break the parallel Paul has constructed. The exegetical question is then: *what* is the scope of "all" in both clauses?

The most natural reading, given the parallel structure, is that both "all"s are *equally universal* — the second "all" is no narrower than the first. What this means for the scope of redemption is `[H]`; what is `[E]` is that *Paul intends a parallel*.

## 15.10 The chapter's argument in summary

The argument of this chapter is that the "one man / many" structure of Romans 5:12–21 and 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49 establishes *federal headship* as the scriptural mechanism by which Christ's obedience suffices for the many. The structural claims are `[E]`: the parallel between Adam and Christ, the universality of representation on both sides, and the sufficiency through the *one* structure rather than through individual merit. The lexical observations (*kathistēmi* as constitution; *dikaiōma* as act/decree; *parakoe*/*hupakoe* as the paired hearing-amiss / hearing-under) are `[I]`: they support the structural reading but are not unambiguous in every translation.

The remaining questions — the scope of "all," the nature of the *en* relation, the relationship between faith and federal inclusion — are `[H]` and will be developed in later chapters (the Conclusions chapter §17 will map each research question to its chapter of treatment).
