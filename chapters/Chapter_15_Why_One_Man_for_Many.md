## 15.1 The question

Chapter 11 established the prior correspondence principle: the redeemer must be of the same order as the debtor (Chapter 11 §§11.1–11.3, especially §11.3's bidirectional man : man argument). That conclusion is the foundation this chapter inherits, not a premise this chapter re-proves. Given one such corresponding human representative, the present question is: why does one man's obedience suffice for many? Why not one man per person, or some other distribution? Why does the debt-debtor structure work at all?

**Backward to Chapter 11 (the Correspondence Principle):** this section presupposes the same-order, pure-human representative established by the quoted debt-side and Adam-Christ evidence in Chapter 11 §§11.1–11.3. The present chapter therefore addresses the numerical and representative mechanism only.

The biblical answer, drawn from Romans 5:12–21 and its parallels, is federal headship: the one acts on behalf of the many because the one is the many's representative. This chapter works through the textual basis for that claim.

## 15.2 The spine verse: Romans 5:18–19

> "Therefore, as one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men. For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous." (ESV)

> "So then as through one transgression there resulted condemnation to all men, even so through one act of righteousness there resulted justification of life to all men. For as through the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, even so through the obedience of the One the many will be made righteous." (NASB 1995)

Two observations begin the argument. The first is explicit in the text `[E]`; the second states the textual mechanism and then marks its explanatory inference separately:

1. The structure is parallel `[E]`. Adam's act leads to condemnation for the many; Christ's act leads to justification and life for the many. The "one" and the "many" are matched within Paul's repeated *hōs ... houtōs* construction.
2. The text places the many under the effect of one man's obedience `[E]`. The claim that this parallel structure is the reason one corresponding man's obedience is sufficient is `[I-high]`: it explains the stated relation, but Paul does not state the explanatory sentence in those terms.

**Evidence sketch — `[I-high]` "The one-for-many parallel explains why one corresponding man's obedience suffices"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Romans 5:18–19 explicitly places one act opposite one act and one man's obedience opposite one man's disobedience, with many receiving the corresponding result. (b) The repeated *hōs ... houtōs* symmetry presents the two representative acts as parallel structures. (c) 1 Corinthians 15:21–22 repeats "by a man" / "by a man" and "in Adam" / "in Christ," reinforcing the representative mechanism.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* The text states the one-to-many relation but does not use the later explanatory phrase "federal headship" or explicitly say that correspondence alone is the ground of sufficiency. The precise relation between representative act, faith, and application remains contested in §§15.8–15.10.
- *Band justification:* The parallel is the strongest explanation of why one act has a many-person effect, but the explanatory claim goes beyond the explicit wording. `[I-high]` is honest; the underlying one-act / many-recipients structure remains `[E]`.

## 15.3 The structural parallel in detail

The Romans 5:12–21 passage sets up the parallel by repeating "the one" and "the many" seven times (vv. 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 [twice], 21). Each repetition reinforces the same structure: one act → many receive its effect. `[E]`

Three Greek terms carry the parallel:

| Greek | ESV | NASB | Literal sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| *paraptōma* | trespass | transgression | a falling aside, deviation |
| *parakoe* | disobedience | disobedience | a hearing-amiss, refusal to listen |
| *hupakoe* | obedience | obedience | a hearing-under, attentive submission |

The contrast is between *parakoe* (the wrong kind of hearing) and *hupakoe* (the right kind). The same root (*akoe* — hearing) underlies both, which is what makes the parallel precise rather than loose: Adam's act was a hearing-amiss of God's command; Christ's act was a hearing-under of God's will. Both are acts of response, and both have representative effect. `[E]`

## 15.4 The verb of constitution: *kathistēmi*

The Greek verb in Romans 5:19 (*kathistēmi*) is rendered variously across translations:

| Translation | Romans 5:19 (constituted/made) |
|---|---|
| ESV | "the many were made sinners" / "the many will be made righteous" |
| NASB 1995 | "the many were made sinners" / "the many will be made righteous" |
| YLT | "the many were constituted sinners" / "the many shall be constituted righteous" |
| LSV | "the many were constituted sinners" / "the many shall be constituted righteous" |
| Rotherham | "the many were constituted sinners" / "the many shall be constituted righteous" |
| REV | "the many were made sinners" / "the many will be made righteous" |
| CLV | "the many were constituted sinners" / "the many shall be constituted righteous" |

*Kathistēmi* in extra-biblical and biblical Greek carries the sense of *to appoint, establish, constitute* — a formal/legal positioning rather than a mere material making. The more literal translations (YLT, LSV, Rotherham, CLV) preserve this. `[I-mid]` for the specific sense — the legal/positional reading is what Paul's argument requires, but the immediate grammar admits a broader "brought into a state" sense.

**Evidence for** the positional/legally-constitutive sense:
(a) The parallel structure of vv. 18–19 — one act → many *made* in a parallel sense on both sides. The symmetry requires the same kind of "making" on each side; the more literal translations preserve this symmetry.
(b) Paul's other uses of *kathistēmi* — 1 Corinthians 4:9 (apostles "appointed"/"set forth" in public display); 1 Timothy 2:12 ("not assuming authority over a man"). Both apostolic and authoritative uses are positional.
(c) The LXX use of *kathistēmi* for priestly installation — 1 Kings 2:35; 2 Chronicles 29:11. The LXX renders Hebrew *nāṣab* (to station, to set up as priest) with *kathistēmi*. Priestly installation is constitutional/positional.

**Evidence against** the positional sense:
(a) The verb can also mean "to make, cause" in some NT uses — e.g., Matthew 24:45; Luke 12:42 (appointing/setting over). This blurs the line between positional and material sense.
(b) ESV and NASB 1995's "made" reflects a translational judgment that the broader "made" sense is operative. The primary translations are not unreasonable to choose "made."
(c) The immediate grammar of Romans 5 doesn't *require* a legal sense — a broader "brought into a state" sense works grammatically. The legal reading is *implied* by the argument structure, not asserted.

**Band justification:** The textual evidence supports both readings, with the Pauline-parallel and LXX-installation arguments pulling toward the positional sense, but the broader-grammatical sense and the primary-translation choice pulling against. `[I-mid]`.

## 15.5 The *dikaiōma* choice in Romans 5:18

The second major lexical point is *dikaiōma* in Romans 5:18:

| Translation | Romans 5:18 |
|---|---|
| ESV | "one act of righteousness" |
| NASB 1995 | "one act of righteousness" |
| YLT | "one declaration of 'Righteous'" |
| LSV | "one righteous declaration" |
| Rotherham | "one righteous deed" |
| REV | "one righteous act" |
| CLV | "one judicial decision" |

*Dikaiōma* in the LXX and NT carries multiple senses: a righteous act, a righteous decree, a justification, an ordinance. Each translation picks a different shade. `[I-mid]` for the specific sense — *dikaiōma* is the right word to highlight in this verse, but its precise shade is contested.

**Evidence for** the *dikaiōma*-as-positional-act reading:
(a) The parallel with *paraptōma* — *paraptōma* in 5:18 is a specific act (the one trespass in Eden), not a state or process. The *dikaiōma* on the other side is parallel: a specific act, not a state.
(b) Paul's use elsewhere — Romans 1:32; 2:26; 8:4 (where *dikaiōma* appears in the same theological register) — all refer to specific divine ordinances or acts, not abstract principles.

**Evidence against** the *dikaiōma*-as-positional-act reading:
(a) The LXX use of *dikaiōma* for divine ordinances (Deuteronomy 33:10; Isaiah 58:2) emphasizes the *decree* sense, which would tilt the reading toward "righteous decree" or "justification" rather than "righteous act."
(b) The CLV's "judicial decision" reading is consistent with this LXX use; CLV's concordant-vocabulary approach picks this sense systematically.
(c) Multiple translations land on different shades — no consensus within the project's translation set on which sense is operative.

**Band justification:** The parallel with *paraptōma* and Paul's other Pauline uses pull toward a specific act reading; the LXX use and CLV's concordant approach pull toward a decree reading. The competing readings are both plausible within the Bible's own lexical range. `[I-mid]`.

What matters for the debt-debtor argument: the *dikaiōma* is one specific act — not a process of becoming righteous, not a state of righteousness, not an abstract principle. One act corresponds to one trespass in the parallel structure. The "one-for-one" structural claim holds regardless of which lexical sense of *dikaiōma* is operative, because all senses preserve the singular (*henos*) — the parallel is between one trespass and one act/decree/declaration, not between one trespass and many acts or a process.

## 15.6 The parallel in 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49

Paul's "one man / many" structure appears again in 1 Corinthians 15:

> "But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep. For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:20–22, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07)

> "Thus it is written, 'The first man Adam became a living being'; the last Adam became a life-giving spirit. But it is not the spiritual that is first but the natural, and then the spiritual. The first man was from the earth, a man of dust; the second man is from heaven. As was the man of dust, so also are those who are of the dust, and as is the man of heaven, so also are those who are of heaven. Just as we have borne the image of the man of dust, we shall also bear the image of the man of heaven." (1 Corinthians 15:45–49, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07)

Paul names Adam "the first man" and Christ "the last Adam" and "the second man" (or "the man of heaven"). The title pairing is the same pairing Chapter 11 §11.2 used to establish the man : man correspondence; this chapter reuses it for the distinct representative-pairing and image argument. The pairing of the two as parallel representatives is `[E]`. The characterization of the pairing — "not as biological ancestors or generic metaphors" — is `[I-mid]` (the author's interpretive gloss distinguishing representative pairing from mere biological descent or loose metaphor; Paul himself does not foreclose the descent framing, but the representative framing is the operative one in the rhetorical structure).

The "in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive" structure is the same Romans 5:18–19 parallel in different vocabulary: *en Adam* / *en Christō* = *in Adam* / *in Christ*. The "in" (Greek *en*) marks the sphere or representative relation — being "in Adam" or "in Christ" is to be within the scope of that one's representative act.

- The parallel is `[E]` — the immediate text pairs Adam and Christ in this construction.
- The precise nature of the *en* relation (sphere vs. organic union vs. legal inclusion) is `[H-mid]` — see §15.10 below.

## 15.7 The federal headship claim itself

The claim that emerges from the Romans 5 + 1 Corinthians 15 texts, taken together, is what systematic theology calls federal headship: Adam and Christ are heads of the respective humanities they represent, and their acts have constitutive effects within those humanities. The technical term is `[I]`; the underlying one-for-many observations are drawn from the text itself.

Federal headship has two distinguishable features:

1. **Universality of representation** `[E]`. Adam's sin affects all who are "in Adam" — i.e. all humanity descended from him, per Paul's framing: "Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned" (Romans 5:12, ESV; verified against esv.org on 2026-08-07). Christ's obedience affects those who are "in Christ" (see Romans 8:1; 1 Corinthians 1:30; 2 Corinthians 5:17; Galatians 3:28). The text explicitly presents the one-to-many relation; the exact scope and mode of inclusion remain examined below.
2. **Sufficiency through the one-for-many structure** `[I-high]`. Paul places one man's obedience opposite one man's disobedience and assigns each act a many-person effect. The claim that this parallel structure explains why one corresponding man's obedience suffices is the inference stated in §§15.1–15.2, not a separate proposition Paul states in those words.

**Evidence sketch — `[I-high]` "The one-for-many structure is the explanatory ground of sufficiency"**
- *Evidence for:* (a) Romans 5:18–19 repeats the one / many correspondence and pairs condemnation with justification and life. (b) The *hōs ... houtōs* construction signals a matched relation rather than an unrelated distribution. (c) 1 Corinthians 15:21–22 repeats the same man / man and in-Adam / in-Christ structure.
- *Evidence against / weakening:* Scripture states the representative effects but does not use the technical term *federal headship* or explicitly formulate the parallel as a theory of sufficient value. Faith, application, and the exact scope of the many remain debated in §§15.8–15.10.
- *Band justification:* The repeated parallels make the structural explanation the strongest reading of why one act affects many, but the explanatory wording is inferential. `[I-high]` is honest.

**Note on vocabulary:** "Federal headship" is a shorthand for these scriptural observations; Paul does not use the term. The chapter's substance is the explicit one-act / many-recipients structure and the `[I-high]` inference that the structure explains sufficiency.

## 15.8 Why "fairness" is not the right objection
This section is a boundary clarification, not a second foundation for the chapter. It tests how the one-for-many structure relates to individual reception; it does not decide the same-order correspondence established in Chapter 11 or add a second ground of sufficiency.

The most common objection to federal headship is fairness: "It's not fair that I'm held responsible for Adam's sin, or that I benefit from Christ's obedience without choosing it."

Paul's answer is structural, not volitional. The "in Adam" / "in Christ" relation is not based on individual choice but on representative constitution. The same structure that holds the many "in Adam" also holds them "in Christ" — both are constitutive relations, not chosen relations. The question "is it fair to be in Adam without choosing it?" has the same answer as "is it fair to be in Christ without choosing it?" — neither is fair in the individual-choice sense, but both are structurally necessary given that humanity exists as a represented humanity rather than as a collection of autonomous individuals. `[H-mid]` — this is the strongest framing from Scripture; it requires the further exegetical work in chapters on election and divine sovereignty.

**Evidence for** the structural-not-volitional reading:
(a) The parallel structure itself (Romans 5:12–21) — Paul's argument is built on the symmetry between the two "in" relations. If one is volitional and the other is not, the parallel breaks. The text doesn't signal such an asymmetry.
(b) 1 Corinthians 15:22 "in Adam all die" — uses present tense (*apothnēskousin*) of a state already in effect for all humanity, including those who have never made a volitional choice about Adam. The constitution is prior to the choice.
(c) The parallel in 1 Corinthians 15:45–49 — Paul names Adam "the first man" and Christ "the second man / last Adam." The paired terminology presupposes representative structure on both sides, not volitional selection.

**Evidence against** the structural-not-volitional reading:
(a) No text directly states that the "in Adam" relation is non-volitional. The text *describes* the relation; it does not argue for its non-volitional character in propositional form.
(b) The "many" in 1 Corinthians 15:22 is sometimes read as limited (e.g., "the many who are in Adam by nature" vs. "the many who are in Christ by faith"). A volitional reading of "in Christ" is plausible.
(c) The "federal headship" framework (a term coined in the systematic-theological tradition, not a phrase Paul uses) speaks of representative relations, but Paul does not provide a propositional argument that the "in Adam" relation is non-volitional. The strongest available reading from the immediate text is structural; the textual evidence does not foreclose readings in which the "in Christ" relation requires a receiving faith while the "in Adam" relation does not. The volitionalist reading has biblical material behind it: the conditionality language of many gospel texts (e.g., John 3:16, "whoever believes"); the "many" of 1 Corinthians 15:22 as potentially narrower than "all" (cf. 1 Corinthians 15:6, where "the many" refers to a defined group); and the way the New Testament elsewhere describes salvation as conditioned on faith (Mark 16:16; Acts 16:31; Romans 10:9).

**Band justification:** The parallel structure and the use of present-tense description both support the structural reading; the absence of explicit anti-volitional statement and the genuine textual weight of a volitional reading on the "in Christ" side pull against. The structural reading is the strongest exegesis of the immediate text but the volitional reading is not foreclosed. `[H-mid]`.

What Scripture explicitly rules out is the volitionalist counter: that the sufficiency of Christ's obedience depends on each individual's act of faith *adding to* it.

**Faith is the instrument by which one enters the "in Christ" relation, not the ground of the sufficiency itself.**

`[I-high]` (originally drafted as `[E]`; reclassified after review — the parallel structure with Adam's representative act and the Ephesians 2:8–9 gift-language make the instrument reading the strongest exegesis of the immediate context, but the absence of explicit propositional statement and the James 2:24 / Galatians 5:4 complications prevent `[E]`).

**Evidence for** the instrument-not-ground reading:
(a) Romans 5:1–2 — "Therefore, since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ. Through him we have also obtained access by faith into this grace in which we stand." Faith is the means of access (Greek *prosagōgēn*, "access"), not the ground of justification. The justification itself is grounded in the work of Christ (5:18–19); faith is how the many receive its effect.
(b) Ephesians 2:8–9 — "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast." Faith is the instrument through which the gift of grace is received. The gift (not the faith) is the ground.
(c) The parallel structure of Romans 5:12–21 — Adam's sin was not "grounded in" or "added to" by any subsequent volitional choice; it sufficed on its own to position the many. Christ's obedience operates the same way. Faith on the receiving end does not change the ground; it changes the application.

**Evidence against** the instrument-not-ground reading:
(a) No text directly states that faith is the instrument rather than a contributing ground. The text *describes* faith's role in justification; it does not argue for the instrument-vs-ground distinction in propositional form.
(b) James 2:24 — "You see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone." This is a textual complication; if James and Paul are read together, the relationship between faith, works, and justification is more complex than "faith is only the instrument."
(c) Galatians 5:4 — "You are severed from Christ, you who would be justified by the law; you have fallen away from grace." If faith can "fall away" and the relationship with Christ depends on continuing faith, faith is doing more work than "instrument."

**Band justification:** The parallel structure with Adam's representative act (which needed no faith to position the many) and the Ephesians 2:8–9 gift-language strongly support the instrument reading; the James and Galatians complications and the absence of explicit propositional statement pull against. The instrument reading is the strongest exegesis of the immediate Romans 5 + Ephesians 2 context. `[I-high]`.

The ground is the one man's obedience; faith is how the many receive its effect.

## 15.9 The "all" in Romans 5:18

A second objection concerns the universality of "all men" in Romans 5:18:

> "Therefore, as one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men."

**The parallel structure of "all" is `[E]`** — the second "all" matches the first "all." Whatever the first "all" denotes, the second "all" denotes the same scope. To deny the second universality while affirming the first is to break the parallel Paul has constructed. The exegetical question is then: what is the scope of "all" in both clauses?

The most natural reading, given the parallel structure, is that both "all"s are equally universal — the second "all" is no narrower than the first.

`[I-high]` (originally drafted as `[H]`; reclassified after review — the parallel structure (*hōs … houtōs*) is tight and the 1 Corinthians 15:22 reinforcement with *pantes* on both sides pulls strongly toward equal universality; the immediate addressee context and *polloi* / *pantes* distinction remain real ambiguities that prevent `[E]`).

**Evidence for** equal universality:
(a) The parallel construction is grammatically tight — *hōs* ("as") ... *houtōs* ("so also") sets up an exact parallel between the two clauses. Any narrowing in the second clause would need to be signaled by Greek syntax; it is not.
(b) The "all" in 1 Corinthians 15:22 — "For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive" — uses the same *hōs ... houtōs* parallel structure with *pantes* ("all") on both sides. The Corinthians parallel reinforces the universality reading.
(c) Romans 5:12 — "Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned" — uses *pantes* for "all" (the same word as in 5:18). If the universality of condemnation is real (and the broader text suggests it is), the universality of justification is structurally required by the parallel.

**Evidence against** equal universality:
(a) The immediate context — Romans 5 is addressed to believers and the "all" in the second clause might be read as "all believers" (those who are "in Christ") rather than "all humanity." This is a common pastoral reading.
(b) Romans 5:15–19 uses *polloi* ("many") for the recipients in the body of the passage, distinct from *pantes* ("all") in 5:12 and 5:18. *Polloi* can be functionally universal ("all without exception") or functionally limited ("many without distinction"), and its use in 5:15–19 alongside *pantes* in 5:12 and 5:18 is contested.
(c) No text directly states that the "all" of justification is coextensive with the "all" of condemnation. The parallel structure *implies* it; the text does not assert it propositionally.

**Band justification:** The parallel structure and the 1 Corinthians 15:22 reinforcement strongly support equal universality; the immediate addressee context and the *polloi* / *pantes* distinction introduce real ambiguity. The equal-universality reading is the strongest exegesis of the parallel structure but the limited-universality reading is not foreclosed by the immediate text. `[I-high]`.

What this means for the scope of redemption is `[H-mid]` — the equal-universality reading has implications for the scope of atonement, election, and evangelism that exceed what the Romans 5 text directly addresses; those questions are developed in later chapters.

## 15.10 The nature of the *en* relation

The Greek preposition *en* ("in") in *en Adam* / *en Christō* does a lot of work in the parallel structure. Possible readings:

- Sphere / domain: "in Adam" = "within the scope of Adam's representative act." The parallel is metaphorical-spacial.
- Organic union: "in Adam" = "ontologically united with Adam (as all humanity descended from him is)." The parallel is biological-descended.
- Legal inclusion: "in Adam" = "legally positioned as a member of Adam's humanity." The parallel is forensic.

`[H-mid]` — the *en* relation is too compressed to decisively point to any single reading.

**Evidence for** the sphere/dominional reading:
(a) The parallel with Adam — Adam is the first man, head of the human race by descent; the *en* relation to Adam is the relation of being in his sphere. Paul is not making a metaphysical claim about Adam being a "person" containing the race.
(b) Paul's use of *en Christō* elsewhere — Ephesians 1:3, 4, 6, 7, 10, 12, 13; 2:6, 10, 13; Galatians 2:4; Philippians 1:1; 2:1 — consistently uses *en Christō* as "in the sphere of Christ," meaning "among those who belong to Christ." The *en* is relational/spatial, not ontological.

**Evidence for** the organic-union reading:
(a) The descent argument — all humanity descended from Adam by biology. 1 Corinthians 15:22 "in Adam all die" is grounded in the descent (Genesis 5; Acts 17:26).
(b) The "first man ... from the earth, a man of dust; the second man is from heaven" parallel (1 Corinthians 15:47) — paired with biological/descent language.

**Evidence for** the legal-inclusion reading:
(a) The forensic context of Romans 5 — the whole passage is about justification, condemnation, and acquittal (legal/forensic categories).
(b) The *kathistēmi* lexical point (§15.4) — if *kathistēmi* carries a positional/legally-constitutive sense, the *en* relation that results from being "constituted" by Adam is itself legal/positional.

**Band justification:** All three readings have textual support in the Bible (sphere/relational in Ephesians; organic/descent in 1 Corinthians 15:47; legal/forensic in Romans 5's forensic categories). No single reading dominates. The chapter works with the sphere/relational reading as the most consistent with Paul's broader *en Christō* usage, but flags the others. `[H-mid]`.

## 15.11 The chapter's argument in summary

The argument of this chapter supports the reading that the "one man / many" structure of Romans 5:12–21 and 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49 is the scriptural mechanism by which Christ's obedience suffices for the many `[I-high]`.

**Structural claims (`[E]`):**
- The parallel between Adam and Christ (Romans 5:18–19; 1 Corinthians 15:20–22, 45–49)
- The one-act / many-recipients structure (Romans 5:18–19; 1 Corinthians 15:22)

**Lexical observations (`[I-mid]`):**
- *kathistēmi* in Romans 5:19 carries a positional/legally-constitutive sense (§15.4)
- *dikaiōma* in Romans 5:18 is a specific act/decree, not a state (§15.5)
- The pairing of Adam and Christ in 1 Corinthians 15:45–49 is representative, not merely biological or metaphorical (§15.6)

**Strong implications (`[I-high]`):**
- The one-for-many structure explains why one corresponding man's obedience suffices (§§15.1–15.2, 15.7)
- Faith is the instrument by which one enters the "in Christ" relation, not the ground of justification itself (§15.8)
- The "all" in Romans 5:18b is coextensive with the "all" in Romans 5:18a (§15.9)

**Hypotheses (`[H-mid]`):**
- The "in Adam" / "in Christ" relation is structural rather than volitional (§15.8)
- The *en* relation is sphere/relational rather than ontological or strictly legal (§15.10)
- The scope of redemption is universal in some sense coextensive with the universality of condemnation (§15.9)
