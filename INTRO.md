## I.1 Working thesis

This project argues that, according to Scripture, humanity's debt to God arose through the disobedience of one man, Adam, and that God Himself established death as the judicial consequence of sin. Because God's justice and word do not fail, redemption required a corresponding human representative who would render the faithful obedience Adam failed to render and willingly accept the death that divine justice required. Jesus Christ fulfills this role as the last Adam: the perfectly obedient man whose lifelong submission to the Father — climaxing in Gethsemane and culminating in the cross — constitutes the biblical pattern of redemption. The Old Testament prepares this reading by portraying Israel's repeated covenant disobedience and by teaching that sacrifice without repentance is unacceptable to God.

This thesis is drawn from the biblical text alone. The detailed argument is developed in chapters 1–16; the present Introduction states the project's commitments before the argument begins.

## I.2 Source constraint

This paper draws its arguments, evidence, and conclusions **only** from the text of the Holy Bible itself. No commentaries, no systematic theologies, no creeds, no church fathers, no modern biblical scholarship are cited as evidence for any theological claim. The sole authority and source material is Scripture.

Three corollaries follow from this constraint:

1. **Where this paper cites secondary literature**, it does so only to *document translation biases and limitations* (in `06_Translation_Bias_Documentation.md`), not to support theological claims. Theological claims are supported only by biblical texts.

2. **Where scholarly consensus differs from the plain reading of the text**, this paper follows the text. The text itself is the authority; consensus about what the text means is one piece of evidence among many, not an authority in its own right.

3. **Where translation choices are contested**, this paper cites the underlying Greek/Hebrew term in question and presents the range of readings across the translations in our set, leaving the reader to evaluate. We do not appeal to a "consensus reading" or to "what the church has always taught" to resolve contested points.

The constraint is methodologically strict but textually generous: every translation in our set has its own editorial frame, and the project's job is to read the text *through* those frames rather than *in deference to* them.

## I.3 What this paper is and is not trying to prove

**This paper argues:** the debt-debtor thesis of redemption — that Scripture presents redemption through a corresponding human representative whose willing obedience culminated in sacrificial death — is the *biblical* reading of the redemption narrative. The argument is *positive*: it shows what Scripture itself says, not what later systems added to what Scripture says.

**This paper is not trying to prove:** the doctrine of the Trinity (one God in three persons); the eternal generation of the Son; the deity of Christ in the Nicene metaphysical sense; the personality of the Holy Spirit; the immortality of the soul; or any other doctrinal commitment that requires systematic-theological synthesis to establish.

The negative claims matter because they bound the argument. Where Scripture *does* present material that the church has read Trinitarianly (e.g., John 1:1; Philippians 2:6; Colossians 1:15), this paper engages those texts exegetically but does not draw the Trinitarian conclusion. Where Scripture *does* present material that the church has read unitarianly (e.g., the Father as the one true God; Jesus as God's only-begotten human Son), this paper engages those texts exegetically and notes the reading without imposing it as the only possible reading.

The project is exegetical, not dogmatic. Its conclusions are *what the text says*, not *what must be true*.

## I.4 Translation set

The paper cites from two primary translations and consults five reference translations. Full per-translation bias and limitations analysis — with citations to publisher prefaces, scholarly reviews, and textual-criticism literature — is in `06_Translation_Bias_Documentation.md`.

**Primary citations** (default citation text):

| Translation | Type | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **ESV** (English Standard Version, 2016) | Essentially literal formal equivalence | Default citation text |
| **NASB** (New American Standard Bible, 1995) | Most literal English translation | Word-order / syntax arguments; cross-check of ESV |

The 1995 NASB is cited (not the 2020 update) because the 2020 update's "gender-accurate" language introduces interpretive choices (e.g., "brothers *and sisters*" for *adelphoi*) that the 1995 edition avoids. For this project's purposes — where the original-language masculine plural is itself theologically significant — the 1995 rendering is preferred.

**Reference translations** (consulted but not cited as primary):

| Translation | Textual basis | Why consulted |
|---|---|---|
| **YLT** (Young's Literal Translation, 1862) | Textus Receptus / Majority Text | Strictly literal word-for-word; surfaces Greek/Hebrew structure. Public domain. |
| **LSV** (Literal Standard Version, 2020) | Textus Receptus / Majority Text | Modern update of YLT in contemporary English; CC BY-SA licensed. |
| **Rotherham's Emphasized Bible** (1902) | Westcott-Hort critical text + Ginsburg OT | Maximal literalism with emphasis marks; anti-creedal translation philosophy. |
| **REV** (Revised English Version, revbible.com) | NA28 / BHS 4 | Modern unitarian-affiliated translation with extensive literal-Hebrew/Greek footnotes. |
| **CLV / CLNT** (Concordant Literal Version, A.E. Knoch) | Westcott-Hort derived; hyper-literal | Concordant vocabulary for Greek word-usage tracing. |

**Translations deliberately excluded:**

- **KJV / NKJV** — known to contain readings that smooth Trinitarian interpretation (e.g., the Comma Johanneum in 1 John 5:7; "only begotten" for *monogenēs* in passages where the Greek is ambiguous). The KJV family preserves 17th-century editorial decisions that the modern critical editions (NA28 / BHS) reject as not original. Excluded for source-material fidelity, not for theological opposition.

Where the two primary translations (ESV, NASB) diverge in a passage significant to the argument, both readings are presented. Where the reference translations add interpretive weight (e.g., on the *monogenēs* lexical choice; on *kathistēmi* as "constituted" vs. "made"), they are cited with the specific textual or structural argument they support. Translation differences are *evidence* about the underlying text, not a license to resolve ambiguity by appeal to outside material.

## I.5 The [E] / [I] / [H] tagging system

Every claim in this paper is tagged at draft time according to its epistemic status relative to the biblical text:

- **`[E]` — Explicit in the biblical text.** The claim is stated in a biblical passage and the statement is unambiguous in both primary translations. Example: "death was set as the consequence of disobedience in Eden" — `[E]` (Genesis 2:16–17).

- **`[I]` — Strong implication from context.** The claim is not stated directly but is the natural reading when the immediate passage is read in light of parallel passages and narrative context. Example: "Adam's sin *positioned* the many as sinners (legal/positional sense)" — `[I]` (Romans 5:19 *kathistēmi*).

- **`[H]` — Hypothesis / inference to be tested.** The claim goes beyond what the immediate passage asserts and depends on systematic synthesis of multiple passages. Example: "the *monogenēs* lexical choice bears on whether Christ is 'eternally generated' or 'uniquely one-of-a-kind'" — `[H]` (the lexical question is real; the doctrinal implication is one inference among several).

The system is intentionally coarse. Three tags suffice to keep honest the boundary between what Scripture *says* and what the argument *infers*. Where a claim could plausibly be tagged at more than one level, the more cautious tag is chosen — an `[E]` claim that could be `[I]` is downgraded to `[I]`, never upgraded.

The system also functions as a *review flag*: any `[H]` claim that survives through to a chapter's acceptance is one the project has affirmatively endorsed after further exegetical work. Any `[I]` claim that has not been *further* tested remains an implication, not an explicit statement.

The system is **not** a hedge or a way to avoid commitment. A `[H]` claim made in this paper is a claim the paper makes *as a hypothesis worth defending*, not a claim the paper declines to make. The tag tells the reader what kind of work has been done; the paper stands behind whatever tag it assigns.

## I.6 Roadmap of the manuscript

The paper has 17 numbered chapters (after this Introduction), plus 3 appendices:

**Part I — Foundations** (Ch 1–3): God's character; creation; the covenant in Eden. Establishes the *why* of the debt-debtor framework: divine justice that does not fail.

**Part II — The Fall** (Ch 4–6): Adam's sin; what was lost; what passed to humanity. Chapter 5 blocks Chapter 6 — do not start Ch 6 until Ch 5 has acceptance.

**Part III — Humanity Under the Debt** (Ch 7–10): innocence and accountability; the growth of disobedience; Israel as pattern of disobedience; obedience above sacrifice. Establishes the *human condition* the redeemer must address.

**Part IV — The Last Adam** (Ch 11–15): why a human redeemer; the perfect obedience of Christ; Gethsemane and the two wills; the cross as payment; why one man for many. **Chapter 15 is the recommended first chapter to draft** (per the prior review §9 Phase B) because it locks the central claim before the spine builds outward.

**Part V — Vindication and Conclusion** (Ch 16–17): the Father's vindication and the new humanity; conclusions mapping the 10 research questions to their chapter-of-answer.

**Appendices B–D** (deferred): alternative exegetical readings of difficult texts (Appendix B); a glossary of terms (Appendix C); the full biblical research map (Appendix D). Appendix A (historical dialogue with non-biblical sources) was present in v1.1 and is removed in v1.2 under the Bible-only constraint.

## I.7 Acknowledgments and limitations

This paper is a working draft. Its conclusions are not final. The text is being developed chapter by chapter from the outline in `01_Architecture_and_Outline.md`, with per-chapter acceptance criteria. A claim in this paper is "accepted" when its supporting chapter has been drafted, its `[E]/[I]/[H]` tags reviewed, and the chapter explicitly meets its acceptance criteria.

The Bible-only source constraint (I.2) limits the paper in two important ways:

1. **It cannot engage with the theological tradition directly.** Where the church has read a passage Trinitarianly for two millennia (e.g., John 1:1), this paper can note that fact but cannot cite Augustine, Aquinas, or the Reformed tradition as evidence. The argument has to work from the text alone.

2. **It cannot settle certain contested questions by appeal to authority.** Where the text itself is genuinely ambiguous (e.g., *monogenēs* lexical choice; *aionios* / *olam* range of meaning), this paper presents the range of readings rather than choosing one on external authority. The paper thus leaves some questions explicitly open where a dogmatic treatment would close them.

These limits are features of the method, not bugs. A paper that argues from Scripture alone is *constrained* in what it can prove but *unconstrained* in what it can show. The text itself is the authority; the paper's job is to read it carefully and let it speak.
