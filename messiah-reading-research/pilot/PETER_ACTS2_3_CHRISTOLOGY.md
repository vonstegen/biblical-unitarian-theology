## Initial evidence matrix

| Passage | [T] Observation from the inspected Greek | [L/I] Interpretive significance and limit |
|---|---|---|
| 2:22 | Jesus is called a man; God performs powerful works through him (`διʼ αὐτοῦ`). | Explicit humanity and divine agency. Calling him a man does not, by itself, exclude an additional nature. |
| 2:23–24,32 | Jesus is killed; God raises him. The speech also invokes God's purpose and foreknowledge. | Distinguishes the recipient of resurrection from God who acts. Foreknowledge alone does not establish personal pre-existence. |
| 2:33 | Exalted Jesus receives the promised Holy Spirit from the Father and pours out what the audience experiences. | Received authority and Jesus' active role both matter. The clause does not independently define the Spirit's ontology. |
| 2:34–35 | Peter quotes Psalm 110:1, distinguishing the speaker, “Lord,” from “my lord,” addressed at his right hand. | Royal enthronement is central. Greek `κύριος` appears for both referents; this alone does not demonstrate identical status or nature. Hebrew Psalm analysis remains pending. |
| 2:36 | God makes the crucified Jesus both Lord and Messiah. | The scope of “made” must be tested as appointment/exaltation language; it does not automatically mean creation or first existence. |
| 3:13 | The God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob glorifies his `παῖς`, Jesus. | “Servant” is the working contextual rendering; the noun can also mean child. An Isaiah-servant connection requires separate testing. |
| 3:14–15 | Jesus is called holy and righteous and `ἀρχηγὸς τῆς ζωῆς`; God raises him from the dead. | “Author,” “originator,” “leader,” and “pioneer” need lexical/contextual assessment. Neither “Creator of all life” nor a minimal title is assumed. |
| 3:20–23 | God sends the appointed Messiah; Moses' prophet promise is applied within this argument. | Divine commission and prophetic authority; the future sending and present heavenly reception require contextual attention. |
| 3:25–26 | Abrahamic blessing frames God's sending of his servant to turn people from wickedness. | Covenant and restoration provide the narrative setting. The meaning of “raising up” in 3:26 requires study rather than automatic identification with either birth or resurrection. |

## Competing explanations [I]

**Biblical Unitarian working reading:** The speeches present the human Messiah whom the God of Israel empowers, raises, glorifies, and appoints to rule. Received authority and obedience to God's commissioned agent provide a coherent account of the explicit relations.

**Classical Trinitarian working reading:** The speeches describe the incarnate Son's human ministry and messianic exaltation. Personal distinction from the Father and received messianic authority are compatible with shared divine nature. That nature is not explicitly formulated in these units; the proposal depends on a wider canonical argument.

**Questions for both:** The Unitarian reading must explain the breadth of Jesus' lordship, Spirit outpouring, and life-related title without minimizing them. The Trinitarian reading must show why an incarnational/divine-nature explanation is warranted here, rather than merely possible, while retaining the explicit humanity and dependence. Neither side may treat its wider premises as statements these speeches explicitly make.

## Provisional assessment

The clearest local pattern is God's action toward and through Jesus, followed by Jesus' exalted authority. The units explicitly establish humanity, messiahship, resurrection, lordship, and divine commission. **They do not explicitly formulate either later two-nature doctrine or an exclusion of any divine nature.**

A Biblical Unitarian account closely follows the local agency pattern; whether it explains the full evidence better requires examining the titles and scriptural quotations. Compatibility with a system is not yet proof of that system.
